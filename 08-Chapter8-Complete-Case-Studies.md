# Chapter 8 The Complete Case Studies

The methodology of the previous seven chapters is entirely distilled from real practice on the ground. This chapter restores those scenes in full: five sets of cases, from the inventor of the pattern to its most recent heirs, from Silicon Valley to China. As you read these cases, it's worth holding one question in mind and looking back at the previous seven chapters: before these methods had names, how were they arrived at, almost by instinct?

## 8.1 Palantir: Twenty Years Turning a "Dumb Method" into a Moat

### Founding: Building Software for "Users You're Not Allowed to Ask"

In 2003, Silicon Valley was still lying in the rubble of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel pulled together a group of young Stanford alumni to do something that sounded crazy: build data analysis software for the U.S. intelligence community. The company took its name from the palantíri in *The Lord of the Rings*—the seeing-stones that let you look far away.

The craziness wasn't in the technology, it was in the users. Bob McGrew's widely circulated recollection, years later, captured the predicament best: "One of the challenges of building software for spies is that I don't know any spies. You probably don't either. And even if you happen to find a spy, if you ask him 'how do you actually work day to day,' he usually won't tell you."

The entire methodology of the software industry—user interviews, questionnaires, usability testing—collectively failed in front of this user base. Co-founder Stephen Cohen's response was endearingly dumb: build a demo, show it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. The answer was merciless: "This thing is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat—he pressed further: "So what would you want it to be like instead?" Then he wrote down every piece of feedback, went back, made changes, and brought it back again. Over and over.

This loop is the genetic fragment of FDE, and it contains two intuitions that would later prove worth their weight in gold: customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something that works; and the fastest way to find out what they want is to put the people who build the thing right next to the people who use it.

### Taking Shape: From an Emergency Measure to Organizational Strategy

Early Palantir moved forward along a path of "one customer, one custom build," and quickly hit the problem every enterprise software company eventually hits: what the second customer wants turns out to be "subtly but critically different" from what the first customer wanted.

The standard solution is to distill the commonalities, build a generalized product, and say no to the differences. But Palantir's customers didn't accept "no"—they were the CIA, they were the U.S. military on the battlefield. The person who truly pulled the company out of this deadlock was early employee Shyam Sankar (later the company's president and CTO). His solution was viewed as heresy at the time: don't build two products, and don't build one rigid product—instead, build a highly customizable platform, and station engineers on-site with customers to complete the last mile.

Sankar's most crucial move was a rebellion in the ledger. In the financial language of the software industry, "customizing for a single customer" is called cost of service, an enemy of margins to be minimized. Sankar redefined it as "product discovery"—on-site customization wasn't spending money, it was gathering intelligence for the product's next evolution. Swapping out one term changed a company's entire capital allocation logic: what looked like a cost center to everyone else became Palantir's R&D center.

The organizational form followed. Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") embedded with customers, "one customer, mobilizing multiple capabilities"; deployment strategists (codename "Echo") were responsible for understanding the customer's mission and organization; platform engineers stayed in the rear, "one capability, serving multiple customers." By 2016, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineer headcount had surpassed its platform engineer headcount—a software company with the bulk of its troops "on the front line."

### Trial by Fire: Battlefields, Hurricanes, and Oil Fields

The value of the FDE pattern was proven again and again in extreme, real-world settings.

On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, roadside bombs were the number one killer of patrols. Palantir engineers stationed in the field discovered that soldiers had zero interest in beautifully designed intelligence charts—all they wanted was to "mark this road as suspicious on the map." The engineers threw together a crude mapping tool on the spot: soldiers clicked once, and the whole team could see the risky stretches of road. This tool directly reduced casualties, and later became a standard feature of the platform. Note: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room—only in the moment an engineer and a soldier looked at the same stretch of road together.

The prototype scene for this playbook predates even the mapping tool. Around 2007, Sankar took a small team into the classified information room of the U.S. military's counter-IED task force and worked jointly there for two weeks. Even speakerphones were banned in the secure room, so he strapped a phone to his head with a rubber band to free up both hands for typing code—one ear listening to analysts give feedback, the other listening to colleagues back at Silicon Valley headquarters. Nineteen hours a day: demo, take in data, collect feedback, make changes on the spot.

At the end of two weeks, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself was completely wrecked, and called Karp: "This isn't sustainable, we're finished." Karp's answer later became company culture: turn this "unsustainable" thing into an institution. (see Appendix C for sources)

At the scene of Hurricane Sandy relief efforts in 2012, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers stitched together scattered relief data into real-time maps to coordinate the deployment of rescue resources. Oil drilling platforms, aircraft final assembly lines, bank trading floors—the engineers pushed "being on-site" to a depth the software industry had never seen before. Former Palantir engineer Barry recalls that the core components of the Foundry platform were born in Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku—a map of the world, with every point representing an act of on-site creation.

The cost was equally real. Barry's account is required reading as a cautionary counter-example for understanding this pattern: top engineers, massive amounts of global travel, a great deal of duplicated wheel-reinventing, free pilots—"a lot of projects had margins that were literally negative infinity"; in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held," chaotic enough to keep equity-holding employees up at night. What allowed Palantir to absorb these costs was two things: the capital thickness underwritten by astronomically priced contracts, and a mindset of "treating pilots like venture capital bets"—it's fine if most bets go to zero, as long as the winning ones pay for everything.

Commercialization also died once already. The first enterprise-facing product, Metropolis, landed with a dismal market response; only the second attempt, Foundry, finally caught on. The turning point was Airbus: at the Toulouse plant, a fuel pump on the A380 kept failing repeatedly, and Airbus's own engineers had spent a full two years chasing it with no leads. Palantir's people came in, fed the sensor data into the platform, and cracked the case in two weeks—fuel was sloshing away from the pump during climb. A trivial fix saved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars.

Marc Fontaine, Airbus's head of digital transformation, later remarked publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months." (see Appendix C for sources) From then on, Airbus became Palantir's most enthusiastic evangelist in Europe, building its own data platform, Skywise, entirely on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and over 50,000 users.

### Explosion: A Bootcamp Ignites a Commercial Empire

In 2023, generative AI exploded. Every enterprise wanted to "use AI," and every enterprise got stuck at the same place: not knowing how. Palantir's answer was to compress twenty years of on-site methodology into an industrialized machine—the AIP Bootcamp.

The rules were minimal: the customer brings real data and a real problem, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers work alongside them, and in one to five days they build a deployable AI application prototype, for free or for a nominal fee. Day 0 locks in an extremely focused core battlefield; Day 1 connects the data and builds the ontology model; Days 2 to 3, engineers and the customer's technical staff write code back-to-back, wiring the large language model into the business process; Days 4 to 5, executives operate the system with their own hands and make the call on the spot.

The throughput and conversion rate of this machine left the entire industry stunned: starting from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, doubling year over year, and reaching nearly 6 sessions per day at peak in 2025; the nine-to-twelve-month sales cycle of enterprise software was compressed to a matter of weeks; pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to 4,000 stores in eight months through the bootcamp; market research company J.D. Power, once it learned the model, started running bootcamps for its own customers—the pattern began replicating itself. The pace of contracts after the bootcamps moved fast enough that peers could barely believe it: a large healthcare company signed a five-year deal worth $26 million a year in annual contract value, five weeks after attending a bootcamp; a global bank signed $2 million after a one-month pilot, then expanded four months later into a three-year deal worth $19 million a year in annual contract value. (see Appendix C for sources)

The financial results exploded accordingly: U.S. commercial revenue grew 137% year over year in Q4 2025; the "Rule of 40" hit 127%, and climbed to 145% in Q1 2026; a single quarter of bookings reached $4.26 billion, net revenue retention hit 139%; market capitalization briefly surpassed $400 billion. Wall Street analysts who once mocked it for "human wave tactics" started rewriting their research reports overnight.

The biggest vote of trust came from the U.S. Navy. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Karp jointly announced a "shipbuilding operating system" contract: $448 million, first using Foundry and AIP to transform the submarine industrial base—two major shipyards, three naval shipyards, a hundred suppliers. The pilot data was discussed earlier: scheduling went from 160 hours down to 10 minutes, material review went from weeks down to an hour. The Secretary of the Navy specifically stated "this is not a concept, not a pilot, not research"—it skipped proof of concept and went straight into production, because the earlier embedded pilot had already proven its value. That same year, the U.S. Army also bundled 75 scattered service contracts into a single ten-year, $10 billion framework agreement with Palantir. The government's trust is compound interest accumulated over twenty years of being on the ground. (see Appendix C for sources)

An engineer named Nabeel Qureshi, who was stationed in Toulouse for a full year, left behind a more vivid footnote: he spent four days a week in the final assembly hall, writing software for the A350's production line alongside factory workers—he called it "building Asana for building airplanes." A Silicon Valley engineer living for a full year in a factory in a foreign country—this is the plainest, most literal meaning of the phrase "forward deployed." (see Appendix C for sources)

### Methodology Restored

Looking back at the previous seven chapters, the Palantir case is almost an index for the entire book: the demo loop and "customers don't know what they want" (Chapter 2); customization as product discovery, the gravel road and the highway (Chapters 1 and 7); the strategic value of lighthouse customers (Chapter 3); the portfolio investment logic of free pilots (Chapter 6); on-site tools growing bottom-up into a platform (Chapter 7); and the most fundamental creed of all—complexity cannot be eliminated remotely, someone must be on the ground (the whole book).

## 8.2 OpenAI: When the People Who Built ChatGPT Started Doing the Grunt Work

### The Turn: From "The Model Is the Product" to "Deployment Is Strategy"

OpenAI's first seven years were a pure technology epic: the GPT series raced ahead nonstop, and ChatGPT set the fastest user growth record in human history. In this kind of company narrative, "enterprise delivery" sounds like something that belongs to a different industry altogether.

The turning point came in 2024. Data from the enterprise market pointed to something impossible to ignore: the now-widely-known "95% failure rate" was playing out in real time; meanwhile, the market share map of enterprise large-model deployments was shifting dramatically—industry tracking showed Anthropic climbing to about 32% of the enterprise market, while OpenAI's once-dominant roughly 50% share in the early years fell back to around 25%. The gap in model capability was narrowing, and the standard by which enterprise customers voted with real money quietly became a different question: **"Who can actually help me put this to use?"**

OpenAI's response came in two steps. Step one: in 2024, it formed a Forward Deployed Engineering team—starting from 2 engineers, led by Colin Jarvis, rapidly expanding into a global network spanning San Francisco, New York, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, and Sydney, growing from 2 people to 52 within a year. The job posting was unabashedly direct: "embed with customers where model performance is critical, delivery is urgent, and ambiguity is the default state," with travel up to 50%. The head of the Europe region, Fournier, put it even more bluntly: "Demand from the market has exceeded our expectations." (see Appendix C for sources)

Step two: on May 11, 2026, OpenAI established "OpenAI Deployments"—a joint venture controlled by OpenAI, together with 19 top-tier capital firms (TPG leading the round, with Advent, Brookfield co-leading, and Goldman Sachs, SoftBank, Warburg Pincus, and others participating; Bain & Company, Capgemini, and McKinsey also serving as founding partners), with initial investment exceeding $4 billion, and a pre-money valuation reported by media at roughly $10 billion; simultaneously, it acquired applied AI consultancy Tomoro, folding in about 150 deployment engineers as an intact unit. COO Brad Lightcap personally leads the effort.

The structural design of this new company is worth writing into business school textbooks, because it welds together "where the customers come from" and "how the capital comes back." The investors are private equity giants, and these giants hold, among their portfolios, hundreds of companies spanning healthcare, manufacturing, finance, retail, and logistics—an existing customer pool ready-made for OpenAI Deployments; in exchange, OpenAI reportedly promised these private equity investors a five-year, 17.5% annualized guaranteed return (as reported by media, not confirmed by OpenAI officially), while retaining super-voting rights and control over strategic direction for itself.

The acquired Tomoro is no unknown either: this UK applied AI consultancy's engineers had previously done exactly this kind of enterprise deployment work at Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and gaming company Supercell. In a single day, OpenAI assembled all three cards: "engineers, channel, capital." (see Appendix C for sources)

A company valued at hundreds of billions of dollars, holding humanity's most powerful models, setting up a separate billion-dollar-scale company just to "send people to work on-site with customers"—this is the heaviest weight ever placed on the scale in the history of the FDE pattern.

### On-Site: John Deere's Farmland

The best showcase of OpenAI's FDE methodology is its collaboration with John Deere. This nearly two-hundred-year-old agricultural giant needed to solve a problem that grows straight out of the ground: weed control. The traditional approach is blanket-spraying herbicide across the entire field—high cost, heavy chemical damage, and a large environmental toll. The ideal of precision agriculture is "only spray when you see weeds," but the difficulty of recognition lies in the fact that every field has different weed patterns, different crop growth stages, and different seasonal timing.

OpenAI's Forward Deployed team's approach was a complete demonstration of the methodology from Chapters 2 and 4 of this book: fly to Iowa, follow agronomists into the fields, understand the workflow and constraints of precision agriculture (including that non-negotiable deadline—the growing season); review hundreds of real field operation cases together with experts, and encode "what counts as a good spraying recommendation" into a custom evaluation system; rapidly iterate on the model and system under the watch of that evaluation system. The final result: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmer engagement frequency increased sixfold.

Three details in this case are worth underlining. First, the deadline was the growing season, not a project schedule—the FDE's rhythm must submit to the customer's business rhythm. Second, the evaluation system came before model optimization—define "good" first, then pursue "good." Third, the figure of 70% wasn't after-the-fact marketing packaging—it was a target set jointly with experts before the work even began.

### On-Site: BBVA's 120,000 People

Another benchmark case is Spain's BBVA. The starting point of the collaboration wasn't especially dazzling—deploying enterprise ChatGPT. But the compound interest of the FDE pattern lies here: the starting point was a tool, and the path was organizational. Starting from 3,300 accounts, employees spontaneously created more than 20,000 custom mini-assistants, 83% of people using them actively every week, saving an average of about 3 hours per week; a year and a half later, the deployment expanded into a system covering 25 countries and 120,000 employees, and the bank's own goal upgraded accordingly to "building an AI-native global bank." Going from "giving someone a tool" to "co-building an organizational form" is the ultimate expression of Chapter 6's "deepening into the installed base." (see Appendix C for sources)

### Methodology Restored

The lesson of the OpenAI case lies in "a model company's self-awareness": as model capability converges, deployment capability becomes the main battleground for differentiation; as the product form remains undecided (nobody knows what an agent is supposed to look like), the field becomes the only place where product discovery can happen. Its three-stage working method (co-creation, validation, delivery), its engineering obsession with evaluation systems, and its capital-structure innovation for OpenAI Deployments (using private equity's industrial network as a customer channel) correspond respectively to the core propositions of Chapters 4, 6, and 3 of this book.

## 8.3 Anthropic and the Vertical Trio: Three Variations on FDE

If Palantir invented the pattern and OpenAI put it on the front page, then Anthropic and a group of vertical AI companies demonstrate three variations of FDE growing in different soil.

### Variation One: Anthropic — Safety Genes and Differentiation Through "Auditability"

Anthropic's FDE function sits under its Applied AI team, and the job posting contains two qualifiers worth dwelling on: one is "safety and reliability"—every deliverable must meet Anthropic's safety standards; the other is "defending the company's mission on the front lines"—the FDE is simultaneously an ambassador for the company's values. In 2025, this team expanded fivefold in a single year, and its head, Kate DeJong, explained: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are simply two different species." (see Appendix C for sources)

The direct product of this differentiation is that "auditability" has become a product feature. The collaboration with fintech giant FIS is the benchmark case: Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineers embedded with FIS to co-design financial crime detection agents, compressing anti-money-laundering investigations from hours down to minutes, with every decision fully replayable and traceable, first landing at Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank. In heavily regulated industries, "every AI decision can be clearly explained to a regulator" isn't a bonus feature—it's the price of admission, and Anthropic turned it into a selling point.

The evidence that "safety paranoia" becomes a killer enterprise-market weapon is written in customer testimonials. Cybersecurity company Palo Alto Networks deployed Claude to 2,500 developers, and its engineering director's assessment was blunt: "They care more about security than anyone else—every meeting is about security implications. For the largest cybersecurity company, that matters to us." The result: feature development speed increased 20-30%, and junior developers completed integration tasks 70% faster. Pharmaceutical giant Novo Nordisk uses it to write clinical research documentation: reports that used to take over ten weeks now come out in ten minutes. At the same time, it partnered with consulting giant Accenture to train 30,000 Claude-certified consultants, and the number of enterprise customers grew from fewer than a thousand to over 300,000 in two years. (see Appendix C for sources)

An even deeper layer of differentiation lies in the official promise of "knowledge transfer": the explicit goal of the embedding is for FIS to be able "to independently build and extend its own agents in the future." Writing "teaching the customer" into the contract looks, in the short term, like weakening one's own irreplaceability; in the long term, it's the most powerful response to concerns about "vendor lock-in"—Gartner predicts that 70% of enterprises will abandon such solutions before 2028 due to vendor cost and skills hollowing-out, and Anthropic's approach is to defuse that time bomb in advance.

In May 2026, Anthropic was reported to be forming an enterprise AI services joint venture with Blackstone and others, reportedly worth about $1.5 billion, focused on embedding Claude into the operations of mid-sized enterprises—facing off against OpenAI Deployments on the very same day. The fact that two model giants completed the organizational move of "deployment as strategy" on the same day says more about FDE's position in the landscape than any analysis could.

### Variation Two: Sierra and Decagon — Startups' "Delivery Is the Product"

Sierra (founded in 2023 by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor and former Google VP Clay Bavor, valued at $4.5 billion at its October 2024 funding round, and reportedly continuing to climb since) turned the FDE pattern into the business model itself: customers don't pay for software, they pay for "resolved conversations"; implementation is managed by Sierra's Forward Deployed team, and customers only need to contribute brand voice and operating rules. Results: brand-name customers including Sonos, Casper, WeightWatchers, and ADT have flocked in; the fastest publicly reported go-live case took four weeks, and the typical annual contract floor starts at hundreds of thousands of dollars. Outcome-based pricing naturally anchors Sierra's pricing to customer value—the most radical real-world example of Chapter 6's "outcome-based pricing."

Sierra's internal naming for this role is also worth noting: not FDE, but "Agent Engineer." Its lead, Natalie Moller (five years at Palantir, running law enforcement, defense, and infrastructure customers), explains that the name should describe "the shape of the technical work," not just "the obsession with the customer"—building voice agents requires a special kind of "taste" (what sounds human, what sounds right), a skill narrower and deeper than the general FDE role. The collaboration with ticketing platform Vivid Seats is her team's brightest track record: go-live in four weeks, self-service resolution rate up 40%, customer satisfaction up 35%—and the mechanism by which conversation data feeds back into the product means that "ten thousand people asking about the same feature in a month" can immediately be bumped up in priority. (see Appendix C for sources)

Decagon (co-founder Ashwin Sreenivas came from Palantir, total funding of $231 million) took a different path: productizing delivery experience directly into an agent operating workflow—letting the customer's operations staff define multi-step workflows in natural language, which the AI executes deterministically. Customer list: Notion, Duolingo, Eventbrite, Rippling. Reportedly, Chime's self-service resolution rate reached 70%, Duolingo's ticket deflection rate reached 80%, and ClassPass's customer service costs fell 95%. Decagon's FDEs play more the role of "the person who teaches the customer's team to use this system"—the goal of delivery is to give the customer's non-technical staff self-service capability, the best practice for the question "can it be used by people who can't write code?" from Chapter 7's "four questions for productization." (see Appendix C for sources)

### Variation Three: Harvey — Breaking Into Places Ordinary Software Can't Reach

The legal industry is enterprise software's "Bermuda Triangle": top law firms have strict confidentiality discipline, data that never leaves their domain, and partners who each run their own fiefdom—self-service online software has a hard time winning even a single victory here. Harvey (founded in 2022, valued at $11 billion after a new funding round completed in March 2026) broke into this market using the FDE pattern by sheer force.

First, remember where this company came from: two founders, one a litigator from a law firm, the other a scientist from DeepMind (Google's AI lab); a $5 million seed round from the OpenAI Startup Fund. (see Appendix C for sources)

Reviewing its playbook is almost a perfect medley of this book's methodology. Lighthouse strategy—the first major customer locked in was top global law firm Allen & Overy (February 2023, 3,500 lawyers, 43 offices), followed in succession by PwC (over 4,000 legal professionals), Paul, Weiss, and Macfarlanes; champion cultivation—Allen & Overy's internal point of contact, David Wakeling (head of market innovation), became the in-house hub for the deployment; depth-first pace—entering through a single practice group, with a partner champion pushing it forward, expanding horizontally after six months of real-world use; customer research loop—partners pay but don't do the work, associates do the work but don't pay, knowledge management lawyers see the blind spots on both sides, so the FDE's interview framework has to penetrate all three layers of people simultaneously, "the hardest part of the deployment isn't the model, it's this customer research loop"; extra-long cycle—a single firm's deployment took six to nine months, with the FDE responsible for everything from the document management system's data pipeline all the way through partner-by-partner adoption demos.

The results show a rare surge of momentum, uncommon even among vertical AI companies: over 100,000 lawyer users, 1,300 organizations, covering the majority of America's top hundred law firms; annual recurring revenue went from about $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026; valuation quadrupled in a single year—$3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, $11 billion. (see Appendix C for sources)

Harvey proves where the boundary of the FDE pattern lies: **wherever the three mountains of "confidentiality discipline, data residency, and key-user autonomy" exist simultaneously, productized delivery has no answer—only by sending people in can you bring the system in with them**. And such markets—law, healthcare, finance, government—happen to be the highest-margin enterprise service markets on Earth.

## 8.4 China Cases: Growing FDE on the Soil of "Project-Based Delivery"

China is the world's most peculiar enterprise software market: it has the most hardworking delivery engineers, the harshest customization demands, and the deepest "curse of project-based delivery"—large enterprises demand customization, vendors lose money on every deal, costs can't be amortized, and vendors end up as outsourced labor for the client, with professional expertise hollowed out. The fate of the FDE pattern on this soil is a question that must be answered separately: is it the cure for the curse, or a new disguise for the same curse?

First, let's see just how tangled this situation is, through the sighs of three practitioners. The first comes from database company DolphinDB, whose long industry essay asks "Who Is Destroying China's Enterprise Software Industry," and answers with six mountains: free-riding, open source, outsourcing, bidding processes, "digital science" (数科, in-house digitization units), and a deformed market structure—China's top engineering talent is chronically consumed by highly customized, relationship-heavy, price-war-driven non-standard delivery work. The second comes from a veteran of the enterprise software industry on the podcast *Hardcore Hacker*: "Customization is SaaS's natural enemy, and this curse can only be dissolved naturally on the day the market matures." The third comes from a former president of DingTalk: many companies get stuck once they reach one or two hundred million RMB in revenue—customer acquisition is hard, delivery is hard, operations and maintenance are hard, customization costs are high, and economies of scale simply never materialize. 36Kr summed up these three sighs: "Software productization amortizes R&D costs that would otherwise be spent on projects; SaaS amortizes sales costs—it's only the project-based companies that suffer the most, because the cost of a project is written off the moment the project ends, with no way to amortize it." (see Appendix C for sources) These three sighs are exactly the Chinese question that the FDE pattern has to answer.

### The Big-Tech Path: Volcano Engine's Doubao FDE

Volcano Engine, under ByteDance, is the clearest player among China's internet giants to fly the FDE flag: in 2026 it formed a dedicated FDE team to co-create deeply with industries and flagship customers. In a public interview, Volcano Engine president Tan Dai's phrasing echoes this book's own definition almost word for word: "FDE is not sales, and it's not pre-sales either—they must have strong technical implementation ability, especially the ability to actually implement AI code." He also revealed that the team deliberately mixes in diverse industry backgrounds (for example, assigning someone with a biological engineering background to work with biopharmaceutical clients), and it currently covers key industries including automotive, healthcare, education, finance, and semiconductors. In July 2026, Volcano Engine also signed a strategic partnership with consulting giant EY, with both sides planning to build a thousand-person-scale FDE team to "explore a new model for full-stack AI delivery." (see Appendix C for sources)

The inherent advantage of big tech companies doing FDE is a thick platform foundation (Volcano Ark provides an integrated base of models, tool chains, and cloud infrastructure); the inherent challenge is the inertia of big tech's sales system and the financial inertia of treating "delivery as a cost center"—whether the FDE team can gain the organizational status of "on-site work equals R&D," Palantir-style, determines whether it becomes a genuine FDE function or just a pre-sales support department with a fashionable new name.

### The Pioneer Path: Writing "FDE Is Not the Same as On-Site Outsourcing" on the Company Website

Even more worth watching is a group of homegrown Chinese startups. They lack big tech's platform foundation, but they use the clearest possible language to distinguish FDE from China's old species of vendor. One service provider focused on the real estate and facilities management industry wrote what is currently the most refined definitional distinction of FDE found domestically, right on its website:

> First, FDE delivers in stages and is accepted based on results; on-site outsourcing is settled by hours worked. Second, FDE brings a product foundation to do engineering work; on-site outsourcing writes and rewrites everything from scratch on-site. Third, when FDE finishes, it leaves—capability is embedded in the system and in your team; on-site outsourcing sticks around longer and longer, and when the people leave, the system stops working.

These three sentences correspond respectively to three of this book's core propositions: outcome-based pricing (Chapter 6), platform foundation (Chapter 7), and knowledge transfer (Chapter 5). It has also built a "dissuasion mechanism": it won't take on unvalidated scenarios, it won't take on cases where the data can be exported to a single spreadsheet, and it won't take on customers who just want to "learn about AI"—"we'd rather you start a bit later than start at the wrong time." This is the Chinese market's own conscious practice of Chapter 2's "reject the expensive proof-of-concept graveyard."

Another service provider moved the FDE pattern into the finance industry, which is the most sensitive to compliance, and distilled a clear three-stage methodology: scenario diagnosis, one to two weeks (identifying the three to five highest-value core scenarios, quantifying ROI) → embedded delivery, eight to sixteen weeks (full-stack delivery from model selection to production integration) → production deployment and continuous evolution (used daily by business units, iterating alongside the business), while also emphasizing "data never leaves the domain"—a local adaptation to the regulatory environment of Chinese financial institutions. (see Appendix C for sources)

In June 2026, a practitioner at a domestic investment institution posted a long essay online with an eye-catching title: "FDE, Silicon Valley's Hottest Job This Year—We've Been Grinding at It Quietly for Three Years." The essay recorded their real experience of applying this playbook in Chinese enterprise services: after signing, a Forward Deployed Engineer "dives into a single customer, with no limit on capability, and actually installs AI into their business"; the standard for measuring success is "not how much code was written or how many documents were delivered, but whether anyone is actually using this system, and whether the customer's business has actually gotten better"; and a conclusion entirely consistent with this book's own—"going live isn't the finish line; you have to stay until the system becomes part of the customer's daily routine, and until the customer's own people can take over." This article was widely shared among domestic practitioners because, for the first time, it laid out a clear comparison table in Chinese: the product engineer is responsible for the roadmap, pre-sales is responsible for closing the deal, implementation is responsible for the acceptance sign-off, customer success is responsible for renewals—and the FDE is responsible for whether the customer's conversion rate and productivity have actually gone up. (see Appendix C for sources)

### A Control Group: Set-Top Boxes and Paper Contracts

Which customers deserve FDE investment and which don't—Wu Guangyu, CEO of Baido Data, offered two starkly contrasting customers at an entrepreneurs' roundtable.

One was a set-top box customer with an extremely clear need—"how do we use AI in the set-top box." The FDE soaked in with them, and ultimately built an agent that watches shows with you and chats about gossip—a very high return on investment. The other was a company expanding overseas, whose boss wanted to spend $1 million on AI, but every time they signed a contract, they'd print it out and mark it up with a pen—not even using Word's track changes feature. Wu Guangyu's judgment was direct: "A customer with this little digital foundation—investing FDE into them will produce zero results. We told them straight up: first buy 100 Gemini accounts and get everyone using it; once you have concrete ideas, then we'll talk."

His conclusion is nearly identical to the rejection mechanism in Chapter 2: **the precondition for FDE is that the customer has a clear, specific need**. Don't easily invest in vague, boundless needs; needs can be broken into phase one, phase two, phase three—do the clearest part first, deliver the result, and the follow-on need will naturally convert into a long-term service contract. (see Appendix C for sources)

### The Other Side: The Vacuum Layer and the Dirty Work

However, if you think China's FDE practice always looks as clean as the above, front-line practitioners will hand you a cold cup of water.

*Growth Hacker AI Weekly* recorded the observations of front-line practitioner Shen Yue: in many domestic projects, FDE ends up looking more like outsourced labor for the vendor, doing the dirty, thankless work of plugging holes and cleaning up messes. In one ten-million-yuan state-owned enterprise project she personally experienced, the top boss signed the contract and then disappeared—there was no one in the project genuinely responsible, and the FDE was stranded in a vacuum layer, with no decision-maker above and no executor below, ultimately having to rely on studying "reporting logic" just to get sign-off. In another case, a private home-renovation company in Guangdong, the boss (a second-generation founder's heir) came straight to the point: help her cut her workforce from 6,000 down to 3,000. But what AI can replace is tasks, not positions—and the project limped forward amid repeated scolding.

Shen Yue's summary is worth copying down here: the difficulty of FDE in China lies in the layer of separation on the client side, in the weak digital foundations of enterprises, and in bosses lacking patience—and the over-promises made by front-end sales in order to close the deal ultimately all have to be paid back by the FDE. She has one more line, which I've used as the epigraph for Chapter 4: "No matter how technology evolves, the human nature and power struggles inside an organization will always be a harder problem than the technology itself." (see Appendix C for sources)

### Another Voice: Is FDE Even a Good Business?

The doubts don't only come from the front line. At the same roundtable, Kuse.ai founder Wu Xiankun raised a question about the FDE pattern itself, and didn't mince words: "FDE is very hard to turn into a good business model." His reasoning was direct: do it shallow, and the model vendors will do it themselves; do it deep, and it's essentially still outsourcing—high delivery costs that are hard to scale. "When you're serving a state-owned or central enterprise, you can't refuse requests outside the project scope—where's your leverage? Do you find yourself wondering, late at night, whether you've just become cheap outsourcing—that's a question everyone doing FDE has to face."

His solution is quite radical: AI roll-ups (consolidation through M&A). Since customers worry about data leaks, and teams feel that outsourcing goes against their original intent—then just buy the company outright, make everyone a shareholder with aligned interests, and turn the entire company upside down to change it; once that's done, use the same methodology to acquire the next one. His logic: if AI transformation really carries that much value, you should own the business, not sell hours. (see Appendix C for sources)

This doubt may not have a standard answer, but it forms a curious echo with the Gartner prediction cited back in Chapter 5—that 70% of enterprises may abandon FDE-led solutions before 2028: **perhaps the biggest enemy of the FDE pattern isn't technology, but its own cost structure**. This book doesn't intend to offer a standard answer, but it's a question everyone considering entering this field should think through first.

### Three Special Propositions for Chinese FDE

Synthesizing these practices and industry observations, for FDE to work in China, it must answer three questions its American counterparts don't have to answer.

First, the pricing dilemma. The American market can accept subscriptions, usage-based billing, and even outcome-based revenue sharing; Chinese customers' muscle memory is "lump-sum purchase plus project-based acceptance." A viable transitional form is a "hybrid model": payment upon phase-based acceptance (in keeping with habit) + value metrics written into acceptance clauses (injecting outcome DNA) + annual maintenance and evolution contracts (cultivating a habit of renewal). Copying usage-based billing wholesale, in one leap, will die in the procurement office in most industries.

Second, the platform dilemma. FDE's economics depend on "platform foundation plus on-site customization," but the exact problem with a large number of Chinese software companies is "on-site presence with no platform"—every deal is a labor business, writing code from scratch every time. For these companies, what matters more than learning FDE's form is filling in FDE's foundation: first distill the highest-frequency customizations into reusable components, and only then talk about forward deployment. Without Chapter 7's replication leverage, FDE in China will only degenerate into "on-site outsourcing with a nicer-sounding name"—which is exactly why the domestic pioneers wrote "bringing a product foundation" into their own definitions.

Third, the talent dilemma and the talent opportunity. Chinese engineering culture—able to endure hardship, quick to respond, full-stack capable, accustomed to solving everything on-site at the customer—is precisely the closest match to FDE's requirements; that's the "opportunity." But the "dilemma" is that, for the past twenty years, this kind of talent has been trapped in a per-diem-priced outsourcing system, with market price signals chronically undervaluing them. The spread of the FDE concept in China may bring about a far-reaching side effect: **re-pricing hundreds of thousands of delivery engineers across China**. Once they learn that their counterparts across the ocean earn a median annual salary of $385,000, the value coordinates of this profession may start to shift.

Established enterprise software companies are also confirming this judgment. SAP's Greater China president, Yuan Xin, said on a podcast that the roles of Forward Deployed Engineer and traditional ERP consultant are converging, and what's most scarce right now is "hybrid talent who have both product engineering ability and a deep understanding of the business." She also offered a line worth remembering: the biggest challenge in landing AI is organizational inertia, not technology—the historical debt of basic data architecture won't disappear automatically just because AI has arrived. (see Appendix C for sources)

### Judgment

FDE will not cure all of Chinese enterprise software's chronic ailments, but it offers a rare opportunity for "rehabilitation": redefining "being close to the customer" from a mark of low-end outsourcing back into a mark of high-end capability. What the Chinese market lacks isn't engineers willing to go on-site—it's the platform, methodology, and pricing structure needed to make on-site work generate compound returns. Whoever fills in these three pieces first will be the one to grow China's own Palantir—or perhaps grow some new species that hasn't been named yet—in this, the world's largest and most complex enterprise services market.

## 8.5 180 Days of an AI Startup Team: A Complete Field Review of FDE in Practice

This final case comes from a Chinese AI startup team I followed and researched (anonymized at the team's request, hereafter "Company N"). It doesn't have Palantir's platform or OpenAI's halo, but its 180-day journey fully demonstrates how this book's methodology operates within a resource-constrained team. To protect commercial information, the numbers have been blurred, but the proportional relationships and decision logic remain true to life.

### Days 1 to 30: Choosing the Battlefield, Not Grabbing the Contract

Company N builds enterprise knowledge-base AI, and had three potential customer leads in hand: a top-tier brokerage (large budget, grand ambitions), a regional chain retail group (mid-size budget, concrete pain point), and a top-tier hospital (high influence, sensitive data). The sales instinct was to lunge at the brokerage. But the team, following the methods of Chapters 2 and 3, ran a triple screening and an on-site due diligence: the brokerage's problem was a "universe-scale requirement" (the very first meeting wanted to cover the entire company), with no clear business owner; the hospital's pain point was genuine, but data-residency restrictions made the delivery timeline uncontrollable; the retail group's CFO personally oversaw the project, the pain point was concrete ("operations oversight reports for 3,000 stores—regional managers simply can't keep up with reading them all"), and although the data foundation was messy, it was reachable.

The team chose the retail group—not the highest lighthouse value, but it passed all three screens. This is the discipline from Section 3.1: **schedule priority by strategic value and learning value, not by contract size**.

### Days 31 to 75: Getting on Site, Shadow-Work Method, and a "Killed Big Plan"

Two engineers and one industry consultant went on site. For the first two weeks they didn't write a single line of product code—they did shadow work, following regional managers as they toured stores, watching how they chased store-level data on WeChat groups and did weekly reports in spreadsheets. The key discovery came from a "workaround": the regional managers didn't look at the reports in the company's data system at all—the data source they actually trusted was a form the store managers filled out by hand every day, because "the system's data is three days late, and often wrong anyway."

This discovery overturned the original grand plan (an intelligent data analysis platform), and the team narrowed the minimum viable deployment down to a single, small cut: **just do a "daily store anomaly report"**—every morning at 8am, roll up yesterday's anomaly signals across 3,000 stores (sales anomalies, inventory anomalies, spikes in complaints) into a report readable in three minutes, pushed straight to regional managers via WeChat. Real data, a single pain point, a six-week deadline.

### Days 76 to 120: The Quiet War of Activation

Launching the system was only the beginning. The activation period ran into two textbook obstacles. The first was trust calibration: in the first week, the AI misjudged two stores' promotional peaks as "anomalous spikes," and the managers complained in the group chat. The team didn't argue—within 48 hours they had wired a "promotion calendar" into the judgment logic, and—the crucial move—publicly thanked, in the group chat, the manager who had reported the error. The second was champion cultivation: a senior regional director (an uncrowned-king type) initially watched coldly from the sidelines; the team invited him to help revise the evaluation criteria, and his three pieces of hard-won experience were written into the system's rules. Two weeks later, he became the system's most enthusiastic evangelist.

By day 90, the report's organic open rate had stabilized above 85%. By day 120, the retail group's CIO voluntarily showcased the system at a quarterly business review—the internal champion had been turned into a salesperson.

### Days 121 to 180: The First Brick of Replication

The renewal negotiation held no surprises, but the team made two more important moves. First, they distilled the assets that had been repeatedly used throughout this delivery: retail-industry data-ingestion components, an "anomaly signal" evaluation framework, an on-site due-diligence checklist for the store scenario—the first scenario playbook took shape. Second, using the retail group's case (the customer agreed to a joint announcement), they opened the door to a second customer—the channel management department of a fast-moving consumer goods brand, in a structurally identical scenario. The delivery cycle of the second project was 40% shorter than the first: the replication flywheel had completed its first turn.

180 days, a small team, no funding announcements, no disruptive technology. But it validated the plainest conclusion of this entire book: **the right question + real data + hands-on service + institutionalized discipline = a snowball that can keep rolling**.

Five sets of cases, complete. From the intelligence community to an Iowa farm field, from top-tier law firms to Chinese chain retail—the wider the span of the scenarios, the clearer the commonality of the methodology becomes. Two questions remain: what boundaries should such a force hold itself to (the Afterword); and the action checklist for those entering this field (the Appendix).
