# Chapter 3 Winning the Customer

> "Enterprises buying AI is like your grandmother getting an iPhone — she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her."
> — a16z (Andreessen Horowitz)

## 3.1 Screening Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson in acquiring internet product users is screening seed users: 100 users who love you beat 10,000 users who think you're okay. The FDE-world equivalent is the lighthouse customer — the kind of customer who not only gives you revenue but sends a signal to the entire industry.

The strategic value of a lighthouse is multiplied under the FDE model, for three reasons.

First, a lighthouse is your strongest sales asset. Enterprise customers have long decision chains and high risk aversion, and peer endorsement is the shortest path to persuasion. The origin story of legal AI company Harvey is a textbook case: the first major customer announced in February 2023 was Allen & Overy, a top global law firm with 3,500 lawyers and 43 offices. Once this lighthouse lit up, major clients like PwC and Latham & Watkins followed in quick succession — in the legal industry, where pedigree matters most, landing Allen & Overy was effectively landing a passport to the entire top-tier law firm market. Palantir's early history follows the same pattern: the CIA was its most demanding, and most endorsement-valuable, customer, and the trust of the intelligence community later became the key that unlocked Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

The process by which Harvey lit up this lighthouse is itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Allen & Overy formed a dedicated group — the Markets Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling — and began secretly trialing the product of this then-unknown startup. Notice how they validated it: not by watching a demo, but through full-firm real-world use. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had posed about 40,000 real work questions to the system, spanning 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling's conclusion became a line repeatedly quoted across the legal industry: "I've been doing legal tech for 15 years, and I've never seen anything this game-changing." Another partner demonstrated a specific scenario to the media: asking the system to prepare a ten-page brief for a U.S. client on "how to open a bank in Luxembourg" — "and it actually did it." (see Appendix C for sources)

Second, a lighthouse determines your product's genetic makeup. Under the FDE model, on-site learning flows back into the product — meaning your first ten customers effectively help shape your product. Choose the wrong lighthouse, and the product gets pulled in a direction with no generality. a16z's first piece of advice to startups, "sell smart," is exactly about this: a young company cannot satisfy everyone, so it must choose an ideal customer profile that shares at least some commonality in system environment and use case, so that the learning from each engagement accumulates instead of canceling itself out.

Third, the quality of a lighthouse matters an order of magnitude more than quantity. Your capacity is inherently scarce — an elite team can deeply serve only a single-digit number of customers at any one time. Pick the wrong one, and the cost isn't "one lost deal" — it's a top team stuck in the mud for half a year. So the three high-risk signals from Chapter 2 (no business owner, refusal to provide real data, cosmic-scale requirements) need to be checked here too, plus one lighthouse-specific test: agree at the outset of the engagement — will they be willing to stand up publicly if it succeeds — co-publishing a case study, speaking at industry conferences, hosting site visits for your prospective customers? A lighthouse unwilling to speak publicly on your behalf is worth at most half as much.

### Beware of "Demand Locusts"

Building internet products means watching out for "product locusts" — early users who swarm in, use it up, leave, and mislead product direction along the way. The FDE-world equivalent species is the "demand locust": customers with ample budgets and strong demand who drain your team without generating any compounding returns. Three identifying traits: their needs clearly diverge from your company's strategic direction and yield no reusable capability once done; they treat FDEs as cheap outsourced labor, dispatching work by headcount rather than aligning on outcomes; and they generate massive internal political friction, turning your main job into helping one department prove another wrong. It's hard to say no to demand locusts — their contract values are often tempting. But remember Barry's accounting: a wrong pilot burns not just the immediate cost, but also your team's time, morale, and the product compounding that could have grown on the right customer instead.

## 3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

Y Combinator, the famous incubator, has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." The founders of the earliest home-sharing platform went door to door photographing hosts' homes; the founder of online payment company Stripe personally installed software for users on the spot.

When McGrew was asked in a podcast about the relationship between FDE and this maxim, he gave a precise formulation: **the FDE model is doing things that don't scale, at scale**.

This line captures the philosophical core of FDE customer acquisition: in the enterprise market, there is no scalable shortcut to earning trust — only the hard, dumb way. Three layers, each building on the last.

Layer one: people must show up in person. On a16z's list of advice for frontline deployment teams, the very last item is just four words: show up in person. The reasoning is that "it's a cliché, but clichés become clichés because they're true" — showing up not only improves sales but also multiplies the success rate when untangling internal power dynamics and driving adoption of new tools. Enterprise customer trust is priced by "number of face-to-face meetings" and "shared experiences." Remote meetings build familiarity; only fighting side by side builds trust.

Layer two: hands must get dirty. OpenAI's FDEs worked the fields alongside agronomists in Iowa farmland; Palantir's engineers lived for weeks at a time on oil rig platforms and aircraft assembly lines; Harvey's FDEs did adoption demos with each partner individually inside law firms. None of these scenarios is "efficient," but each produces something remote communication never could: a firsthand feel for the customer's situation. That embodied sense translates directly into solution quality — only after tramping through the mud of a farm field will you understand why that seemingly perfect phone interface is completely unusable under harsh outdoor sunlight.

Layer three: be a servant first, a mentor second. The most common mistake when an FDE first arrives on-site is coming in with a "we're here to save you" posture. Get the posture wrong, and every intelligence channel closes. The correct order is to first perform the most unglamorous services: fix a data problem for the client's analyst, fill in an interface document for the IT department, automate a weekly report for the business team. These "dumb tasks" earn three strategic assets in return: a true map of the internal organization (who has real say, who is trusted, who is a hidden key node), the truth about the data environment (which data is trusted, which is for show), and — most importantly — certification of "this outsider is one of us." Only once that identity is established does what you say begin to carry weight.

The endpoint of doing dumb things is not staying dumb forever. Chapter 7 will discuss how to distill this grunt work into a replicable playbook — but before you can scale, you must first wade through the mud to find the path worth replicating.

## 3.3 Trust Dividends: The Endorsement Goldmine of Benchmark Customers

Enterprise procurement is a market of "extreme information asymmetry, extremely high cost of failure," where decision-makers rank the trustworthiness of information in a steep hierarchy: a vendor's own marketing is less trusted than analyst reports; analyst reports are less trusted than peers' public case studies; peers' public case studies are less trusted than peers' private recommendations. That last tier — "someone I know used it and said it actually works" — outperforms every other channel in conversion. And the FDE model happens to be the best machine for producing "private recommendation material": what you deliver isn't a software license, it's a client executive's line at an industry dinner: "they really know what they're doing."

Mining this goldmine involves three layers of action.

Layer one: turn delivery into "a story worth telling." For a client executive to be willing to spread the word for you, your delivery must be tellable as a story that makes them look good in front of peers. This requires the delivered outcome to have three narrative elements: a concrete number ("70% reduction in chemical use," "anti-money-laundering investigations down from hours to minutes"), a concrete person ("our agronomist worked the fields alongside their team"), and a concrete contrast ("what used to take three months now takes five days"). When the engagement wraps up, proactively help your internal champion prepare this narrative kit — a one-pager, three slides, a version that can be told in 30 seconds. How finely crafted this material is determines how much gets lost when others retell it.

Layer two: turn the customer's success into the customer's social currency. Pharmacy chain Walgreens, after deploying to four thousand stores in eight months using a bootcamp model, became a star at industry conferences; market research firm J.D. Power went even further — after attending Palantir's bootcamp as a customer, it began running bootcamps for its own customers. (see Appendix C for sources) This is the ideal state: the customer showcases your methodology as its own industry leadership. Endorsement upgrades from "thank you" to "proud to be associated with you."

Layer three: be wary of the endorsement's "backlash mechanism." The enterprise market holds grudges. One high-visibility client's failed delivery spreads far faster than ten successes — because failure stories make better gossip. This is exactly why the "rejection mechanisms" of Chapter 2 and the "activation discipline" of Chapter 4 matter so much: the fatal flaw of a lighthouse strategy isn't failing to find a lighthouse — it's letting the lighthouse go dark in your hands.

## 3.4 Using Data to Understand the Customer Inside Out

Before and after an FDE arrives on-site, there's a step called "entry due diligence": before writing a single line of code, use a structured method to fully understand this customer's situation. A complete due diligence checklist contains five maps.

- **Data map**: What data sources does the customer have, who owns each, how is quality, who controls access, and is there dark data you'd never think to ask about — a spreadsheet some veteran employee keeps privately, a report that only circulates by email, a paper ledger? The point isn't "what exists," it's "which one is trusted." In nearly every organization, the official data source and the one employees actually trust are not the same one — and it's the latter you need to connect to.

- **Process map:** The true operating diagram of the target business process — not the version in the process documentation, but the version observed through shadowing, including every step, exception, and workaround never written down. Specifically flag three points: the step that consumes the most time, the step where errors are most costly, and the step where emotions run highest. These three points are usually where the richest value lies.

- **Organization map:** Who initiated this, who pays, who uses it, who can veto it, who is the uncrowned king. The most common cause of death for enterprise projects isn't technology — it's failing to correctly calculate the organizational account: your internal champion isn't senior enough, or is too senior (so senior they have no time for you), or the right people were never brought in. Pay special attention to finding the "knowledge hub" type: not high-ranking, but the person everyone goes to when they hit a real problem. They're both your best source of requirements and, later, the seed node for rollout.

- **System map:** The true state of the technical environment — the list of systems to integrate with and their interface status, security and compliance boundaries, change management processes. The system map determines your deployment architecture and timeline. Many project schedules are doomed on day one because nobody asked: on the customer's side, releasing a new version takes six weeks of process.

- **Political map:** The most sensitive, and most critical, of the five. Whose cheese does this project move? Is the target process being automated someone's department's source of power? Whose job will your solution make look redundant? That well-known report ranked "employees unwilling to adopt new tools" as the number one obstacle, and the root of resistance is rarely laziness — it's fear: fear of being replaced, fear of being proven incompetent, fear of losing one's reason for existing. The function of the political map is to identify the carriers of these fears in advance, and design a "path to a new role" for them into the solution, rather than a "dead end." Chapter 5's discussion of renewal will circle back to this: whoever you've designed into the role of "victim" will become the most determined opponent at renewal time.

Only once all five maps are complete have you truly "entered the site." This due diligence process typically takes one to two weeks, carried out in parallel by "Echo" and "Delta," synchronizing daily. It looks like pure cost, but the math works like this: what two weeks of due diligence saves you is three months of frantic sprinting on the wrong battlefield.

## 3.5 Technical Content Marketing: Building a Trust Engine of Continuous Output

Content marketing is the classic weapon internet companies use to acquire customers. The customer acquisition logic of FDE companies gives their content marketing a distinctive positioning: **not chasing traffic, but chasing the "pre-sale of professional trust."**

The way an enterprise customer finds you isn't by scrolling past your ad — it's this: they hit a hard problem, search, ask around, discover that some company has published extremely professional public commentary on this exact problem, and conclude: "they know what they're doing, let's go with them." The role content plays here is "pre-qualification" at the very front end of the customer's decision chain. Palantir has published scenario-based technical articles for years; OpenAI and Anthropic turn enterprise customer case studies into detailed technical narratives; one influential a16z industry essay set the tone for the entire track — none of this is brand promotion, it's a carefully cultivated trust asset.

FDE company content marketing follows three rules distinct from conventional enterprise content.

- **Rule one: write from the "foxhole view," not the "showroom view."** Conventional enterprise content talks about how powerful the product is, how big the vision is; FDE content talks about how hard the problem was and how we slogged through it. Why did that article by former Palantir engineer Barry about frontline deployment engineering go viral in the industry? Because it was written entirely about things you'd never see on the showroom floor: the waste of reinventing the wheel, negative-margin pilots, burned-out engineers — and that article, which "aired the company's dirty laundry," became the best evangelism for the Palantir model. Truth from the foxhole carries its own penetrating power, because readers can tell the difference between someone reciting marketing lines and someone describing the reality they live in every day.

- **Rule two: open-source your methodology to manufacture "citation-worthy authority."** Publishing your methodology for how you discover, validate, and deliver teaches your peers in the short term, but in the long term defines the industry standard — once the whole industry's customers start using your framework to ask questions ("how do you build your evaluation system?" "what does your deployment checklist look like?"), you've moved from being a vendor to being the one who sets the exam. The emergence and rapid growth of the practitioner-run open-source community OpenFDE in 2026 shows exactly how far this industry's hunger for knowledge remains unsatisfied; whoever systematically satisfies it first gets to hold the pen that defines it. The writing of this very book is, in some sense, a practice of the same logic.

- **Rule three: let the customer's internal champion be the hero of the content.** The attribution logic of a case study article is subtle: the protagonist should be the visionary manager on the customer side, and your team should be "the partner who helped them succeed." Giving the champion the stage is, in effect, extending an invitation — "become like him" — to the next similar character sitting inside a prospective customer.

## 3.6 Getting Through Procurement, Legal, and Security Review

The "gates" of the enterprise market are procurement, legal, and the security review board. A large number of technically successful FDE projects die at these three gates — and they die with no technical dignity at all: contract terms collapse, data processing agreements stall, security questionnaires drag on into the fourth month.

The key to getting through is how you view these three gates: **treat them as obstacles, and you'll crash in the final mile; treat them as part of the delivery, and you actually gain an advantage** — because most technical companies are careless about these three things, and simply taking them seriously wins you the game.

The procurement gate: first understand that procurement's metrics are not your enemy. Procurement's job is to control price, control risk, and ensure compliance. Their pushing back on price isn't personal, it's their job. The right response isn't to push back harder — it's to give them tools they can "hand in as work done": clear staged pricing (so a discount has steps to land on), comparable market benchmarks (so approval has a basis), and — most important — measurable outcome commitments (so the accusation "we overpaid" has no ground to stand on). Outcome-based pricing has an unexpectedly strong advantage here: what's hardest for procurement to approve is "spending where you can't say what you'll get." A pricing model like "X dollars per ticket resolved" is something procurement can understand at a glance.

The legal gate: treat the data processing agreement as a product to be polished. The legal focal points of an enterprise AI project are highly concentrated: will the data be used to train the model? Where is the data stored, who can access it? Who pays if there's a security incident? Whose fault is it when the AI's output is wrong?

Smart teams productize the standard answers to these questions in advance — pre-built agreement templates, clear statements on model usage, tiered incident liability frameworks. Palantir's ability to survive in the intelligence community rests on having built "permissions and audit" into the product core (who viewed which data is fully traceable); Anthropic, in its work with financial customers, makes "traceability and auditability" a selling point of agent design. **Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.**

The security review gate: use a "pre-answered whitepaper" to steal back two months. The standard drill for enterprise security review is a questionnaire with hundreds of items. If you wait for the questionnaire to arrive before answering, every single item needs cross-department confirmation, and the wait is measured in months. High-performing teams instead proactively maintain a "security whitepaper": deployment architecture diagrams, data flow diagrams, encryption and permissions schemes, compliance certifications, and standard answers to every question ever asked in past reviews. Roughly eighty percent of most questionnaires can simply be copy-pasted from the whitepaper. Your response speed itself is the most direct signal of security maturity — security teams have seen countless vendors, and they know exactly what a flustered response means.

## 3.7 Ecosystem Bundling: Standing on the Channel's Shoulders

The ecosystem strategy of the FDE era borrows the shoulders of three types of partners.

Type one: cloud providers and major platforms. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud are themselves one of the master entry points for enterprise AI procurement. Getting into their co-selling systems is like getting a pass straight to the enterprise procurement shortlist. Cloud providers' marketplaces also solve a real pain point: customers can use existing cloud budget commitments to purchase your service, sidestepping the procurement process required for new vendors.

Type two: consulting firms and systems integrators. This is the most dramatic ecosystem shift of 2026. On OpenAI's list of deployment company founding partners, sitting right there side by side, are Bain, Capgemini, and McKinsey — the world's largest consulting and integration giants, having moved from "potential competitors" to "equity-holding allies." The logic is clear: model companies have the technology, consulting firms have customer relationships and industry depth, integrators have the boots-on-the-ground labor — only when all three converge can the giant market called "AI transformation" be swallowed whole. For startups, the lesson cuts both ways: be wary of consulting giants using the ecosystem as cover to eat your delivery layer, but also recognize the real dividend of allying with regional or industry-specific integrators — they hold customer relationships you couldn't build in three years.

The same script is playing out almost simultaneously in China. In July 2026, ByteDance's Volcano Engine signed a strategic partnership with Ernst & Young, one of the Big Four accounting firms: building joint solutions around core scenarios like data governance, financial management, and marketing growth, and most notably, the two sides plan to build a thousand-person-scale FDE team, "creating an AI-native delivery team." Volcano Engine president Tan Dai's words were blunt: the FDE team should "put engineers with both technical and industry backgrounds forward, at the customer site, deeply involved in the entire process of turning solutions into reality." On one side sits a consulting giant flush with customer relationships; on the other, a model platform lacking industry depth — FDE has become the weld point between the two. (see Appendix C for sources)

Type three: the customer's customer. The highest-order form of bundling is embedding yourself into the customer's own value chain. After attending Palantir's bootcamp, J.D. Power began running bootcamps for its own customers — Palantir's capability spills outward along J.D. Power's own customer relationships, and customer acquisition cost approaches zero. You can design this "re-deliverability" into your engagements from the start: can this solution be taken by the customer and used to serve their own customers? If yes, you've moved from being a vendor to being part of the customer's own business model — that's the deepest moat there is.

## 3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Itself a Strategy

Scarcity can manufacture appeal — invitation-only systems, waitlists — these are classic internet product tactics. FDE's scheduling problem is opposite in shape but identical in principle: **your capacity is always smaller than demand, so "who goes first" itself becomes a strategic tool.**

Let's first state the brutal constraint. A qualified frontline deployment squad (one business-side person plus two or three technical-side people) can typically deliver no more than two projects at high quality at the same time. OpenAI started with just 2 engineers in its early days, Sierra's deployment cycles run in months, and a single Harvey deployment at one law firm takes six to nine months — elite delivery capacity is a naturally scarce resource. Scarcity itself isn't scary; failing to price scarcity is.

The first principle of scheduling is to rank by strategic value, not by contract value. The decision matrix has only two dimensions: this customer's lighthouse value (strength of industry signal), and this customer's learning value (how much reusable capability it will generate). Customers who score high on both dimensions can — and even should — be prioritized with subsidized resources: Barry recalls Palantir's early days of doing free pilots and burning millions of dollars, betting on exactly this matrix. Large contracts that score low on both dimensions are actually the most dangerous kind — they look like a lot of money, but they lock an elite team down for half a year.

The second principle is to productize "waiting." If a customer has to wait three months in the queue before their engagement starts, don't let those three months go to waste: give them a checklist for data preparation (so entry accelerates delivery), suggestions for organizational warmup (which key people to line up first), and lightweight remote diagnostics (keep the relationship warm, clear mines in advance). A well-managed waiting period can shorten the delivery cycle after entry by a third — waiting shifts from a burden on customer experience to a component of delivery quality.

The third principle is: never commit to parallel work beyond your capacity. Your delivery quality relies on the same team's continuous focus — every project carries an entire body of customer context on its back, and switching contexts means reloading that context, so the loss from running multiple projects in parallel is far greater than it seems. It's better to let sales sell "we're booked out through next quarter" as scarcity (which often even lets you raise your price) than to run a team ragged across three customers. The better business gets, the harder it is to resist taking on one more deal — and the cost of an overload collapse is often far greater than the cost of turning down one deal.

## 3.9 Making Your Brand Ubiquitous with Open-Source Tools and Experience Kits

There's a distinctive customer acquisition play in the FDE world: turn the things you do well into open-source tools and demos, so the target customer's engineers can start using them first.

The logic: the initiator of enterprise procurement is an executive, but the technical veto power sits with engineers. Getting a target customer's engineers to start using your stuff in their daily work is like planting a vote of trust at the technical end of the decision chain in advance. Three typical carriers.

Open-source tools and components. Take the general-purpose modules distilled from your delivery process — evaluation frameworks, connectors, deployment templates — and open-source them. Anthropic turned the Model Context Protocol (an open standard for connecting models to external tools) into a published specification, and the artifacts an FDE delivers on-site at the customer are built on this protocol — the very first thing the customer's engineers learn is Anthropic's technology stack. **The process of a standard becoming widespread is the process of trust accumulating.**

Self-service "technical showcases." Enterprise products usually don't have self-service trials, but they can have technical showcases: public demo environments, sandboxes (isolated practice environments), interactive tutorials. Palantir's bootcamp is, at its core, turning "trial" into a ritual; for a resource-constrained team, a quick-start kit that lets a customer engineer hook up sample data and get something running in a single afternoon is the best sales engineer you'll ever have.

Presence in engineering communities. In the technical communities of your target industry, your engineers' presence keeps showing up — answering questions, sharing lessons from mistakes, submitting code. The conversion path for this kind of presence is long, but it reaches exactly the people who hold technical veto power. FDE companies' recruiting and customer acquisition converge on this exact action: the best candidate pool and the best customer leads often come from the same community.

## 3.10 Writing Proposals and Proof-of-Concept Plans

The highest-value-per-word text in the enterprise market is the proposal and the proof-of-concept plan — worth its weight in gold, no exaggeration.

The bad news is that most technical teams' proposals make the same mistake: writing entirely about "what we're going to do," instead of "what you will get." A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted-pyramid structure.

Layer one: the business outcome, stated in one paragraph. The proposal's first paragraph must state the business outcome in the customer's own language: "Within eight weeks, cut your bank's average anti-money-laundering investigation processing time from 4 hours to 15 minutes, freeing up investigative capacity equivalent to 2.5 times your current team." Without this sentence, the customer's executives simply won't read past it.

Layer two: the value validation path — how we'll prove it's been achieved. Spell out acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, and the exit mechanism for each phase. What this layer signals is "we're willing to be tested" — in an enterprise market that has been burned by over-promising, being willing to be tested is the scarcest form of sincerity.

Layer three: the delivery method — how we'll do it. Only now does the technical approach come in, and it must be written so non-technical readers can follow along: architecture diagrams annotated with business context, milestones paired with decision points. Make sure to spell out what's required of the customer — data access, key personnel's time commitment. Writing clearly what the customer needs to do serves two purposes: clearing mines in advance, and filtering customers.

Layer four: risks and countermeasures — how we've thought through how this could fail. Most proposals avoid discussing risk, as if mentioning it were bad luck. Quite the opposite — what sophisticated buyers trust most is a vendor who "spells out exactly how this could die": what if data quality doesn't meet the bar, what if key personnel change, what if accuracy doesn't hit the threshold. This layer is the proposal's trust amplifier, and the textual embodiment of Chapter 2's principle of "rejecting expensive failure."

The proof-of-concept plan has one further special feature: it's the vehicle for the "graduation criteria." The plan must clearly state the maximum duration, acceptance metrics, and both possible follow-ups — graduation and parting ways. A vaguely written proof-of-concept plan is effectively issuing a construction permit for a proof-of-concept graveyard.

## 3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundaries of Hybrid Delivery

FDE delivery has undergone an important evolution over the past few years: from purely on-site, to a hybrid mode of "primarily remote plus on-site presence at key milestones." Palantir's own official position confirms that many projects today are executed mostly remotely, with on-site presence concentrated at key milestones.

The rhythm design of the hybrid model is a craft. Which things absolutely require physical presence? Experience converges on three categories: the early stage of relationship-building (first meetings, shadowing, building trust with executives — trust is something you cannot build through a screen, you must meet in person); high-intensity co-creation (bootcamp-style joint building, whiteboard battles over key architectural decisions); politically sensitive periods (rolling out the solution, coordinating across departments, change management — these are moments where you need to read the room). Which things are actually better done remotely? Deep coding, documentation, routine iteration — work that needs uninterrupted flow.

The hybrid model has two additional hidden dividends. One is cost structure: on-site presence is one of the largest variable costs in FDE, and a well-tuned hybrid ratio can pull ten-plus margin points of room out of delivery. Two is talent sustainability: teams who travel half the year burn out at markedly higher rates — on practitioner forums, complaints about travel top the list. The hybrid model isn't laziness — it's insurance for organizational endurance.

But the bottom line must be drawn clearly: **"never meeting in person" FDE is a false proposition**. If a project goes from kickoff to delivery entirely remote, what you lose isn't just the relationship — it's the thing this chapter and Chapter 2 have argued for repeatedly: firsthand knowledge of the ground truth. Remote work sustains trust and context that already exist — and those must first be created in the physical world.

## 3.12 Overseas Expansion

The proposition of FDE going global presents two interesting camps in 2026.

American companies' globalization is demonstrated by the giants: OpenAI's FDE hiring footprint covers San Francisco, New York, Seattle, as well as London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi — the geographic distribution of FDE teams precisely traces the global map of enterprise AI purchasing power: North America, Western Europe, developed East Asian economies, and Middle Eastern sovereign wealth.

FDE going global in the Chinese context follows a different logic. The past predicament was "product genetics": domestic vendors have long run highly customized, project-based businesses, and their organizational capabilities don't match the standardization and productization overseas markets demand. But the FDE model offers a reverse perspective: **the delivery culture of Chinese engineers — able to endure hardship, quick to respond, full-stack capable, accustomed to solving everything on-site at the customer — is precisely the closest fit to what FDE requires.** So the question was never whether Chinese engineers are suited to FDE work, but whether the platform foundation behind them is thick enough. Among the giants, ByteDance's Volcano Engine has established an FDE team for the Doubao large model; among startups, some domestic teams have written "FDE does not equal on-site outsourcing" into their websites — both paths are being explored.

On the specific strategy for overseas expansion, FDE has one inherent advantage and one inherent constraint. Advantage: the "lighthouse to endorsement" logic is equally effective in global markets, and enterprise customers in developed countries have more mature willingness to pay and stronger contractual discipline. Constraint: FDE is a heavily localized business — language, time zones, compliance, local trust networks all require local teams, not remote support. This means FDE's overseas expansion inherently carries a higher cost structure than online software going global, and the pace must be more disciplined: first gain a foothold with products that can be delivered remotely, then build local delivery teams in key markets, country by country.

The twelve things this chapter covers boil down to one sentence: **the essence of winning a customer is becoming, in a market that has been let down countless times, the exception worth trusting.** Signing the contract is only the ticket to entry — what comes next is the real battle: bringing the system to life, and getting people to actually use it.
