# Chapter 1 The Rise of the FDE

> "The biggest challenge in building software for spies is that I don't know any spies."
> — Bob McGrew, early Palantir executive, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI

## 1.1 First, Let's Talk About a Dead Nine-Million-Dollar Project

The story always starts the same way. In the conference room of a large enterprise, the vendor's demo has just wrapped up. The large language model answers fluently, the dashboards glow with data, and even the pickiest executives can't find fault with it. The CEO makes the call on the spot: sign it. The contract is worth several million dollars, hands are shaken, photos are taken, and a press release goes out.

Nine months later, the project is dead.

It doesn't die with a bang — it dies quietly. The system is still running, the servers are still on, but no business unit is actually using it. The vendor delivered every feature in the contract; the enterprise paid every dollar in the contract. The only thing that never arrived was "value."

If you think this is just bad luck, MIT's famous 2025 report will tell you: this is the norm. They interviewed 52 organizations, collected 153 executive surveys, and reviewed more than 300 public enterprise AI projects. Their conclusion boiled down to a single sentence: of the thirty-to-forty billion dollars poured in, **95% produced no measurable financial return whatsoever**.

95%. In other words, the dominant story of enterprise AI adoption isn't success — it's systemic failure.

What's more interesting is the shape of the failure. The report specifically noted: the problem isn't the model. The models that dazzled everyone in the demo remain just as intelligent once in production — they simply "don't remember feedback, don't retain context, and don't enter the workflow." They look like products but function like exhibits. Stanford's aggregate data from the same year corroborates this from another angle: 88% of organizations are using AI in some form, yet only a single-digit percentage of agentic applications actually make it into production. McKinsey delivered the final blow: only 6% of enterprises can say AI has contributed more than 5% to their profits.

Around the time the report was published, a manufacturing COO's complaint circulated widely in the industry: "Everyone online says everything has changed. Back on our shop floor, nothing has moved." (see Appendix C for sources) That line hits harder than any statistic — his company wasn't short on budget or tools; what it lacked was someone to actually wedge the tools into the real workflows of the shop floor.

Around the same time, the report's lead author told a story about a control group. A handful of nineteen- and twenty-year-old founders had built startups on generative AI that reached twenty million dollars in revenue within a year. Their playbook was the exact opposite of established enterprises': pick a single pain point and drive it all the way through, locking in tightly on customers who actually use their product. Older companies always try to swallow everything in one bite; the young ones take one small bite, and only move to the next bite once the first is finished. (see Appendix C for sources)

Keep this contrast in mind. It's almost a preview of Chapter 2 of this book.

In truth, this chasm wasn't dug by the AI era. China's enterprise software industry has been lying in it for over a decade: large clients demand customization, vendors lose money on every single deal, the code is abandoned the moment delivery is complete, and the whole industry collectively degrades into "the client's outsourcing shop." Some in the industry put it bluntly: customization is SaaS's natural enemy, and the curse can only be lifted once the market matures on its own. Things are somewhat more dignified in the US, but the script isn't so different: sales signs the deal, implementation moves in, and six months later delivers a system that is "feature-complete but nobody loves," followed by endless finger-pointing.

At bottom, it's the same wall: **the place where software is built and the place where value is created are not the same place**. On this side of the wall, requirements get passed along through tickets, meeting notes, and weekly reports, losing a layer of fidelity with every retelling; on that side of the wall, the customer's real workflow is hidden in spreadsheets nobody ever documented, in conventions passed down by word of mouth, in the kind of tacit knowledge summed up as "you'll have to ask old Wang about that." The software industry has invented countless ladders to scale this wall — requirements documents, user research, implementation methodologies, customer success programs — and the wall is still standing.

Until one company decided: forget the ladder. Send the people over instead.

## 1.2 Palantir's Victory

In 2003, Silicon Valley was just crawling out of the wreckage of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel and a handful of young Stanford alumni founded a company named after something out of *The Lord of the Rings* — Palantir, the seeing stones, magical rocks in the story that let you glimpse things far away. What they set out to do sounded like science fiction: build data-analysis software for the US intelligence community, weaving together fragments scattered across countless classified databases into a coherent picture, helping analysts catch terrorists.

This business came with a premise that would break any product manager on the spot. Years later, early executive Bob McGrew recounted the story vividly on a podcast:

> "Our goal when we started the company was to build software for the intelligence community — bluntly, to build software for spies. And one challenge with building software for spies is that I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either. And even if you happened to find a spy, and asked him, 'so how exactly do you do your job,' he typically wouldn't tell you."

No user interviews, no requirements documents, no usability testing. The first lesson of internet startup methodology was entirely void here. One of the founders, Stephen Cohen, came up with a solution so simple it was almost charming: build a demo, take it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. The response was blunt: "This is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat — he asked back: "So what would you want it to be like instead?" Then he pulled out a notebook, wrote everything down line by line, went back, made the changes, and brought it right back.

This clumsy loop was the embryo of the FDE. Buried inside it were two intuitions that would later prove worth their weight in gold: first, in complex domains, customers don't know what they want until they see something that works; second, the fastest way to find out what a customer wants is to put the person who builds the thing right next to the person who uses it.

The person who elevated this intuition into a full corporate strategy was Shyam Sankar, employee number 13. As Palantir moved from its first customer to its second and third, the team discovered a counterintuitive fact: what each customer wanted differed in ways that were subtle but critical. The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a generic product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's customers were the CIA, the FBI, the US military on the battlefield — saying no meant getting cut out entirely. Sankar did the opposite: build a platform flexible enough to be customized, then station engineers on-site at the customer to fix the last mile.

His single most important move was to rewrite the accounting of the whole thing. In the software industry's ledger, "customizing for a single customer" is called services, and it's the enemy of margins. Sankar flipped it around: **on-site customization isn't a cost — it's product discovery**. Every pitfall an engineer hits at the customer site is a signpost for the platform's next evolution.

Sankar himself was the first forward deployed engineer, and his earliest deployment became something like the archetype for the role. Around 2007, roadside bombs were the leading cause of US military casualties in Iraq, and the inter-agency combat operations center formed to address this let Sankar bring a small team and a product that was "still very rough" into a shared secure information facility for two weeks. A secure information facility is a physically isolated classified space where even hands-free phones are prohibited. Sankar came up with a rough-and-ready workaround: he strapped a phone to his head with a rubber band, freeing both hands to type code — one ear listening to feedback from analysts, the other ear listening to colleagues back at Silicon Valley headquarters. For two weeks he worked nineteen hours a day, demoing, ingesting data, collecting feedback, and revising on the spot. By the end, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself, however, was completely wiped out, and he called CEO Alex Karp: "This isn't sustainable, we're finished." Karp's answer would later become part of the company's culture: institutionalize this "unsustainability." (see Appendix C for sources)

Years later, colleagues recalled that Sankar's criticism never carried any emotion. Once, he and colleague Akshay Mehr were chatting happily over coffee at an airport, and moments later Mehr's inbox got a sharply worded critical email from him — written and sent while sitting right across the table. "There was no personal attack in it, just an implicit message: I owe you these hard truths because I want us to win." (see Appendix C for sources)

This playbook quickly proved itself in blood on the battlefield. The Palantir engineers on-site discovered that soldiers didn't need any fancy intelligence charts at all — they just needed a small tool that could mark "this road is suspicious" on a map, since roadside bombs were the biggest killer of patrols. The engineers threw together a crude mapping tool on the spot: soldiers could tap once to mark a dangerous stretch of road, visible in real time to the whole unit. This tool saved lives, and it later became a standard feature of the platform. Note this: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room — only in the moment an engineer and a soldier looked at the same road together.

On the commercial side, the company had to die once first. Palantir's first enterprise-facing product was called Metropolis, and the market response was dismal — only a handful of financial firms limped along using it. Only with the second attempt, Foundry, did things finally take off, and the turning point was Airbus: at the factory in Toulouse, an A380's fuel pump kept failing intermittently, and Airbus's own engineers had spent two years chasing it with no answers. Palantir's people came in, hooked sensor data into the platform, and cracked the case in two weeks — the fuel was sloshing away from the pump during climb. A minor fix that saved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars. Airbus's head of digital later remarked publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months to diagnose." (see Appendix C for sources) From then on Airbus became Palantir's most loyal advocate in Europe, building its own data platform, Skywise, entirely on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and more than fifty thousand users.

There's also a piece of gossip worth recording. It's widely rumored in the industry that Palantir's software played a role in the 2011 operation that killed Osama bin Laden. This claim has never been confirmed, nor has it ever been debunked — the journalist who wrote Palantir's biography deliberately left this ambiguous footnote in place. But regardless of whether it's true, the rumor itself is one of Palantir's best sales weapons. (see Appendix C for sources)

By some point before 2016, the number of forward deployed engineers at Palantir had actually surpassed the number of traditional software engineers. Here was a software company where more than half its engineers weren't writing product at headquarters — they were scattered across customer sites worldwide. For years, Wall Street couldn't make sense of it, dismissing it as "human-wave tactics" or "more like a consulting firm."

Then time gave its answer. In 2023, Palantir launched its AI platform, AIP, paired with a playbook called "Bootcamps" (covered in detail in Chapter 8), compressing enterprise software's typical nine-to-twelve-month sales cycle down to a few weeks. In the fourth quarter of 2025, its "Rule of 40" score — revenue growth rate plus profit margin, a health metric in the software industry where 40 is a passing grade — hit 127%; in the first quarter of 2026, it hit 145%. Bookings for a single quarter reached $4.26 billion, net revenue retention was 139%, and cash on hand stood at $7.2 billion. On the earnings call, Karp offered just one line: "We are a species unto ourselves." The market cap briefly broke through $400 billion. (see Appendix C for sources)

Those who once laughed at "human-wave tactics" had nothing left to say. Palantir proved one thing over twenty years: that wall can't be climbed with a ladder — it can only be climbed by people. Those who did the climbing eventually got a formal name — Forward Deployed Engineer.

## 1.3 What Is an FDE

### The One-Sentence Definition

This book adopts the definition from the best articulator of this model, Bob McGrew — who worked as an engineer at PayPal in his early career, later became an early executive at Palantir, and later still became Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, where the team he led produced ChatGPT and GPT-4:

> **A forward deployed engineer is an engineer stationed on-site at a customer, whose job is to fill the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."**

Every word in this plain-spoken sentence carries weight.

"Stationed on-site" means your working context is embedded within the customer's world: joining the customer's chat groups, reading the customer's data, sitting in the customer's meetings, getting to know the person who "knows why the process works the way it does" — not necessarily sitting in the customer's office every single day. "The gap" is the reason this role exists at all: where a product works right out of the box, you're not needed; the deeper the gap, the more you're needed — in intelligence, finance, manufacturing, medicine, law. "Engineer" is the most important qualifier of all: you write code that runs in production, not reports. When Palantir named this role, it deliberately kept the words "software engineer" in it, to make a point to the world: this is not a consulting position. As for "Forward Deployed," it's a military term referring to forces deployed at the front line — putting your most capable people closest to the problem.

### What It Is Not

The fastest way to understand a new role is by elimination.

It's not pre-sales. Pre-sales work ends before the contract is signed; its goal is to win the deal, and its output is a slide deck. The FDE's work only enters the deep water after the contract is signed; its goal is to win the outcome, and its output is a system running in production. Pre-sales is responsible for convincing the customer "this can work"; the FDE is responsible for actually making it work.

It's also not staff augmentation or outsourced on-site consulting — a distinction that matters especially for Chinese readers, given how long and how disreputable the history of "engineers stationed on-site" has been domestically. The first wave of service providers in China to fly the FDE banner used three sentences on their websites to draw a clean line between it and staff augmentation: staff augmentation bills by the hour; FDE delivers in stages and is accepted based on results. Staff augmentation writes everything from scratch on-site; FDE brings a product foundation to the engineering work. Staff augmentation drags on the longer it stays, and the system stops when the people leave; the FDE finishes and departs, but leaves the capability embedded in the system and the customer's team. In short: staff augmentation sells headcount; FDE sells results.

Jove, who heads the FDE function at Silicon Valley AI customer-service company Cresta, drew this boundary even more finely in a video interview. His team is scaling from 30 to 100 people this year, and his view is: **the FDE role only makes sense when it's bound to an AI platform** — if it's just traditional data integration and system building, it becomes hard to distinguish from a traditional implementation engineer or an outsourcing shop. He has one hard bar for hiring: in the agent era, not being able to code is like being illiterate. Another notable mechanism is the dual mandate: FDEs are not only accountable for making the deployment succeed, but also carry a metric for "making the product more mature" — whatever is learned on-site must feed back into the platform. (see Appendix C for sources)

It's not a management consultant. A consultant delivers advice on a project basis and isn't accountable for execution; the FDE is accountable for the ultimate operation of the system, and the finish line is "the customer's own team can operate it independently." The collaboration between Anthropic and fintech company FIS is a good example: engineers embedded with FIS to co-build an anti-money-laundering agent, cutting investigation time from hours to minutes, but the stated goal of the collaboration was not to deliver a system — it was to "transfer knowledge so that FIS can build its own agents going forward." A consultant hopes you'll keep needing them; an FDE hopes that sooner or later you won't.

It's also not a traditional product engineer. A product engineer faces an abstract user — personas (what does the user look like), funnels (how many people go from visit to paying), daily active users; the FDE faces a concrete customer — a bank's risk-control department, a farm in Iowa, a patrol unit outside Baghdad. Palantir internally has a well-known division of roles: platform engineers are responsible for "one capability, serving many customers," while forward deployed engineers (internal codename "Delta") are responsible for "one customer, marshaling many capabilities." What platform engineers pursue is a feature that works everywhere; what FDEs pursue is solving the problem of the one customer in front of them, completely.

### How This Trend Took Off

A role invented in 2003 — why did it only become the hottest thing in 2025?

The most direct trigger was generative AI. Large language models created an unprecedented gap: anyone can put together a dazzling demo in five minutes, but connecting that demo to an enterprise's real data, permissions, compliance, and workflows is an order of magnitude harder. Model companies gradually realized: **the decisive battle going forward isn't model quality — it's deployment capability**.

The numbers trace a steep curve: in the first nine months of 2025, FDE job postings rose eightfold; on YC's job board, more than a hundred startups were listing this position, which barely existed three years earlier. Venture firm a16z called it flatly "the hottest job in tech," with a vivid metaphor to go along with it: enterprises buying AI is like your grandmother getting handed an iPhone — she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her.

A set of Financial Times reports from November 2025 offers the best cross-section for observing this wave. Fournier, OpenAI's head of forward deployment for Europe, said the team was only founded a year earlier and was about to scale to 50 people, "the demand has exceeded our expectations"; Anthropic's head of applied AI, de Jong, put it even more vividly: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are completely different species." — which is why her team is scaling fivefold in a year. Prit John, who leads Palantir UK, condensed the company's creed into a single line: "Software only has value if it's genuinely meaningful to the end customer." Even Cohere's CEO, Gomez, came out to endorse the model: "We embed engineers right from the start of the contract, and pull back once the customer is running smoothly." (see Appendix C for sources)

The intensity of the talent war shows up in three hard numbers as well: OpenAI's forward deployment team started with 2 people and grew to 52 within a year; Salesforce, which literally wrote "we don't do our own implementation" into the textbook, publicly committed to hiring 1,000 FDEs; even Deloitte, which makes its living selling consulting, set up a dedicated FDE business line in December 2025. (see Appendix C for sources)

Then came the giants voting with their capital. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the formation of a "Deployment Company": majority-owned by OpenAI itself, jointly backed by 19 top-tier capital partners including TPG, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, with an initial investment exceeding $4 billion and a reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion — and, in the same stroke, it acquired a consulting firm with 150 deployment engineers. A few hours later, it was revealed that Anthropic was forming a rival venture with Blackstone. On the same day, the two largest model companies both elevated "deployment" from a cost center to a strategic asset — with capital markets casting the most expensive possible vote for the FDE model.

The deepest reason of all is still the one McGrew saw clearly: "AI agents are a category with no incumbent, so there is a massive amount of product discovery still to be done." What customer-management software should look like has had a standard answer for twenty years; what an agent should look like, nobody knows — including the customers themselves. The only place to find the answer is on-site at the customer.

History has come full circle here: in 2003, Palantir invented the FDE because it "didn't know how spies worked"; in 2025, the entire industry embraced the FDE en masse because it "didn't know how agents should work inside an enterprise." Twenty-two years, the same answer.

## 1.4 The Responsibilities and Traits of an FDE

### A Résumé Built for This Role

If the question is "what kind of career is FDE," McGrew's résumé is almost the standard answer.

His first job was at PayPal, as an early engineer. That cohort later became known as the "PayPal Mafia," and it profoundly shaped all of Silicon Valley. After leaving, he joined Palantir in its startup phase and rose all the way to executive, personally living through the entire process of FDE going from an emergency measure to corporate strategy. When he was managing the product and engineering teams, he offered a famous metaphor: forward deployed engineers carve out "gravel roads" leading to value at the customer site; the product team's job is to judge which gravel roads are worth widening and paving into "highways" that can serve ten more customers.

Later still, he became Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, leading the development of ChatGPT, GPT-4, and the o1 reasoning model. In other words, this is a person who built the platform on this side of the wall, climbed over to the customer site on that side of the wall, and, in the end, helped build with his own hands the very technology that made this wall higher than it had ever been.

Something interesting happened at a 2025 YC AI conference. McGrew expected entrepreneurs to crowd around him asking "how did you invent ChatGPT," but instead everyone kept asking the same question: **how does Palantir's FDE model actually work?** A man who helped invent ChatGPT was, above all, being grilled on delivery methodology. That detail is the best footnote to the direction of the times.

### Three Layers of Traits

Drawing on more than twenty job postings and firsthand accounts from practitioners, the traits of an FDE can be summarized into three layers.

Layer one: a sufficiently broad technical generalist. An FDE doesn't need to be the deepest expert in any one domain, but they must be able to independently solve full-stack problems on-site at the customer: write code, debug interfaces, understand data pipelines, deploy to the cloud, get a feel for how large language models behave, and also understand the "utilities" of an enterprise environment — single sign-on (log in once, access everything), permissions, and compliance certifications. The hiring market puts a clear price tag on this combination: a 2026 compensation report shows that mid-level FDEs at top-tier AI labs have a median total compensation of about $385,000; senior FDEs, about $610,000; and principal-level FDEs, over a million — higher than most pure research-and-development roles at the same seniority, because the market knows how scarce this combination of skills is. (see Appendix C for sources)

Layer two: the ability to translate technology into business outcomes. This is the watershed between an FDE and an ordinary engineer. One widely quoted line from a practitioner: "The model is usually the cleanest part. The hard part is finding the workflow nobody ever wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Palantir's hiring bar states it even more bluntly: "The candidate's articulateness, clarity, and comfort in communication should make me want them to run a meeting with a customer." Interviews also screen for this translation ability. OpenAI's and Palantir's FDE interviews both feature a signature stage called "problem decomposition": you're handed a huge, ambiguous, real enterprise problem, and for sixty minutes you don't write a single line of code — the interviewer is only watching how you probe further, how you scope the problem, and how you build order out of chaos. The interviewers' advice is: understand the problem before you jump in — slow is smooth, and smooth is fast. In a normal interview, "I optimized the query by 40%" is a perfect answer; in an FDE interview, the perfect answer is: "I optimized the query by 40%, which meant the customer's analysts got their reports two hours earlier every day, and the team's processing capacity tripled." A technical achievement only counts as a complete answer once it's been converted into the customer's language.

Layer three: ownership, plus a touch of "rebelliousness." A line circulates among practitioners that captures exactly the kind of accountability this role demands: "The deployment goes down at 2am. You don't file a ticket. You don't blame another team. You don't go back to sleep. You fix it. Period." Palantir has an even subtler expectation for people in business-facing roles: they must have deep industry knowledge and also be bold enough to be a "rebel" — able to see the absurdities in a customer's current state, and willing to push for ten-x change rather than incremental improvement. CEO Karp's benchmark of behavior back in the day was "the French waiter": embedded within the service, sensitive to real needs, yet possessing enough confidence and taste to guide the customer from "what they think they want" to "what's actually good for them."

### A Day in the Life

In practice, an FDE's time roughly breaks down as follows: 40–50% is spent on the customer's side writing code and configuring systems; 20–30% is spent aligning direction with customer leadership, decomposing problems, and making architectural decisions; 10–20% is spent distilling patterns learned on-site back into the company's product line; and the remainder is evaluation, optimization, and knowledge sharing — writing playbooks, evangelizing internally, and training the customer's team.

Buried in this schedule is an important piece of information: an FDE is not "an engineer dispatched elsewhere," but "an engineer carrying a dual mission" — delivering results to the customer on one side, and feeding intelligence back to the company on the other. That's the subject of the next section.

## 1.5 Let Results Do the Talking

If you had to sum up the FDE's working creed in one sentence, it would be: let results do the talking.

First, separate "data" from "results." Enterprise software history has never been short of projects with beautiful data and terrible outcomes: a feature checklist 100% ticked, but only 5% of users actually using it; system availability at four nines, while business units still prefer to keep using spreadsheets. In the MIT report, the vast majority of the 95% of failed projects weren't lacking dashboards — the numbers were all there, but the value never showed up, because nobody was accountable for that one line item on the P&L.

The FDE model structurally guarantees that "results" don't get diluted, through three specific mechanisms.

- **Payment tied to results:** In its early days doing government work, Palantir widely adopted arrangements where "you only pay once it works." McGrew's recollection is blunt: "Early on, it was reasonable for a startup to bear all the risk itself — you succeed, then we get paid." This logic has evolved even further in the AI era: Sierra charges by "resolved conversation" — no resolution, no charge; many FDE service providers deliver in stages, with acceptance tied to results. Once payment is tied to outcomes, the entire behavior of the delivery team gets reordered — you'll stop spending three weeks polishing a feature nobody uses, because "nobody uses it" comes out of your own pocket.

- **Success metrics defined before work begins:** The first step of an FDE project isn't writing code — it's defining, together with the customer, "what counts as success." Palantir's Bootcamps require the customer to first lock in an extremely focused core battlefield — "cut scheduling conflicts on this one production line by 30%," rather than "explore AI-enabled manufacturing" — precisely to prevent the project from drifting into unfalsifiability under the banner of "exploration." OpenAI's collaboration with John Deere is a textbook example: agronomy experts first reviewed hundreds of real work cases together to build a custom evaluation framework, and only then did model iteration begin. The final figure — "up to a 70% reduction in chemical use" — wasn't a marketing gloss applied after the fact; it was the target locked in before work started.

- **The final judge is a change in the customer organization's behavior:** The report contained a biting finding: only around 40% of enterprises provide employees with official, subscribed AI tools, yet as many as 90% of employees routinely use consumer-grade personal products to solve work problems. This means a large number of "successfully launched" projects are actually in a state where "the official system idles while employees route around it." In the FDE philosophy, going live is not the milestone — the customer's team changing how it works is. Sierra deliberately named the role internally "agent engineer," and its head, Moehler, explained the selection criteria this way: they only take on two kinds of problems — problems that are genuinely hard, and problems that genuinely matter to the business — and both conditions must hold at once.

"Let results do the talking" sounds like common sense, but executing on it is an affront to the entire structure of interests involved: salespeople can no longer over-promise, because the delivery team is accountable for results; the customer's IT department can't get away with handing in a "feature checklist," because business-unit adoption rates have become the acceptance criteria; and the FDE themselves can't hide behind "I built what was requested," because whether the requirement itself was right or wrong is also on their ledger. This is precisely why this role is so valuable — and also precisely why it's so expensive.

## 1.6 The FDE's Four Faces on a Team

An FDE lives simultaneously in four worlds, and functions as the connective tissue between them.

- **To the customer, they are an "embedded product manager plus full-stack engineer":** they observe the customer's real work the way an anthropologist would — the most valuable discoveries usually come from *watching*, not *asking* — and, like an entrepreneur, they act directly on what they observe, building it on the spot. Palantir has institutionalized this pairing: the deployment strategist, codenamed "Echo," is responsible for understanding the customer's mission, stakeholders, and adoption path; the forward deployed engineer, codenamed "Delta," is responsible for technical execution. The two work as a pair — one diagnoses, one builds; neither can be missing.

- **To the company's product line, they are "outposts and intelligence officers":** this is the most fundamental difference between an FDE and a traditional delivery team. In traditional implementation, the cost is a sales cost — every person-day spent has to be earned back out of the contract. A healthy FDE organization treats on-site work as R&D — if three customers all hit the same integration gap, that's not three separate headaches, it's one piece of product intelligence; if five deployments all need the same workflow, that should be abstracted into the platform's next standard capability. A former Palantir engineer recalled that key components of the Foundry platform were born on-site at customers scattered across the globe — Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse — growing organically from the bottom up, eventually feeding back into a product generating billions of dollars a year in revenue. (see Appendix C for sources)

- **To sales, they are a "trust amplifier":** enterprise customers have been let down too many times, and they're immune to slide decks. FDEs rebuild trust through two moves: first, hands-on action — building something that actually runs, on the spot, using the customer's own data in the customer's own environment; second, honesty — being willing to say no to a customer's mistaken assumptions. Palantir's Bootcamps have turned this trust-building into a repeatable process: the customer brings real data, a deployable prototype is built within one to five days, and executives try it out with their own hands. Early Bootcamps had a paid-conversion rate of only 5–10%; the company has since disclosed that the later conversion rate is close to 75%. Trust, it turns out, can be engineered.  (see Appendix C for sources)

- **To the organization itself, they are a "talent furnace":** an easily overlooked fact — Palantir has produced a startlingly dense cluster of entrepreneurs. This isn't surprising: the daily training of an FDE — taking something valuable end-to-end from resource-constrained, ambiguous, multi-stakeholder conditions to something people actually use — is almost a complete dress rehearsal for being a founder. Srinivas, who went on to found Decagon; Moehler, who built Sierra's agent engineering team; and several of the authors behind the industry's most widely circulated methodology essay — all came up through Palantir's forward deployed roles. One company's talent overflow became an entire industry's talent infrastructure. (see Appendix C for sources)

Put these four identities together and they point to the same conclusion: the FDE isn't a box on an org chart — it's an upgrade to how an organization learns. It turns "understanding the customer" from information passed down through layers of hearsay into muscle memory built by an engineer's own hands.

## 1.7 How to Hire an FDE

First, a splash of cold water: the FDE is one of the hardest roles in the software industry to hire for, because it demands that one person excel simultaneously along two dimensions that usually trade off against each other.

Former Palantir engineer Barry laid this out clearly in a retrospective piece: Palantir's bar for hiring an FDE is "an engineer who could get into Google or Meta" — because they're going to the customer site to *build* systems, not to tune parameters. But technical strength alone is far from enough; forward deployed people also need creativity, judgment, and charisma in front of customers. He added a sharp remark: this is far more expensive and difficult than hiring a traditional pre-sales team.

Breaking down real-world hiring practice, there are three key components to hiring an FDE.

- **Candidate profile:** hire "curious bulldozers," not "polished craftsmen." a16z's advice to startups used the phrase "relentlessly curious doers": strong self-direction, low reverence for the status quo, and a hunger for the customer's problem. McGrew is even more specific: an FDE team needs two kinds of people — "domain rebels," people who understand an industry but aren't beholden to its conventions, and "prototype speedsters," who prioritize speed over perfection and accept that the first version will get thrown out and rewritten. Conversely, two types of people prized in traditional engineering culture are actually red flags for an FDE role: "craftsmen" who put elegant code above customer outcomes, and "loyal executors" who treat every word from the customer as gospel.

- **Interviewing:** replace rote formulas with a "decomposition round." The "problem decomposition" mentioned earlier is the soul of the FDE interview: give the candidate a vague, sprawling problem with the rough edges of a real business — "a bank's compliance team manually reviews thirty thousand transaction alerts a day, ninety percent of which are false alarms; what do you do?" — and then observe for sixty minutes. What's being evaluated isn't the answer, it's the process: does the candidate clarify constraints before diving in, do they distinguish root causes from symptoms, do they remember there's a real user sitting at the other end of the system, and can they clearly explain trade-offs. Palantir also embeds roughly twenty minutes of behavioral questions into every technical round, and it explicitly rejects candidates who are technically strong but a poor culture fit — with the single most important culture item being a sense of order in the face of ambiguity.

- **Compensation:** accept a hybrid structure of "engineering pay plus business-performance flexibility." 2026 market data offers an anchor: the median total compensation for a Palantir forward deployed engineer is around $215,000 a year; mid-level FDEs at top AI labs are around $385,000, senior FDEs around $610,000; Anthropic's role has a base salary between $200,000 and $300,000. Another notable detail is bonus design: Palantir's bonuses are often tied to business metrics like account expansion, sitting somewhere between an engineering bonus and a sales commission; a16z's advice is to align incentives with account managers, but not to load hard sales quotas onto FDEs — that would steer behavior toward closing deals rather than results. Compensation structure is the final step in defining a role: how you pay people ultimately shapes how they behave. (see Appendix C for sources)

## 1.8 How to Become an FDE

Now flip the perspective: if you're an engineer, product manager, or consultant looking to break into this fast-growing market, what's the path?

Start by self-assessing: the glamour and the cost of this role are two sides of the same coin. In practitioner forums, discussions of FDE carry an unusually candid tone. On the upside: the best combination of technical depth and brand credibility available, one of the rare roles that lets you accumulate technical, commercial, and customer relationship capital simultaneously. On the cost side: a quarter to half of your time on the road is normal — OpenAI's job posting explicitly states travel of up to 50%; your work rhythm is dictated by the urgency of the customer, not your own schedule; and there's a recurring warning — the risk of burnout is real.

One comment is sharp enough to be worth quoting verbatim: "Some people treat it as a brand springboard, others call it consulting with a cool title slapped on — both are right. The difference is whether the company you're at feeds what's learned on-site back into the product, or just sells you as a body." This line is both a career-choice criterion and the subject of Chapter 7 of this book. (see Appendix C for sources)

Next, fill the gap — and the gap isn't technical, it's the craft of "translation." Technical foundations are just the entry ticket; most engineers have those. What's genuinely scarce is three kinds of translation ability — translating a business problem into a technical problem (Chapter 2), translating a technical solution into language executives can understand (Chapter 3), and translating on-site experience into knowledge the team can reuse (Chapter 7). To practice these three, doing beats studying: sit in on a pre-sales call, take a shift on-site, train a real user, and see which of these discomforts teaches you the fastest.

When preparing for interviews, rewrite your résumé to be "customer-outcome oriented." The principle was stated earlier and bears repeating: every technical achievement on your résumé needs to travel the last mile into the customer's language. "Built a retrieval-augmented system" is engineer-speak; "Built a retrieval-augmented system that cut first-response time in customer service from four hours to eight minutes, prompting the customer to proactively ask to expand the contract at renewal" is FDE-speak. Also have two kinds of stories ready: one where you established order amid unclear requirements, and one honest failure — Palantir's interviewers are notoriously insistent on "tell me about a real failure," because the essence of this job is moving forward through uncertainty, and someone who won't admit failure can't evolve.

When choosing a company, ask three questions back. First, "what is your product platform" — an FDE without a platform foundation is pure staff augmentation. Second, "how does on-site learning flow back into the product" — ask the company to give a recent example of something learned on-site becoming a product feature; if they can't produce one, that's a red flag. Third, "who does the FDE report to" — reporting into product or engineering usually means the model is being taken seriously; reporting into sales means you should be wary of becoming a manpower pool for pre-sales.

## 1.9 The FDE's Standard Toolkit

To close this chapter, here is a full picture of the tools this role currently relies on. Tools go out of date, but the layers of capability behind them don't. Five layers, from the ground beneath your feet to the base at your back.

- **Platform foundation layer — the company's weapon.** The entire premise of the FDE model is "bringing the platform to the field"; otherwise it degenerates into pure custom development. Palantir's Foundry and AIP are built around something called the "Ontology" — modeling an enterprise's data, logic, and actions into a semantic layer, letting AI operate on a foundation that "understands the business"; OpenAI's model APIs and agent tool-chains; Sierra's agent platform. When evaluating any FDE opportunity, the depth of this layer is the top priority.

- **AI engineering layer — the individual's craft.** The everyday craft of the post-2025 era includes: prompt engineering and context management; retrieval augmentation, letting the model look things up before answering; evaluation frameworks, building measurable yardsticks for fuzzy notions of business quality — this is the signature skill that separates an FDE from a traditional implementation engineer in the AI era; agent architecture — tool calling, multi-agent collaboration, keeping a human in the loop at critical checkpoints; and engineering optimization for cost and speed.

- **Data and integration layer — the first battle upon arrival.** Nearly every FDE project spends its first week wrestling with this layer: data pipelines, enterprise system connectors, permissions and identity authentication, vector databases for the AI to look things up in, and data governance and masking (obscuring sensitive information). One practitioner's takeaway: 70% of a project's schedule gets stuck here, but it's invisible in the demo — it's the part of the iceberg below the waterline.

- **Delivery and collaboration layer — survival gear inside the customer's environment.** Working inside a customer's security perimeter means "double adaptation": you have to know how to use your own modern tool-chain, but also be able to fit yourself into the customer's environment — which might be an internal network physically air-gapped from the internet, might mean you can only deploy inside the customer's own cloud environment, or might mean you can't even reach a code-hosting website. Containerization (packaging up your environment to carry with you), infrastructure as code (managing server environments through code), and the contingency plan for "getting your environment running even in a conference room with no internet" all belong to this layer.

- **Knowledge distillation layer — the lever for scale.** This is the layer most easily overlooked, yet it's the one that determines whether a team can escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount": playbooks, component libraries, deployment checklists, and the habit of rewriting "one customer's solution" into "a pattern for a class of customers." Chapter 7 will dedicate itself entirely to this layer.

Put the five layers of the toolkit together and you get the complete silhouette of this role: platform underfoot, engineering in hand, results in sight, and the product line at your back.

That is the FDE. In the seven chapters that follow, we move into the heart of the methodology, starting from the very first decision of any project — how to make sure you're solving the right problem.
