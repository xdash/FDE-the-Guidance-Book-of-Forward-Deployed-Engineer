# Chapter 8 — The Complete Casebook

The methodology of the first seven chapters was all distilled from real practice on the ground. This chapter restores those sites in full: five sets of cases, from the model's inventor to its latest inheritors, from Silicon Valley to China. As you read, it helps to look back at the first seven chapters with one question in mind: how were these methods done, as if by intuition, before the day they were named?

## 8.1 Palantir: Twenty Years Refining a "Dumb Method" into a Moat

### The founding: building software for "users you can't ask"

Silicon Valley in 2003 was still lying in the wreckage of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel pulled together a group of young Stanford graduates to do something that sounded insane: build data-analysis software for US intelligence agencies. The company was named after the seeing-stone in *The Lord of the Rings* — Palantir, the stone that lets you see far away.

The insanity wasn't in the technology, it was in the users. Bob McGrew's widely circulated recollection years later put the predicament most clearly: "One challenge with building software for spies is, I don't know any spies. You probably don't either. And even if you happen to find a spy and ask him, 'So how do you actually do your job day to day,' he usually won't tell you."

The entire methodology of the software industry — user interviews, questionnaires, usability testing — collectively failed in front of this user group. The response of co-founder Stephen Cohen was charmingly clumsy: build a demo, show it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. The answer was merciless: "This thing is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat; he pressed: "Then how would you want it to be different?" Then he wrote down every comment, went back and revised, and brought the revision back to their door. Round and round.

This loop is the FDE's genetic fragment, and it contains two intuitions that later proved worth a fortune: customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something usable; and the fastest path to knowing "what they want" is to stand the person who builds the thing next to the person who uses it.

### Taking shape: from a stopgap to an organizational strategy

Early Palantir advanced along the path of "one customer, one customization," and soon hit the problem every enterprise-software company hits: what the second customer wanted differed "in small but critical ways" from the first.

The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a general-purpose product, and say no to the differences. But Palantir's customers wouldn't accept "no" — they were the CIA, US forces on the battlefield. The one who truly led the company out of this deadlock was early employee Shyam Sankar (later company president and CTO). His solution was seen as heresy at the time: not building two products, nor a rigid single product, but a highly customizable platform, sending engineers to embed at the customer's site to finish the last mile.

Sankar's most crucial move was a rebellion on the ledger. In the financial language of the software industry, "customizing for a single customer" is called services cost, the enemy of margins, to be minimized. Sankar redefined it as "product discovery" — on-site customization isn't spending money, it's collecting intelligence for the product's next evolution. Swapping one term changed a company's capital-allocation logic: what others saw as a cost center became Palantir's R&D center.

The organizational form took shape accordingly. Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") embed with the customer, "one customer, marshaling many capabilities"; deployment strategists (codename "Echo") understand the customer's mission and organization; platform engineers in the rear, "one capability, serving many customers." By some point before 2016, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers outnumbered its platform engineers — a software company with most of its forces at "the front."

### Trials: the battlefield, the hurricane, and the oilfield

The value of the FDE model was proven repeatedly at one extreme site after another.

On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, roadside bombs were the patrol's number-one killer. Embedded Palantir engineers found that soldiers had no interest in elegant intelligence charts — all they wanted was "mark this road as suspicious on the map." The engineers cobbled together a crude map tool on the spot: a soldier taps once, and the whole squad sees the dangerous stretch. This tool directly reduced casualties and later settled into a standard platform feature. Note: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room, only in the moment an engineer and a soldier looked at the same road together.

And the archetypal site of this play predates the map tool. Around 2007, Sankar led a small team into the SCIF of the US counter-roadside-bomb operations center for two weeks of joint work. Even speakerphones were banned in the SCIF, so he strapped the phone to his head with a rubber band, freeing both hands to write code — one ear on the analysts' feedback, the other on colleagues back at Silicon Valley HQ. Nineteen hours a day: demo, ingest data, gather feedback, revise on the spot.

At the end of the two weeks, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself was wrecked, and called Karp: "This isn't sustainable, we're finished." Karp's answer would later become company culture: turn that very "unsustainability" into an institution. (Source in Appendix C.)

At the 2012 Hurricane Sandy disaster-relief scene, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers stitched scattered relief data into a real-time map, coordinating the deployment of rescue resources. Oil rigs, aircraft final-assembly floors, bank trading halls — the engineers took "embedding" to a depth unprecedented in the software industry. Former Palantir engineer Barry recalled that the core components of the Foundry platform were born at customer sites in Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku — a world map with every point a site of on-site creation.

The cost was just as real. Barry's record is essential reading as a cautionary tale for understanding this model: top engineers, global travel, heaps of reinvented wheels, free pilots, "many projects with literally negative-infinity margins"; in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held," chaotic enough to keep shareholding employees up at night. Palantir could swallow these costs thanks to two things: the capital depth propped up by sky-high contract values, and a "run pilots like venture bets" mentality — most bets going to zero is fine, and the few that win have to earn it all back.

Commercialization died once first, too. The first enterprise-facing product, Metropolis, got a dismal market reception, and the second attempt, Foundry, is what opened the account. The turning point was Airbus: at the Toulouse plant, a fuel pump on the A380 kept failing, and Airbus's own engineers spent two whole years without a clue. Palantir's people came in, wired the sensor data into the platform, and cracked it in two weeks — the fuel sloshed away from the pump body as the aircraft climbed. A trivial fix preserved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars.

Airbus's head of digital transformation, Marc Fontaine, later remarked publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months." (Source in Appendix C.) Airbus became Palantir's most enthusiastic evangelist in Europe from then on, building its entire Skywise data platform on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and more than fifty thousand users.

### The explosion: the bootcamp ignites a commercial empire

In 2023, generative AI exploded. Every enterprise wanted to "get AI going," and every enterprise was stuck at the same place: it didn't know how. Palantir's answer was to compress its twenty years of embedding methodology into an industrialized machine — the AIP bootcamp.

The rules are minimal: the customer brings real data and a real problem, a Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer accompanies them, and in one to five days they build a deployable AI-application prototype, free or for a nominal fee. Day 0, lock onto an extremely focused core battleground; Day 1, connect the data and build the Ontology model; Days 2 to 3, engineers and customer technical staff write code back to back, wiring the large language model into the business flow; Days 4 to 5, executives operate the system with their own hands and decide on the spot.

The throughput and conversion rate of this machine stunned the whole industry: from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, multiplying year over year, to nearly 6 a day at the 2025 peak; the enterprise-software sales cycle of nine-to-twelve months compressed to weeks; the pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to four thousand stores in eight months via the bootcamp; customers like the market-research firm J.D. Power, once they learned it, began running bootcamps for their own clients — the model started to self-replicate. The contract cadence after the bootcamp was fast enough that even peers might not believe it: a large healthcare company signed a five-year, $26-million-a-year agreement five weeks after attending a bootcamp; a global bank, after a one-month pilot, first signed for $2 million and four months later expanded to a three-year, $19-million-a-year deal. (Source in Appendix C.)

The financial results then exploded: US commercial revenue grew 137% year-over-year in Q4 2025; the "Rule of 40" reached 127%, surging to 145% in Q1 2026; $4.26 billion signed in a single quarter, net revenue retention of 139%; market cap briefly breaking $400 billion. The Wall Street analysts who had mocked its "human-wave tactic" began rewriting their research reports overnight.

The biggest vote of trust came from the US Navy. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Karp jointly announced the "shipbuilding operating system" contract: $448 million, first using Foundry and AIP to transform the submarine industrial base — two large shipyards, three naval dockyards, a hundred suppliers. The pilot data was covered earlier: scheduling from 160 hours to 10 minutes, material review from weeks to an hour. The Secretary of the Navy specifically declared "this isn't a concept, isn't a pilot, isn't research" — it skipped the proof of concept and went straight to production, because the earlier embedded pilots had already proven the value. That same year, the US Army also bundled 75 scattered service contracts into a single ten-year, $10-billion framework agreement handed to Palantir. The government's trust is the compounding of twenty years of embedding. (Source in Appendix C.)

An engineer named Nabeel Qureshi, who spent a full year embedded in Toulouse, left an even more vivid footnote: he spent four days a week on the final-assembly floor, writing software for the A350 production line alongside manufacturing workers — he called the thing "Asana for building airplanes." A Silicon Valley engineer living a full year in a foreign factory — that is the plainest literal meaning of the phrase "forward deployed." (Source in Appendix C.)

### Methodology restored

Looking back at the first seven chapters, the Palantir case is practically an index to the whole book: the demo loop and "the customer doesn't know what they want" (Chapter 2); customization as product discovery, gravel roads and highways (Chapters 1 and 7); the strategic value of lighthouse customers (Chapter 3); the portfolio-investment logic of free pilots (Chapter 6); on-site tools growing bottom-up into a platform (Chapter 7); and the deepest creed of all — complexity can't be eliminated remotely, someone has to show up (the whole book).

## 8.2 OpenAI: When the People Who Built ChatGPT Start Hauling Bricks

### The turn: from "model as product" to "deployment as strategy"

OpenAI's first seven years were a pure technical epic: the GPT series racing ahead, ChatGPT setting the fastest user-growth record in human history. In such a company narrative, "enterprise delivery" sounds like the business of an industry next door.

The turn came in 2024. The enterprise-market data made one thing impossible to ignore: the later-famous "95% failure rate" was playing out; meanwhile, the share map of the enterprise-large-model market shifted violently — industry tracking showed Anthropic climbing to about 32% in the enterprise market, while OpenAI fell back from its early absolute lead of around 50% to about 25%. The gap in model capability was narrowing, and the standard by which enterprise customers voted with real money quietly became another question: **"who can help me actually get this thing going?"**

OpenAI's response came in two steps. First, in 2024 it formed a forward-deployment engineering team — starting with 2 engineers, led by Colin Jarvis, rapidly expanding into a global network across San Francisco, New York, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, and Sydney, growing from 2 to 52 in a year. The job posting was unabashed: "embed with customers where model performance is critical, delivery is urgent, and ambiguity is the default," with travel up to 50%. Europe head Fournier put it more plainly: "Market demand has exceeded our expectations." (Source in Appendix C.)

Second, on May 11, 2026, it formed a "deployment company" — a joint venture majority-owned by OpenAI, backed by 19 top-tier capital firms (TPG leading, with Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield co-leading, and Goldman Sachs, SoftBank, Warburg Pincus, and others participating, and Bain, Capgemini, and McKinsey as founding partners), with an initial investment over $4 billion and a reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion; it simultaneously acquired the applied-AI consultancy Tomoro, folding in about 150 deployment engineers as a unit. COO Brad Lightcap took the helm personally.

The structural design of this new company deserves a place in business-school textbooks, because it welds "where customers come from" to "how capital comes back." The funders are private-equity giants, and these giants hold hundreds of portfolio companies spanning healthcare, manufacturing, finance, retail, and logistics — a ready-made customer pool for the deployment company; in exchange, OpenAI reportedly promised these PE funders a five-year annualized floor return of 17.5% (per media reports, unconfirmed by OpenAI officially), while retaining super-voting rights and control of strategic direction.

The acquired Tomoro is no nobody either: engineers at this British applied-AI consultancy had previously done enterprise implementation at Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and the game company Supercell. In a single day, OpenAI assembled all three cards of "engineers, channel, capital." (Source in Appendix C.)

A company valued in the hundreds of billions, holding humanity's strongest model, forming a separate ten-billion-dollar company just for "sending people to do work at customer sites" — this is the heaviest weight in the history of the FDE model.

### On the ground: John Deere's fields

The best display window for OpenAI's FDE methodology is the collaboration with John Deere. This nearly-two-hundred-year-old agricultural giant set out to solve a problem grown out of the soil: weed control. The traditional approach is to spray herbicide across the whole field — high cost, heavy chemical damage, high environmental cost. The ideal of precision agriculture is "spray only when you see a weed," but the difficulty of recognition is: every field has a different weed profile, different crop growth, a different season.

The OpenAI forward-deployment team's approach is a full demonstration of the methodology of Chapters 2 and 4: fly to Iowa, go into the fields with agronomy experts, understand the workflow and constraints of precision agronomy (including that non-negotiable deadline — the growing season); with the experts, review hundreds of real field cases and encode "what a good spraying recommendation is" into a bespoke evaluation system; and rapidly iterate the model and system under the evaluation system's care. The final result: chemical use reduced by up to 70%, and farmer interaction frequency up 6×.

Three details worth underlining hide in this case. First, the deadline is the growing season, not the project schedule — the FDE's rhythm must obey the customer's business rhythm. Second, the evaluation system precedes model optimization — define "good" first, then pursue "good." Third, the 70% figure isn't marketing dressed up after the fact; it's the target set jointly with the experts before work began.

### On the ground: BBVA's 120,000 people

Another benchmark is BBVA. The starting point of the collaboration wasn't dazzling — deploying ChatGPT Enterprise. But the compounding of the FDE model lies in this: the start is a tool, the path is the organization. Starting from 3,300 accounts, employees spontaneously created more than 20,000 custom mini-assistants, 83% used it actively every week, saving on average about 3 hours a week; a year and a half later, the deployment expanded into a system covering 25 countries and 120,000 employees, and the bank's goal upgraded accordingly to "building an AI-native global bank." From "giving a tool" to "co-building an organizational form" — this is the extreme form of Chapter 6's "cultivating the base." (Source in Appendix C.)

### Methodology restored

The lesson of the OpenAI case lies in "a model company's self-awareness": when model capabilities converge, deployment capability becomes the main battlefield of differentiation; when the product form is undecided (no one knows what an agent should look like), the field becomes the only site of product discovery. Its three-stage working method (co-creation, validation, delivery), its engineering obsession with the evaluation system, and the capital-structure innovation of the deployment company (using private-equity capital's industrial network as a customer channel) correspond respectively to the core propositions of Chapters 4, 6, and 3.

## 8.3 Anthropic and the Vertical Three: Three Variations of the FDE

If Palantir invented the model and OpenAI pushed it onto the headlines, then Anthropic and a batch of vertical-AI companies show three variations of the FDE in different soils.

### Variation one: Anthropic — safety genes and the differentiation of "auditability"

Anthropic's FDE hangs under the applied-AI team, and its job posting has two qualifiers worth savoring: one is "safe and reliable" — all delivery must meet Anthropic's safety standards; the other is "defending the company's mission on the front line" — the FDE is simultaneously an ambassador of values. In 2025, this team expanded at a rate of fivefold a year, and its lead, Kate de Jong, explains: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are two completely different species." (Source in Appendix C.)

The direct product of differentiation is "auditability" becoming a product feature. The collaboration with fintech giant FIS is the benchmark: Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineers embedded in FIS to co-design a financial-crime detection agent, compressing anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes, with every decision fully replayable and traceable, first landing at Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank. In a heavily regulated industry, "every AI decision can be explained clearly to the regulator" isn't a bonus point, it's the entry pass — and Anthropic made it a selling point.

The evidence that "safety obsession" becomes a killer weapon in the enterprise market is written in customers' reviews. The cybersecurity company Palo Alto Networks deployed Claude to 2,500 developers, and the engineering director's review was blunt: "They care more about safety than anyone else — every meeting talks about safety implications. As the biggest cybersecurity company, this matters to us." The result: feature-development speed up twenty to thirty percent, and junior developers completing integration tasks 70% faster. The pharma giant Novo Nordisk uses it to write clinical-research documents: reports that used to take a dozen-odd weeks now draft in ten minutes. Meanwhile, it partnered with the consulting giant Accenture to train 30,000 certified Claude consultants, and its enterprise-customer count grew from fewer than a thousand to over 300,000 in two years. (Source in Appendix C.)

The deeper differentiation lies in the official commitment to "knowledge transfer": the explicit goal of embedding is to let FIS "independently build and scale its own agents in the future." Writing "teach the customer" into the contract weakens your own indispensability in the short term, but in the long term is the most forceful answer to the "vendor lock-in" challenge — Gartner predicts that before 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon such solutions due to vendor cost and skill hollowing-out, and Anthropic's approach is to defuse this time bomb in advance.

In May 2026, Anthropic was reported to be forming an enterprise-AI-services joint venture with Blackstone and others, reportedly around $1.5 billion in scale, focused on embedding Claude into mid-sized enterprises' operations — going head-to-head with OpenAI's deployment company on the same day. Two model giants completing the organizational form of "deployment as strategy" on the same day says more about the FDE's coordinates than any analysis.

### Variation two: Sierra and Decagon — startups' "delivery as product"

Sierra (founded in 2023 by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor and former Google VP Clay Bavor, already valued at $4.5 billion at its October 2024 raise and reportedly climbing since) turned the FDE model into the business model itself: the customer pays not for the software but for "resolved conversations"; the implementation is managed by Sierra's forward-deployment team, and the customer need only contribute the brand's tone and operating rules. The effect: brand customers like Sonos, Casper, WeightWatchers, and ADT clustering in, a fastest publicly reported go-live of four weeks, and an annual-contract threshold commonly from the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Outcome-based pricing anchors Sierra's pricing naturally to customer value — the most radical real-world sample of Chapter 6's "outcome-based pricing."

Sierra's internal name for the role is worth noting too: not FDE, but "Agent Engineer." Its lead, Natalie Meurer (five years at Palantir, having handled law-enforcement, defense, and infrastructure customers), explains: the name should describe "the shape of the technical work," not just "obsession with the customer" — building a voice agent requires a special "taste" (what sounds human, what sounds right), a narrower and deeper craft than the general FDE. The collaboration with the ticketing platform Vivid Seats is her team's brightest battle report: four-week go-live, self-resolution rate up 40%, customer satisfaction up 35% — and the mechanism of conversation data feeding back into the product lets "ten thousand people asking about the same feature in a month" immediately jump in priority. (Source in Appendix C.)

Decagon (co-founder Ashwin Sreenivas came out of Palantir, total funding $231 million) took another path: productizing delivery experience directly into agent operating flows — letting the customer's operations staff define multi-step workflows in natural language, which the AI executes deterministically. The customer list: Notion, Duolingo, Eventbrite, Rippling. Reportedly, Chime's self-resolution rate reached 70%, Duolingo's ticket-deflection rate reached 80%, and ClassPass's customer-service cost dropped 95%. Decagon's FDEs play more the role of "the people who teach the customer's team to use this system" — the goal of delivery is to give the customer's non-technical staff self-serve capability, the best practice of Chapter 7's "can it be used by people who can't write code" among the four questions of productization. (Source in Appendix C.)

### Variation three: Harvey — breaking into places ordinary software can't reach

The legal industry is the "Bermuda Triangle" of enterprise software: top law firms have strict confidentiality discipline, data doesn't leave the domain, and partners each govern their own turf — self-serve online software struggles for a single win here. Harvey (founded in 2022, already valued at $11 billion after a new round in March 2026) broke in by sheer force with the FDE model.

First remember this company's origins: two founders, one a litigator from a law firm, one a scientist from DeepMind (Google's AI lab); a $5 million seed round from the OpenAI Startup Fund. (Source in Appendix C.)

Reviewing its play is practically a perfect medley of the book's methodology. Lighthouse strategy — the first major customer locked was the top global firm Linklaters (February 2023, 3,500 lawyers, 43 offices), followed in succession by PwC (over 4,000 legal professionals), Cleary, and Macfarlanes; champion cultivation — Linklaters' internal liaison David Wakeling (head of market innovation) became the deployment's in-firm axis; deep rhythm — cut in through a single practice group, driven by a partner champion, expand horizontally after six months of live use; the customer-research loop — partners pay but don't do the work, associates do the work but don't pay, KM lawyers see both sides' blind spots, and the FDE's interview system must penetrate all three tiers at once, "the hardest part of the deployment isn't the model, it's the customer-research loop"; an ultra-long cycle — a single-firm deployment takes six to nine months, with the FDE responsible from the document-management system's data pipeline all the way to partner-by-partner adoption demos.

The effect is a rare, fierce upward momentum in vertical AI: over 100,000 lawyer users and 1,300 organizations, covering most of the US top-100 firms; annual recurring revenue rising from about $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026; a valuation jumping four times in a year — $3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, $11 billion. (Source in Appendix C.)

Harvey proved where the boundary of the FDE model lies: **in any market where the three mountains of "confidentiality regime, data residency, and key-user autonomy" exist at once, productized delivery has no solution — only when people go in can the system be brought in.** And such markets — law, healthcare, finance, government — happen to be the highest-margin enterprise-service markets on Earth.

## 8.4 China Cases: Planting the FDE on the Soil of "Project-Based Business"

China is the world's most distinctive enterprise-software market: it has the most diligent delivery engineers, the most demanding customization needs, and the deepest "project-based curse" — big enterprises want customization, vendors lose money on every deal, cost can't be amortized, they're reduced to client outsourcing, and the profession hollows out. The fate of the FDE model on this soil is a question that must be answered separately: is it the antidote to the curse, or a new disguise for it?

First, see clearly how contorted this situation is, through three practitioners' sighs. The first comes from the database company DolphinDB, whose industry long-read asks *Who Is Destroying China's Enterprise-Software Industry*, and answers with six mountains: freeloading, open source, outsourcing, tenders, government-tech subsidiaries, and a deformed market structure — China's top engineering talent long consumed in "highly customized, relationship-heavy, price-war" non-standard delivery. The second comes from an enterprise-software veteran on the podcast *Yingdi Haike*: "Customization is the natural enemy of SaaS, and this curse can only dissolve on its own when the market matures." The third comes from a former president of DingTalk: many companies get stuck at one or two hundred million in revenue — hard to acquire, hard to deliver, hard to operate, high customization cost, with no economies of scale to speak of. 36Kr summed up these three sighs: "Software productization amortizes project-based R&D expense, SaaS amortizes sales expense — only the project-based company suffers most, because the project's cost is void once the team leaves, with no way to amortize it." (Source in Appendix C.) These three sighs are the Chinese question the FDE model has to answer.

### The big-company path: Volcengine's Doubao FDE

Volcengine, under ByteDance, is the most explicit player to fly the FDE flag among China's internet giants: in 2026 it set up a dedicated FDE team to deeply co-create with industries and benchmark customers. Volcengine president Tan Dai's public statements echo this book's definition almost word for word: "The FDE isn't sales, and isn't pre-sales, they must have strong technical implementation ability, especially the ability to land AI code." He also revealed that the team deliberately staffs diverse industry backgrounds (for example, putting someone with a bioengineering background on the biopharma industry), and it now covers key industries like automotive, healthcare, education, finance, and semiconductors. In July 2026, Volcengine also signed a strategic partnership with consulting giant EY, planning to build a thousand-strong FDE team to "explore a new full-stack AI delivery model." (Source in Appendix C.)

The innate advantage of a big company doing FDE is a thick platform base (Volcano Ark provides an integrated base of models, toolchain, and cloud infrastructure); the innate challenge is the big company's sales-system inertia and the financial inertia of "delivery is a cost center" — whether the FDE team can gain a Palantir-style organizational status of "the field is R&D" decides whether it's a real FDE or a pre-sales support department wearing a new name.

### The early-mover path: writing "FDE ≠ staff-augmentation outsourcing" onto the website

More noteworthy is a batch of local startups. They lack the big companies' base, yet use the clearest language to distinguish the FDE from the Chinese market's old species. A service provider focused on the real-estate and facilities-management industry wrote on its website what is currently the most concise defining distinction of the FDE in China:

> One, the FDE delivers by phase and is accepted by results, while staff-augmentation outsourcing bills by the hour; two, the FDE builds engineering on a product base, while staff-augmentation writes and edits from scratch on site; three, the FDE finishes and leaves, with capability settled in the system and your team, while staff-augmentation stays longer and longer, and when the people leave the system stops.

Three sentences, corresponding respectively to three of the book's core propositions: outcome-based pricing (Chapter 6), the platform base (Chapter 7), and knowledge transfer (Chapter 5). It also established a "turn-away mechanism" — won't take an unvalidated scenario, won't take data that exports from a single spreadsheet, won't take a customer who just wants to learn about AI, "we'd rather you started a little later than start at the wrong time." This is a self-aware practice, in the Chinese market, of Chapter 2's "refusing the expensive proof-of-concept graveyard."

Another service provider moved the FDE model into the compliance-most-sensitive finance industry and settled a clear three-stage methodology: scenario diagnosis of one to two weeks (identify the three-to-five highest-value core scenarios, quantify ROI) → embedded delivery of eight to sixteen weeks (full-stack delivery from model selection to production integration) → production deployment and continuous evolution (used daily by the business unit, iterating with the business), while stressing "data doesn't leave the domain" — an on-the-ground adaptation to the regulatory environment of Chinese financial institutions. (Source in Appendix C.)

In June 2026, a practitioner at a domestic investment firm posted a long article online with an eye-catching title: *FDE, Silicon Valley's Hottest Job This Year — We've Been Quietly Doing It for Three Years*. The article records their real felt experience applying this play in Chinese enterprise services: the Forward Deployed Engineer "dives into one customer after signing, with no limit on capability, actually installing AI into his business"; the standard of measurement "isn't how much code was written or how many docs were delivered, it's whether anyone is actually using this system and whether the customer's business got better"; and the summary, fully consistent with this book — "going live isn't the end, he has to stay until this system becomes the customer's daily routine and the customer's own people can take it over." This article was widely reshared in China's practitioner circles, because it was the first to clearly lay out, in Chinese, a comparison table: the product engineer is accountable for the roadmap, pre-sales for signing the deal, implementation for the sign-off sheet, customer success for renewal — and the FDE, for "whether the customer's conversion and labor efficiency truly rose." (Source in Appendix C.)

### The control group: set-top boxes and paper contracts

On whom to invest an FDE and whom not to, Wu Guangyu, CEO of Baidao Data, gave two starkly contrasting customers at a founders' roundtable.

One was a set-top-box customer with an extremely clear need — "how to use AI in a set-top box." The FDE hung out with them and finally built an agent that watches shows and chats about gossip with you, a high return on investment. The other was a going-global customer whose boss opened by wanting to invest $1 million in AI, yet at signing time always printed the contract out and marked it up with a pen, not even using Word's track-changes. Wu Guangyu's judgment was blunt: "A customer with no digital foundation at all — putting an FDE in definitely yields nothing. We told him directly: first buy 100 Gemini accounts and get everyone using it, and talk once you have concrete ideas."

His conclusion mirrors Chapter 2's refusal mechanism: **the premise of the FDE is that the customer has a clear point of need.** Don't lightly invest in vague, unbounded needs; you can split them into phase one, two, three, do the clear part first, and after delivering results, follow-on needs naturally convert into a long-term service contract. (Source in Appendix C.)

### The other side: the vacuum layer and the dirty work

But if you think China's FDE practice is all as clean as the above, a frontline practitioner will hand you a bucket of cold water.

*Growth Hacker AI Weekly* recorded the observations of frontline executor Shen Yue: in quite a few domestic projects, the FDE is more like a vendor's outsourced labor, doing the dirty, exhausting work of filling holes and cleaning up messes. In a ten-million-yuan-level state-owned-enterprise project she lived through, the big boss vanished after signing, there was no one truly responsible in the project, and the FDE was suspended in a vacuum layer — no decision-maker above, no executor below — and could only fight for acceptance by studying the "reporting logic." At another privately-owned home-renovation company in Guangdong, the second-generation-heir boss got straight to the point: help her cut 6,000 people to 3,000. But what AI can replace is tasks, not roles, and the project limped forward amid repeated scoldings.

Shen Yue's summary deserves to be copied here: the difficulty of the Chinese FDE lies in the demand side being layered away, in enterprises' weak digitalization foundation, in bosses' lack of patience — and the over-promises the front-end sales made to sell the product all end up for the FDE to repay. She has another line I used as the epigraph of Chapter 4: "No matter how technology evolves, the human nature and the power-and-responsibility games inside an organization are always a harder problem than technology." (Source in Appendix C.)

### Another voice: is the FDE even a good business?

The doubts don't come only from the execution layer. At the same roundtable, Kuse.ai founder Wu Xiankun raised a question about the FDE model itself, in no light terms: "The FDE has a hard time becoming a good business model." His reasoning is direct: do it shallow, and the model vendors will do it themselves; do it deep, and it's essentially outsourcing, with high delivery cost and hard to scale. "Serving state-owned and central enterprises, you can't refuse out-of-scope requests — where's your leverage? Will you, late at night, doubt you've become cheap outsourcing — this is a question everyone doing FDE has to face."

His solution is quite radical: AI roll-up (consolidation via M&A). Since customers worry about data leakage and teams resent outsourcing betraying their original intent, then buy the company outright, turn everyone into shareholders with aligned interests, and overhaul the whole company top to bottom; after the overhaul, use the same methodology to keep acquiring the next one. His logic: if AI transformation truly has that much value, then you should own the business, not sell hours. (Source in Appendix C.)

This doubt may not have a standard answer, but it forms a curious echo with the Gartner forecast cited in Chapter 5 — that before 2028, 70% of enterprises may abandon FDE-led solutions: **the FDE model's biggest enemy may not be technology, but its own cost structure.** This book doesn't intend to give a standard answer, but it's a question everyone who wants to enter should think through first.

### Three special propositions for the Chinese FDE

Synthesizing these practices and industry observations, for the FDE to hold in China, it must answer three questions its American peers needn't answer.

First, the pricing predicament. The US market can accept subscription, metered billing, even outcome-sharing; the muscle memory of Chinese customers is "perpetual license plus project-based acceptance." A viable transitional form is the "hybrid": payment on phased acceptance (accommodating habit) + value metrics written into acceptance terms (injecting outcome genes) + an annual operations-and-evolution contract (cultivating renewal awareness). Copying per-resolution charging wholesale, in one step, will die at the procurement desk in most industries.

Second, the platform predicament. The economics of the FDE depend on "platform base plus on-site customization," and the problem of a great many Chinese software companies is precisely "has field, no platform" — a labor business writing code from scratch on every deal. For these companies, more important than learning the FDE's form is shoring up the FDE's foundation: first settle the highest-frequency customization into reusable components, then talk forward deployment. Without Chapter 7's replication lever, the FDE in China will only degenerate into "staff-augmentation outsourcing with a nicer name" — which is exactly why the local early movers wrote "brings a product base" into the definition.

Third, the talent predicament and the talent opportunity. Chinese engineering culture — able to endure hardship, fast to respond, full-stack, used to solving every problem at the customer's site — happens to fit the FDE's requirements most closely, and that's the "opportunity"; but the "predicament" is that over the past twenty years, this kind of talent has been trapped in a person-day-priced outsourcing system, with market price signals long undervaluing them. The spread of the FDE concept in China may bring a far-reaching side effect: **repricing China's hundreds of thousands of delivery engineers.** When they learn that their peers across the ocean earn a median annual salary of $385,000, this industry's value coordinates will loosen.

Legacy enterprise-software companies are corroborating this judgment too. Yuan Xin, president of SAP Greater China, said on a podcast that the roles of the forward-deployed engineer and the traditional ERP consultant are merging, and what's most scarce right now is "composite talent with both product-engineering ability and deep business understanding." She also offered one line worth remembering: the biggest challenge of landing AI is organizational inertia, not technology — the historical debt of a foundational data architecture won't automatically vanish just because AI arrived. (Source in Appendix C.)

### The verdict

The FDE won't cure all the chronic ills of China's enterprise software, but it offers a rare chance at "rehabilitation": redefining "getting close to the customer" from a mark of low-end outsourcing into a mark of high-end capability. What the Chinese market lacks isn't engineers willing to go to the field, but the platform, methodology, and pricing structure that let field work compound. Whoever shores up these three first can, in this largest and most complex enterprise-service market in the world, grow a Chinese Palantir — or grow some new species not yet named.

## 8.5 An AI Startup's 180 Days: A Full Debrief of FDE in Practice

This last case comes from a Chinese AI startup I followed and studied (anonymized at the team's request, hereafter "Company N"). It has neither Palantir's platform nor OpenAI's halo, but its 180-day journey fully demonstrates how the book's methodology runs on a resource-limited team. To protect commercial information, the numbers are blurred, but the ratios and decision logic stay real.

### Days 1 to 30: choose the battlefield, don't grab the contract

Company N does enterprise knowledge-base AI, and had three prospective customer leads in hand: a top securities firm (big budget, grand needs), a regional retail chain group (medium budget, concrete pain), and a Grade-A tertiary hospital (big influence, sensitive data). The sales instinct is to pounce on the securities firm. But following the methods of Chapters 2 and 3, the team ran the three tests and on-arrival due diligence: the securities firm's problem was "cosmic-scale requirements" (wanting to cover the whole company in the first meeting) with no clear business owner; the hospital's pain was real, but data-residency restrictions made the delivery cycle uncontrollable; the retail group's CFO personally watched the project, the pain was concrete ("operations-supervision reports for 3,000 stores, regional managers simply can't get through them all"), and the data foundation, though messy, was reachable.

The team chose the retail group — not the highest lighthouse value, but it cleared all three tests. This is the discipline of Section 3.1: **schedule by strategic value and learning value, not by contract size.**

### Days 31 to 75: arrival, the shadowing method, and a "big solution that got cut"

Two engineers and one industry consultant arrived. For the first two weeks they wrote not a line of product code, doing the shadowing method: following regional managers on store visits, watching how they chased store data in WeChat groups and made weekly reports in spreadsheets. The key discovery came from a "workaround": the regional managers didn't look at the reports in the company's data system at all; the data source they actually trusted was a sheet the store managers hand-filled every day — because "the system's data is three days late, and often wrong."

This discovery overturned the initial big solution (an intelligent data-analytics platform), and the team narrowed the minimum viable deployment into a small incision: **just a "store anomaly daily."** Every morning at 8 a.m., yesterday's anomaly signals from 3,000 stores (sales swings, inventory anomalies, complaint spikes) are summarized into a daily readable in under three minutes and pushed to regional managers' WeChat. Real data, single pain point, six-week deadline.

### Days 76 to 120: the hidden war of activation

Going live was only the start. The activation period hit two textbook obstacles. First, trust calibration: in the first week, the AI misjudged two stores' promotional peaks as "anomalous spikes," and the managers grumbled in the group. The team didn't argue; within 48 hours they wired the "promotion calendar" into the judgment logic and — the key move — publicly thanked the manager who reported the error in the group. Second, cultivating influencers: a senior regional director (the uncrowned-king type) initially watched coldly; the team invited him to help revise the evaluation criteria, and three of his rules of thumb were written into the system's rules. Two weeks later, he became the system's most energetic evangelist.

By Day 90, the daily's natural open rate stabilized above 85%. By Day 120, the retail group's CIO proactively showcased the system at the quarterly operations meeting — the internal champion, armed into a salesperson.

### Days 121 to 180: the first brick of replication

The renewal negotiation held no suspense, but the team made two more important moves. First, they settled the assets repeatedly used in this delivery: the retail-industry data-ingestion component, the "anomaly signal" evaluation framework, the store-scenario due-diligence checklist — the first scenario playbook took shape. Second, they used the retail group's case (the customer agreed to a joint announcement) to knock open a second customer — the channel-management department of a consumer-goods brand, an isomorphic scenario. The second project's delivery cycle was 40% shorter than the first: the flywheel of replication turned its first revolution.

180 days, a small team, no funding news, no disruptive technology. But it validated the book's plainest conclusion: **the right problem + real data + close-up service + settled discipline = a snowball that can keep rolling.**

The five sets of cases are told. From intelligence agencies to Iowa fields, from top law firms to Chinese retail chains, the greater the span of scenes, the clearer the commonality of the methodology. Two questions remain: what boundaries a force like this should keep (the afterword); and the action checklist for those entering (the appendices).
