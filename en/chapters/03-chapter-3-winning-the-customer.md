# Chapter 3 — Winning the Customer

> "An enterprise buying AI is like your grandmother getting an iPhone — she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her."
> — a16z (Andreessen Horowitz)

## 3.1 Screening Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson of customer acquisition for internet products is screening seed users: 100 users who love you beat 10,000 who think you're okay. The FDE-world counterpart is the lighthouse customer — the kind that not only gives you revenue but sends a signal to the entire industry.

The strategic value of a lighthouse is amplified severalfold under the FDE model, for three reasons.

First, the lighthouse is the strongest sales asset. Enterprise customers have long decision chains and high risk aversion, and a peer's endorsement is the shortest path to persuasion. The origin story of the legal-AI company Harvey is a textbook case: the first major customer it announced in February 2023 was Linklaters, a top global law firm with 3,500 lawyers and 43 offices. Once this lighthouse lit up, major clients like PwC and Cleary Gottlieb followed in quick succession — the legal industry cares most about pedigree, and landing Linklaters was like getting a passport to the entire top-tier law-firm market. Palantir's early history is isomorphic: the CIA was its most demanding and most endorsement-worthy customer, and the intelligence community's trust later became the key that opened Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

The way Harvey lit up this lighthouse is itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Linklaters set up a dedicated Markets Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling, and began secretly trialing the product of this then-unknown startup. Note the way they validated it: not by watching a demo, but by firm-wide live use. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had posed about 40,000 real work questions to the system, spanning 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling's conclusion is the line the legal world has quoted over and over: "I've done legal tech for 15 years, and I've never seen anything this much of a game-changer." Another partner gave the media a concrete demonstration: he had the system prepare a ten-page brief for a US client on "how to open a bank in Luxembourg," and "it actually did it." (Source in Appendix C.)

Second, the lighthouse determines your product's genes. Under the FDE model, on-site learning flows back into the product — which means your first ten customers in effect help shape your product. Pick the wrong lighthouse and the product gets pulled in a direction with no generality. a16z's first piece of advice to startups, "sell smart," is exactly about this: a young company can't satisfy everyone, and should pick an ideal customer profile that at least shares commonalities in system environment and usage scenario, so that the learning from each delivery accumulates rather than cancels out.

Third, the quality of a lighthouse matters an order of magnitude more than quantity. Your capacity is inherently scarce — an elite team can serve, in depth and concurrently, only a single-digit number of customers, and taking on the wrong one costs not "one less deal earned" but a top team tied up in the mire for half a year. So the three high-risk signals from Chapter 2 (no business owner, refusing to provide real data, cosmic-scale requirements) must be checked here too, plus one test specific to lighthouses: agree at the start of the collaboration whether, once successful, they're willing to stand up for you — jointly publish the case, speak from experience at industry conferences, host site visits for your prospective customers. A lighthouse unwilling to speak publicly on your behalf is worth at least half as much.

### Beware the "requirement locusts"

Building internet products, you must beware "product locusts" — early users who swarm in, use you and leave, and mislead your product direction along the way. The FDE-world counterpart species is the "requirement locust": customers with ample budget and strong demand who will drain your team while producing no compounding return whatsoever. Three identifying features: their requirements clearly deviate from the company's strategic direction, so doing them settles no reusable capability; they treat the FDE as cheap outsourcing, dispatching work by headcount rather than aligning by results; and they consume enormous internal politics, so your main job becomes helping one department prove another wrong. Saying no to requirement locusts is hard — their contract values are often tempting. But remember Barry's accounting: what a wrong pilot burns is not just the immediate cost, but the team's time, morale, and the product compounding that could have grown on the right customer.

## 3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

The famous incubator YC has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." The founders of the earliest home-rental platform photographed hosts' homes door to door; the founders of the online-payments company Stripe installed the software for users on the spot.

When McGrew was asked on a podcast about the relationship between FDEs and this old maxim, he gave a precise formulation: **the FDE model is doing things that don't scale, at scale.**

That line reveals the philosophical undertone of FDE customer acquisition: in the enterprise market, acquiring trust has no scalable shortcut, only the dumb way. Three layers, each deeper than the last.

The first layer: the person has to show up. The last item on a16z's advice list for forward-deployment teams is just three words: show up in person. The reason is "a cliché, but a cliché becomes a cliché because it's true" — showing up not only improves sales, but severalfold raises the success rate of untangling the customer's internal power relations and driving adoption of a new tool. An enterprise customer's trust is priced by "number of meetings" and "things gone through together." A remote meeting builds familiarity; only fighting side by side builds trust.

The second layer: the hands have to get dirty. OpenAI's FDEs go into the fields in Iowa with agronomists; Palantir's engineers live for weeks on oil rigs and aircraft final-assembly floors; Harvey's FDEs run adoption demos partner by partner in law firms. Not one of these scenes is "efficient," but every one produces something a remote conversation never could: a firsthand feel for the customer's situation. That felt sense converts directly into solution quality — only after you've trodden the mud of the field will you understand why that seemingly perfect phone interface is unusable in bright outdoor sunlight.

The third layer: be a servant first, a mentor second. The most common mistake when an FDE first arrives is to come in with a "we're here to save you" posture. Get the posture wrong and every intelligence channel closes. The right order is to do the least glamorous service first: help the customer's analyst fix a data problem, help the IT department fill in an interface doc, help the business team automate away a weekly report. These "dumb things" buy three strategic assets: a real map of the organization's interior (who calls the shots, who is trusted, who is the hidden key node), the truth about the data environment (which data is trusted, which is for show), and — most important — the identity certification of "this outsider is one of us." Once that identity is established, what you say starts to carry weight.

The end of the dumb way isn't being dumb forever. Chapter 7 will cover how to settle this dumb legwork into a reproducible playbook — but before you can scale, you must first wade through the mud to blaze the path worth reproducing.

## 3.3 The Trust Dividend: The Endorsement Ore of Benchmark Customers

Enterprise procurement is a market with "extremely severe information asymmetry and extremely high cost of failure," and decision-makers' trust ranking of information is extremely steep: a vendor's own marketing is worse than an analyst report; an analyst report is worse than a peer's public case study; a peer's public case study is worse than a peer's private recommendation. The last tier — "someone I know used it and says it really works" — crushes every other channel in conversion efficiency. And the FDE model happens to be the best machine for producing "private-recommendation material": what you deliver isn't a software license, it's a customer executive's line at a peer dinner — "they really know the business."

Mining this ore takes three layers of action.

The first layer: make the delivery into "a story worth telling." The premise for a customer executive being willing to spread your story is that your delivery can be told as a story that makes him look good in front of peers. This requires the delivery to have three narrative elements: a concrete number ("chemical use reduced by 70%," "anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes"), a concrete person ("our agronomist worked in the fields with their team"), and a concrete contrast ("it used to take three months; this time it took five days"). At the end of the delivery, proactively help the customer's internal champion prepare this narrative — a one-pager, three charts, a version tellable in 30 seconds. How finely you prepare the material decides whether it gets garbled when others retell it.

The second layer: turn the customer's success into the customer's social currency. After the pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to four thousand stores in eight months via the bootcamp model, it became a star at industry conferences; the market-research firm J.D. Power went further — after attending Palantir's bootcamp as a customer, it began running bootcamps for its own clients. (Source in Appendix C.) This is the ideal state: the customer showcases your methodology as its own industry leadership. The endorsement is upgraded from "thank you" to "proud to use you."

The third layer: watch out for endorsement's "backlash mechanism." The enterprise market holds a grudge. A failed delivery for a high-profile customer spreads far faster than ten successes — because failure stories go down better with drinks. This is why Chapter 2's "mechanism for refusal" and Chapter 4's "activation discipline" matter so much: the fatal flaw of the lighthouse strategy isn't failing to find a lighthouse, it's letting a lighthouse go dark in your hands.

## 3.4 Using Data to Map the Customer's Estate

Around the time an FDE arrives, there's an action called "on-arrival due diligence": before writing the first line of code, use a structured method to map out all of this customer's holdings. A complete due-diligence checklist contains five maps.

- **The data map:** what data sources the customer has, who owns each, what quality they are, who manages permissions, and whether there's dark data you'd never have imagined — a spreadsheet privately hoarded by a veteran employee, a report that only circulates in email, a paper ledger. The point isn't "what exists," it's "which one is trusted." In almost every organization, the official data source and the source employees actually trust are not the same one, and it's the latter you need to connect.

- **The process map:** the real operating diagram of the target business process — not the version in the process document, but the version observed through the shadowing method, including every step, exception, and workaround that isn't written down. Specially mark three points: the step that consumes the most time, the step where an error costs the most, and the step with the most intense emotion. These three points are usually rich ore for value.

- **The org map:** who initiates, who pays, who uses, who can veto, who is the uncrowned king. The most common cause of death for enterprise projects isn't technology, it's miscounting the organizational math — your internal champion isn't senior enough, or is too senior (so senior he has no time for you), or the right people weren't brought in. Especially find the "knowledge hubs": people who aren't senior but whom everyone goes to when they hit a real problem. They are both the best source of requirements and the seed nodes for later rollout.

- **The system map:** the real face of the tech environment — the list of systems to integrate and the state of their interfaces, the security-and-compliance perimeter, the change-management process. The system map decides your deployment architecture and schedule. Many projects' timelines are doomed on day one because nobody asked clearly: on the customer's side, shipping a release takes a six-week process.

- **The political map:** the most sensitive and most crucial map. Whose cheese does this project move? Is the target process you're automating some department's source of power? Whose work will your solution make look redundant? That report listed "employees unwilling to adopt the new tool" as the number-one roadblock, and the root of the resistance is mostly not laziness but fear — fear of being replaced, of being proven incompetent, of losing one's reason to exist. The political map's function is to identify these carriers of fear in advance, and design into the solution a "new path forward" for them rather than a "dead end." Chapter 5's renewal topic returns to this: anyone you design as a "victim" will become the staunchest opponent at renewal time.

Only with all five maps in hand have you truly "arrived." This due diligence usually takes one to two weeks, conducted separately by "Echo" and "Delta," reconciling daily. It looks like pure cost, but do the math this way: what two weeks of due diligence saves is three months of sprinting across the wrong battlefield.

## 3.5 Technical Content Marketing: Building a Continuous Trust Engine

Content marketing is a classic customer-acquisition weapon for internet companies. The customer-acquisition logic of an FDE company gives its content marketing a distinctive positioning: **it doesn't chase traffic, it chases the "pre-sale of professional trust."**

The way an enterprise customer finds you isn't by scrolling past your ad; it's this: they hit a hard problem, search, ask around, discover that some company has published an extremely professional public treatment of the problem, and then conclude — "they get it, go to them." The role content plays here is the "pre-qualification" at the very front of the customer's decision chain. Palantir publishes scenario-based technical articles year-round; OpenAI and Anthropic turn enterprise-customer cases into detailed technical narratives; a16z's one industry manifesto set the tone for the whole category — none of these are brand promotion, they are carefully cultivated trust assets.

FDE-company content marketing has three iron rules distinguishing it from ordinary enterprise content.

- **Rule one: write from the "trench view," not the "trade-show view."** Ordinary enterprise content talks about how strong the product is, how grand the vision; FDE content talks about how hard the problem is and how we waded through it. Why did former Palantir engineer Barry's article on forward-deployed engineering spread like wildfire in the industry? Because it wrote about everything you can't see from the trade-show floor: the waste of reinventing wheels, the negative-margin pilots, the burned-out engineers — and so this "airing of dirty laundry" became the best evangelism for the Palantir model. Truth from the trench carries its own penetrating power, because the reader can tell who's speaking marketing lines and who's speaking the reality they live in every day.

- **Rule two: open-source the methodology to manufacture "the right to be cited."** Publicly share how you do discovery, validation, and delivery. In the short term it teaches your peers; in the long term it defines the industry standard — when the whole industry's customers start asking questions using your framework ("how do you build your evaluation system?" "what does your deployment checklist look like?"), you go from being a vendor to being the one who sets the questions. The rise of practitioner open-source communities like OpenFDE in 2026, rapidly gathering momentum, shows exactly how far from sated this industry's hunger for knowledge is; whoever satisfies it systematically first holds the power to define. The writing of this book is, in a way, a practice of the same logic.

- **Rule three: make the customer's internal champion the hero of the content.** The authorship logic of a case study is subtle: the protagonist should be the visionary manager on the customer side, and your team is "the partner who helped him succeed." Giving the champion the stage is like handing the same kind of role at the next prospective customer an invitation to "become him."

## 3.6 The Art of Clearing Procurement, Legal, and Security Review

The "gates" of the enterprise market are procurement, legal, and the security-review committee. A great many technically successful FDE projects die at these three gates, and die with no technical dignity at all: contract terms falling through, the data-processing agreement stuck, the security questionnaire still being filled out in the fourth month.

The key to clearing them is how you regard these three gates: **treat them as obstacles and you'll wreck at the last mile; treat them as part of delivery and you'll build an advantage instead** — because most tech companies are careless about these three things, and if you're serious, you win.

The procurement gate: first understand that procurement's metrics aren't your enemy. The procurement department's job is to control price, risk, and compliance. Their haggling isn't aimed at you, it's their duty. The countermeasure isn't to butt heads, it's to give them tools they can "report up": clear phased pricing (so a discount has a step to stand on), comparable market benchmarks (so approval has a basis), and — most important — measurable outcome commitments (so the charge of "overpaid" has no ground to stand on). Outcome-based pricing has an unexpected advantage here: what procurement finds hardest to approve is "spending where you can't say what you'll get," and a quote like "pay X per resolved ticket" is something procurement can understand at a glance.

The legal gate: polish the data-processing agreement like a product. The legal focus of an enterprise AI project is highly concentrated: is the data used to train the model? Where is the data stored, and who can access it? Who pays if there's a security incident? Who's at fault when the AI's output is wrong?

Smart teams productize the standard answers to these questions — pre-built agreement templates, a clear model-usage statement, a tiered incident-liability framework. Palantir survives in the intelligence community precisely because it built "permissions and audit" into the product core (who viewed what data, fully traceable); in its work with financial customers, Anthropic makes "traceable and auditable" a selling point of agent design. **Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.**

The security-review gate: use a "pre-answered white paper" to grab back two months. The standard move of enterprise security review is a questionnaire of hundreds of questions. Wait for the questionnaire to arrive before answering, and each item requires cross-department confirmation — a wait measured in months. The efficient team's approach is to proactively maintain a "security white paper": deployment architecture diagram, data-flow diagram, encryption and permissions scheme, compliance certifications, and standard answers to every question ever asked in past reviews. Eighty percent of most questionnaires can be copy-pasted straight from the white paper. Your response speed itself is the most intuitive signal of security maturity — security teams have seen it all, and they know what a flustered answer means.

## 3.7 Ecosystem Bundling: Standing on the Channel's Shoulders

The ecosystem strategy of the FDE era is to borrow the shoulders of three kinds of partners.

The first kind: cloud vendors and large platforms. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud are themselves among the main gateways to enterprise AI procurement. Entering their co-selling systems is like getting a passport straight to the enterprise procurement list. Cloud vendors' marketplaces also solve a practical pain point: the customer can use existing cloud-budget commitments to purchase your service, bypassing the procurement process for a new vendor.

The second kind: consultancies and system integrators. This is the most dramatic ecosystem shift of 2026. On the founding-partner roster of OpenAI's deployment company sit, side by side, Bain, Capgemini, and McKinsey — the largest global consulting and integration giants, turning from "potential competitors" into "equity-holding allies." The logic is clear: model companies have the technology, consultancies have the customer relationships and industry depth, integrators have the on-the-ground labor — only the three streams combined can swallow the giant market of "AI transformation" whole. For startups, the lesson runs both ways: beware the consulting giants eating your delivery layer in the name of ecosystem, but also see the real dividend of allying with regional and industry-specific integrators — in their hands are customer relationships you couldn't build in three years.

The same script played out in China at almost the same time. In July 2026, Volcengine, under ByteDance, signed a strategic partnership with EY, one of the Big Four accounting firms: co-building solutions around core scenarios like data governance, financial management, and marketing growth. Most notable, the two planned to build a thousand-strong FDE team, "creating an AI-native delivery team." Volcengine president Tan Dai put it plainly: the FDE team is to "put engineers with both technical and industry backgrounds forward to the customer's site, deeply involved in the whole process of landing the solution." On one side, a consulting giant sitting on customer relationships; on the other, a model platform lacking industry depth — the FDE became the weld point between the two. (Source in Appendix C.)

The third kind: the customer's customers. The most advanced bundling is embedding into the customer's own value chain. After J.D. Power attended Palantir's bootcamp, it began running bootcamps for its own clients — Palantir's capability spilled out along J.D. Power's customer relationships, and the acquisition cost approached zero. When you design your delivery, you can pre-embed this "re-deliverability": can this solution be taken by the customer to serve its customers? If yes, you go from being a vendor to being part of the customer's business model — the deepest moat of all.

## 3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Itself Strategy

Scarcity can manufacture appeal — invite-only access, waitlists, are classic internet-product plays. The FDE's scheduling problem is opposite in shape and identical in principle: **your capacity is always smaller than demand, so "who goes first" itself becomes a strategic tool.**

First the cruel constraint. A qualified forward-deployment squad (one business-side plus two or three technical-side) can usually deliver, at high quality and concurrently, no more than two projects. OpenAI started with 2 engineers in its early years; Sierra's deployment cycles are measured in months; Harvey's single-firm deployment takes six to nine months — elite delivery capacity is an inherently scarce resource. Scarcity isn't the scary part; the scary part is failing to price the scarcity.

The first principle of scheduling is to schedule by strategic value, not by contract size. The decision matrix has only two dimensions: this customer's lighthouse value (industry-signal strength) and this customer's learning value (how much reusable capability can be settled). A customer high on both dimensions can, even should, be prioritized at a loss of resources — Barry recalled Palantir doing pilots for free in its early days and burning millions, betting exactly on this matrix. The big contract that's low on both dimensions is the most dangerous: the money looks large, but it locks an elite team in for half a year.

The second principle is to productize the "waiting." If a customer has to wait three months to arrive, don't let those three months drain away: give them a data-preparation checklist (speed things up on arrival), advice on organizational warm-up (which key people to win over first), and lightweight remote diagnosis (keep the relationship warm, defuse landmines early). A well-managed waiting period can shorten the post-arrival delivery cycle by a third — turning the queue from a burden on customer experience into a component of delivery quality.

The third principle is to never promise parallelism beyond capacity. Your delivery quality relies on the continuous focus of one team — each project carries an entire set of customer context, and every switch means reloading it, so the loss of multi-project parallelism is far greater than imagined. Better to have sales sell "we're booked through next quarter" as scarcity (which can often raise the price too) than to have the team run ragged among three customers. The better business gets, the harder it is to resist taking on more; and the cost of blowing up from over-taking is usually far larger than one deal fewer.

## 3.9 Making the Brand Omnipresent with Open-Source Tools and Experience Kits

The FDE world has a distinctive acquisition play: turn the things you do well into open-source tools and demos, and get the target customer's engineers using them first.

The logic: the initiator of enterprise procurement is an executive, but the technical veto is in the engineers' hands. Getting the target customer's engineers using your stuff in their daily work is like pre-embedding a trust vote at the technical end of the decision chain. Three typical vehicles.

Open-source tools and components. Open-source the general modules settled during delivery — evaluation frameworks, connectors, deployment templates. Anthropic turned the Model Context Protocol (an open standard for connecting models to external tools) into a public spec, and the artifacts FDEs deliver at customer sites are built on this protocol — the first lesson the customer's engineers learn is Anthropic's tech stack. **The spread of the standard is the accumulation of trust.**

A self-serve "technical shop window." Enterprise products usually have no self-serve trial, but they can have a technical shop window: a public demo environment, a sandbox (an isolated practice environment), interactive tutorials. Palantir's bootcamp essentially turns "trial" into a ritual; for a resource-limited team, a quick-start kit that lets the customer's engineers connect sample data and get it running in one afternoon is the best sales engineer.

Presence in the engineer community. In the target industry's technical community, your engineers appear continuously — answering questions, sharing the pitfalls, submitting code. The conversion path of this presence is long, but what it reaches is exactly the people holding the technical veto. For an FDE company, hiring and acquisition are one and the same in this move: the best candidate pool and the best customer leads often come from the same community.

## 3.10 Writing Proposals and Validation Plans

The highest-priced text in the enterprise market is the proposal and the proof-of-concept plan — "a word is worth a thousand pieces of gold," and that's no exaggeration.

The bad news: most technical teams' proposals make the same mistake — they talk throughout about "what we're going to do," not "what you'll get." A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted-pyramid structure.

The first layer: the business outcome, in one paragraph. The proposal's first paragraph must be the business outcome in the customer's language: "In eight weeks, cut your bank's average anti-money-laundering investigation time from 4 hours to 15 minutes, freeing investigation capacity equivalent to 2.5× your current team." Without this sentence, the customer's executives simply won't read the rest.

The second layer: the value-validation path — how you'll prove it was done. Spell out the acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, and the exit mechanism at each stage. The signal this layer conveys is "we dare to be tested" — and in an enterprise market wounded by over-promising, daring to be tested is the scarcest sincerity.

The third layer: the delivery method — how we do it. Only here does the technical plan get its turn, and it must be written so a non-technical reader can follow: architecture diagrams with business annotations, milestones with decision points. Especially spell out what requires the customer's cooperation — data access, the time investment of key personnel. Writing clearly what the customer has to do defuses landmines early and also screens the customer.

The fourth layer: risks and countermeasures — how we've thought about the ways this could die. Most proposals dodge risk, as if naming it were bad luck. On the contrary, what mature buyers trust most is a vendor who "spells out the ways it could die": what if data quality falls short, what if key personnel change, what if accuracy doesn't reach the threshold. This layer is the proposal's trust amplifier, and the textual embodiment of Chapter 2's "refuse expensive failure" philosophy.

The proof-of-concept plan has one more special quality: it is the vehicle for the "graduation standard." The plan must spell out the maximum duration, the acceptance metrics, and the two follow-ons of graduating and parting ways. A vaguely written validation plan is like issuing a construction permit for the proof-of-concept graveyard.

## 3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundary of Hybrid Delivery

FDE delivery has undergone an important evolution in recent years: from pure on-site to a hybrid model of "mostly remote + on-site at key nodes." Palantir's official line also confirms that many projects are now executed largely remotely, with on-site concentrated at key milestones.

Designing the rhythm of the hybrid model is a craft. What must be physically present? Experience groups it into three kinds: the early stage of relationship-building (first meetings, the shadowing method, building trust with executives — trust is something you can't build through a screen, you must meet); high-intensity co-creation (bootcamp-style joint building, whiteboard sieges over key architecture decisions); and politically sensitive periods (rolling out the solution, coordinating departments, change management — moments when you need to read the air in the room). What's actually better done remotely? Deep coding, documentation, routine iteration — work that needs undisturbed flow.

The hybrid model has two hidden dividends. One is the cost structure: on-site travel is one of the largest variable items in FDE cost, and a sensible hybrid ratio can open up more than ten points of delivery margin. The other is talent sustainability: a team traveling half the time year-round has a markedly higher burnout rate — complaints about travel top the practitioner forums. The hybrid model isn't laziness, it's a guarantee of the organization's endurance.

But draw the bottom line clearly: **a "never-meet" FDE is a false proposition.** If a project is remote from launch to delivery, what you lose isn't just the relationship, it's the very thing this chapter and Chapter 2 argue for repeatedly — firsthand cognition of the site. Remote maintains existing trust and context, and those must first be created in the physical world.

## 3.12 Overseas Expansion

The going-global proposition of the FDE model presents two interesting camps in 2026.

American companies' globalization is modeled by the giants: OpenAI's FDE hiring map covers San Francisco, New York, and Seattle, as well as London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi — the geographic distribution of the FDE team precisely traces the global map of enterprise-AI ability to pay: North America, Western Europe, developed East Asian economies, Middle Eastern sovereign wealth.

FDE going global in the Chinese context follows a different logic. The past predicament was "product genes": domestic vendors have long done highly customized, project-based business, and their organizational capability doesn't match the standardization and productization overseas markets demand. But the FDE model offers a reverse view: **Chinese engineers' delivery culture — able to endure hardship, fast to respond, full-stack, used to solving every problem at the customer's site — happens to fit the FDE's requirements most closely.** So the question was never whether Chinese engineers are suited to be FDEs, but whether the platform base behind them is thick enough. Among the big players, ByteDance's Volcengine set up an FDE team for its Doubao large model; among startups, some local teams have written "FDE ≠ staff-augmentation outsourcing" onto their websites — both paths are being explored.

On the concrete strategy of going global, the FDE has one innate advantage and one innate constraint. The advantage: the "lighthouse-to-endorsement" logic works just as well in global markets, and developed-country enterprise customers have more mature willingness to pay and respect for contracts. The constraint: the FDE is a heavily localized business — language, time zone, compliance, local trust networks, each demanding a local team rather than remote support. This means the cost structure of FDE going global is inherently higher than that of online software going global, and the pace must be more restrained: first establish a foothold with a product that can be delivered remotely, then build local delivery teams in priority markets, one country at a time.

The twelve things this chapter covered come down to one sentence: **the essence of winning the customer is becoming, in a market let down countless times, the one exception worth trusting.** Signing the contract is only the entry ticket — next, the real hard battle begins: making the system come alive, making people use it.
