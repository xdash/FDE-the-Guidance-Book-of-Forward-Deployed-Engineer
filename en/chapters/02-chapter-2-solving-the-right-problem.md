# Chapter 2 — Solving the Right Problem

> "On the wrong problem, all execution is waste."

## 2.1 An Autopsy of the Proof-of-Concept Graveyard

Silicon Valley has a term for it: "POC purgatory." Projects that go in don't come out — not dead enough to be declared a failure, not alive enough to justify more investment. So, quarter after quarter, they remain "actively progressing" in the status reports, like a ward full of patients on tubes.

MIT's researchers performed a systematic autopsy on this graveyard. They distilled the five biggest roadblocks on the path to scaling an enterprise AI project, ranked by frequency:

1. Employees unwilling to use the new tool — ironically, the same people who privately use ChatGPT all day long
2. Concerns about the quality of model output
3. Poor user experience
4. Lack of executive support
5. Difficulty with change management

Note what's absent from this list: the model not being smart enough, compute not being cheap enough, technology not being advanced enough — none of it. Nearly everything that kills these projects happens at the level of "problem definition" and "organizational reality," not the technical level.

The report has one much-quoted detail worth telling on its own. An enterprise spent $50,000 to procure a professional contract-analysis tool with a lengthy feature list. But one of the company's senior lawyers simply wouldn't use it — she kept drafting contracts with the free ChatGPT. Her reason was plain: the tool they bought produced summaries too rigid to customize to her habits. The procurement department's report said "deployed," while the real day-to-day was the official system spinning idle and the employee routing around it. (Source in Appendix C.) This detail reveals the true cause of the number-one roadblock: it's not that employees are set in their ways; it's that consumer-grade products have spoiled their appetite — someone who uses the good stuff at home all day can't stomach the "artificial stupidity" in the office.

There's another, more biting comparison: **projects done with an external professional vendor succeed at roughly twice the rate of internally built ones.** Why do external teams have the higher win rate? Not because they're smarter — because they can't afford to lose. People who get paid by results pay for defining the wrong problem out of their own pocket. One Japanese company's internal project is a textbook counter-example: they pulled three or four of their strongest engineers into a strike team, the demo dazzled, leadership nodded — the only thing was that from day one, no one could answer "by what standard is this system's goodness measured." Six months later the project quietly vanished from the status decks; no one declared it a failure, and it simply died a natural death. (Source in Appendix C.)

This is the first principle of this chapter: **on the wrong problem, all execution is waste; and the density of wrong problems inside an enterprise far exceeds imagination.** This chapter is about one thing only: before you write the first line of code, how to make sure you're solving the right problem.

## 2.2 PSF: Finding Problem-Solution Fit

Internet startup methodology has a core concept called PMF (Product-Market Fit): get the product right, and the market pulls growth along by itself. In the FDE's world, the corresponding unit isn't "product and market" but "problem and solution" — I call it PSF (Problem-Solution Fit).

The difference is subtle and crucial. PMF asks "does my product have a market that wants it," from the supply side; PSF asks "is this customer's specific problem worth solving, and can it be solved by our capabilities," from the demand side. Inside a single enterprise customer, there may be hundreds of "what could AI do here" opportunities, but the ones truly worth doing must clear three gates at once.

The first gate, the pain test: is this problem a specific person's specific pain? Note the two "specifics." "Improve customer-service efficiency" is not a pain point, it's a direction; "the customer-service manager spends three hours every Monday morning manually consolidating last week's escalated tickets from four systems, when her real job should be analyzing why they escalated" — *that* is a pain point. McGrew offered a sharper criterion: go solve one of the CEO's top five concerns. The reason is practical — only a problem of that magnitude can help you bulldoze through the enterprise's internal bureaucratic resistance (Chapter 3 will unpack how to use it to get past the IT department). The criterion of Sierra's agent-engineering lead, Meurer, is another way of saying the same principle: only take two kinds of problems — the genuinely hard, and the ones with genuine business impact. Hard but without impact is showing off; impactful but not hard won't fall to you.

The second gate, the economics test: how much is solving this problem worth? Many pain points are genuinely painful but not worth money; a project you can't do this math for won't outlive the next budget cycle even if you build it. You can estimate roughly: how many person-hours a week does this problem eat? What does that translate to in labor cost? What does one mistake cost? What could the freed-up people do instead? Palantir's bootcamp simply front-loads this gate — before starting, the customer must lock onto a "core battleground" and give a quantified target: "cut scheduling conflicts by 30%," "shorten inventory turnover by 15%." That report has a widely quoted finding that shows exactly how easily this gate gets skipped: over half of enterprise AI budgets go to front-office sales and marketing, while the returns concentrate in back-office contract review, procurement, and risk control — the "unsexy" places. Everyone's solving "problems that demo well," not "problems worth money."

The third gate, the feasibility test: with our current capabilities and this customer's data reality, how far can we get? This gate is the easiest to drown in enthusiasm. There are two questions you must answer on site: where is the data, and what state is it in? The answer is often worse than imagined — scattered across seven systems, three versions that don't reconcile, and the most authoritative copy sitting in some veteran employee's personal spreadsheet. And, what accuracy threshold does this problem require? "99% usable" and "90% usable" are separated by an order of magnitude of engineering effort, and in many business scenarios 90% plus human review is actually the optimal solution — judging this takes not technology but an understanding of the business consequences.

Only when all three gates are cleared have you touched problem-solution fit. And these three gates must be cleared on site — sitting in an HQ conference room making judgments off secondhand information is the root source of every error in this chapter.

## 2.3 Refusing the Expensive "Proof-of-Concept Graveyard"

In 2025, a Chinese service provider doing enterprise AI implementation wrote a rather cutting passage on its website to turn away prospective customers: if your scenario isn't validated yet, go attend a demo event first; if your data exports from a single spreadsheet, a lightweight service is enough; if you just want to learn about AI, use the free options. The last line is the sharpest: "The FDE is a heavy investment. We'd rather you started a little later than start at the wrong time." (Source in Appendix C.)

That passage deserves to be pinned to the wall of everyone doing enterprise AI. It voices a truth counter to sales intuition: **refusing the wrong project is the FDE model's most important profit capability.**

Why is the wrong project dangerous? Because the FDE's cost structure is front-loaded — the best engineers, the priciest travel, the longest on-site investment, all happen before any money comes back. Once you're stuck in the mire, it's not a matter of losing one deal; it's a matter of an entire elite team getting bogged down and the opportunity cost avalanching.

Former Palantir engineer Barry recalled that the company "burned millions of dollars on customer pilots, and the margins on many projects were literally negative infinity, because we did them for free." The perspective he added right after is the point: Palantir could afford to burn it because it invested pilots as an R&D portfolio — like venture capital, most bets going to zero is fine, and the ones that hit have to win everything back. But if you have neither its capital depth nor a mechanism to "convert failed pilots into product assets," then every wrong pilot is pure blood loss. (Source in Appendix C.)

So an FDE team needs a "mechanism for refusal," not just the courage to refuse. Three actionable lines of defense.

- **Line one: the proof of concept must have a "graduation standard."** At the launch of every validation project, write it down in black and white: after how many weeks, by what metric, reaching what number, the project "graduates" into a paid deployment; if it doesn't, both sides part ways gracefully. Palantir's bootcamp takes this logic to the extreme — it's not a proof of concept, it's the industrialized replacement for one: one to five days, the customer brings real data, a deployable prototype is built on site, an executive decides on the spot. Either you see the real thing within days, or don't start. Traditional POCs become graveyards precisely because they are "open-ended, metric-less, and judge-less."

- **Line two:** beware of three high-risk signals. Synthesizing practitioners' experience, if two or more of these three appear, be highly alert. First, "ownership with no land" — the project has no clear business owner inside the company, only the IT department liaising; IT cares about compliance and stability, and compliance and stability are never a reason to do a new project. Second, "look but don't touch" — the customer asks you to prove your capability first but refuses to provide real data; a validation without real data is destined to produce self-deceiving false positives. Third, "cosmic-scale requirements" — a customer who in the first meeting wants to "cover every scenario in the whole company" is usually not yet ready to do any single one.

- **Line three:** leave a graceful step down for "no." Refusing isn't cutting ties. The best approach is to translate "not now" into "when": "This scenario's data foundation is still missing three things. We suggest doing another scenario first, and shoring up these three along the way, then coming back next quarter." Packaging a refusal as a roadmap protects your capacity and preserves the relationship — Chapter 3's "lighthouse-customer screening" continues along this line.

## 2.4 Pain, the Prime Mover Behind a Deployment

Where do the right problems come from? Textbooks will tell you: from requirements research. FDE field experience will tell you: **from pain. And pain doesn't show up in the conference room; it only shows up at the scene of the work.**

Palantir wrote this method in blood twenty years ago. Look back at that Iraq-battlefield story in Section 1.2: soldiers needed a roadside-bomb warning tool — a need you could never elicit in any interview, because the soldiers didn't know "you could ask software for this"; they assumed it was just part of patrol life. Pain points are "seen" by the engineer, not "spoken" by the user. It was only because the embedded engineer went out on missions with the patrol and saw with his own eyes the hesitation and fear before a suspicious stretch of road that the crude map tool that changed the battlefield came to be.

This method has a name in anthropology: "participant observation"; in the Toyota Production System it's called *genchi genbutsu* — go to the actual place, look at the actual thing, get the actual facts. The FDE turned it into an operable field method I call "the shadowing method."

Follow a real user through a real day of theirs. Not interviewing him — sitting beside him and watching him work: watching which systems he opens, between which spreadsheets he copies and pastes, at which steps he frowns, which "official processes" he routes around. OpenAI's FDE team did exactly this on the John Deere project: they flew to Iowa and went into the fields with agronomy experts and farm owners, watched how they made spraying decisions, watched which information actually entered the decision, watched how the seasonal deadline governed everything. That much-quoted practitioner maxim describes exactly what this method sets out to discover: "The hard part is finding the workflow nobody wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Each of these three can only be found on site.

Focus on the "workarounds," not the "process." The official process chart tells you how the organization "is supposed to run"; the workarounds tell you how the organization "actually runs" — and every workaround is an unmet pain point screaming. Why does the employee insist on exporting the data to a spreadsheet and recomputing it? Why is it common knowledge in the department that "you go to Little Wang for this sheet"? Why, when there's a data system right there, does someone always manually reconcile the numbers before the decision meeting? Workarounds are the organization's scars, and beneath every scar is a failure of the system — and an opportunity for the FDE.

Beware the "translated pain point." If the need you hear has been relayed through the customer's IT department, procurement, or a consultant, it gets distorted with every hand it passes through — IT translates a business pain into a technical requirement ("we need a data platform"), procurement translates it into a compliance line item ("we need to meet such-and-such standard"). The disasters of traditional software projects often start right here: the vendor is accountable to the translation, not to the pain itself. So your first duty is to bypass the translation and reach straight to the pain's nerve endings. This is also the core responsibility of "Echo" in Palantir's two-person model — understanding the customer's "mission," not "requirements": a requirement is a secondhand narration of the pain; the mission is the pain's firsthand source.

Once you've found the real pain, the next step is to validate at minimal cost: can our solution actually stop this pain?

## 2.5 Validating Value with the "Minimum Viable Deployment"

Internet startup methodology has a famous concept, MVP (Minimum Viable Product): validate market demand with the smallest possible product. The FDE's corresponding concept I call **MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment)** — with the smallest engineering investment, in the customer's real environment, against a real pain point, validate one real occurrence of value.

One word apart, but the difference is in the bones. MVP validates "should we build this product," judged by the market; MVD validates "can this solution produce value on this customer," judged by this specific customer's specific business. A solution that validated successfully at ten customers can still fail at the eleventh — different data foundations, different organizational inertia, a differently shaped pain point. This is the cruelty of enterprise delivery: **value cannot be inherited, only validated one at a time.**

MVD has three iron rules.

- **Rule one:** real data, no exceptions. Validating with the customer's "de-identified sample data" or your own fabricated demo data is the first brick of the proof-of-concept graveyard. Real data hides every devil: field meanings that don't match the docs, thirty percent nulls, encoding rules from three years ago, and the deadliest — the data itself recording a broken process. **How to make this rule concrete:** Palantir's bootcamp writes "the customer must bring their own real business data" into the rules; the John Deere project put "reviewing hundreds of real field cases" before any modeling. A solution that holds up on fake data is just an elaborately crafted self-consolation.

- **Rule two:** shrink the incision, not the ambition. The common mistake is to understand MVD as "a castrated version of the big solution" — cut seventy percent of the features and end up with a mess. The right approach is to shrink along a different dimension: don't cut value, cut scope. Don't chase "an intelligent customer-service system covering the whole company," but "cover only return-and-exchange tickets, but do it end-to-end, with no human intervention"; don't chase "supply-chain optimization for the whole group," but "just the scheduling conflicts on this one production line, but save 20 person-hours a week for real." Shrink the incision until the value density is high enough — high enough that the business unit sees it with the naked eye and spreads it on its own. The expansion path of the legal-AI company Harvey is the textbook of this play: don't roll it out firm-wide, drill through one global practice group first, make the first partners into believers, and only after six months of live use expand laterally.

- **Rule three:** set a hard deadline to force trade-offs. An MVD's validation cycle should be counted in "weeks," not "months." Palantir's bootcamp is one to five days; Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live case is four weeks; Decagon's typical deployment is four to eight weeks. The point of the deadline isn't speed, it's forcing both sides to make honest trade-offs: anything that can't demonstrate value within these few weeks is not yet core value. A six-month "minimum validation" will almost inevitably grow back into a want-it-all mega-project — and that's the proof-of-concept graveyard breaking ground once more.

### The bootcamp: industrializing the MVD

The AIP Bootcamp Palantir launched in 2023 deserves to be unpacked on its own as the benchmark for "industrializing the MVD" — it's currently the only MVD assembly line to have been validated at scale.

First the data: starting from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, sessions multiplied year over year; media tracking shows the cumulative count long ago passed a thousand, with an average of nearly 6 a day at the 2025 peak; the traditional enterprise-software sales cycle (nine to twelve months) was compressed to weeks; US commercial revenue grew 137% year-over-year in Q4 2025, and the company publicly attributes almost all of the growth to this. (Source in Appendix C.)

Now the process, which breaks the MVD into five standardized moves. Day 0, preparation: both sides lock onto an extremely focused core battleground — "optimize scheduling on a certain production line," "reduce inventory turnover days" — refusing all grand narratives. Day 1, connection: wire into the customer's existing systems, pull the isolated data out, and build a preliminary Ontology model. Days 2 to 3, building: FDE and customer technical staff write code and configure rules back to back, wire the large model into the business flow, and produce an automated workflow that can execute real actions. Days 4 to 5, demo and decision: what's produced isn't a report, it's a live software interface, which the business executive clicks with their own hands, watching AI give recommendations based on their own company's data — and after the shock, going straight into commercial negotiation.

The bootcamp's elegance is that it solves the MVD's three classic problems at once: the real-data problem (the customer brings it themselves), the deadline problem (five days, capped), and the judge problem (the executive uses it with their own hands). And it incidentally solves a deeper problem — trust. Letting the decision-maker operate a system based on their own data beats a hundred-page feasibility report.

How fast do contracts get signed after the bootcamp? On earnings calls, Palantir disclosed a set of real cadences fast enough that even peers might not believe them: a large healthcare company attended a bootcamp in December and five weeks later signed a five-year agreement worth $26 million a year; a global bank piloted for one month, first signed a $2 million initial contract, and four months later expanded it into a three-year deal worth $19 million a year; the pharmacy chain Walgreens first piloted in 10 stores, improved in-store operational efficiency by 30%, then rolled out to 4,000 stores within eight months, with AI-driven end-to-end workflows automatically handling about 384 billion decisions a day that people used to make. (Source in Appendix C.) This set of numbers answers "what happens after the MVD": a validated project doesn't grow slowly, it scales in leaps — the customer has already seen value with their own eyes in five days, and the rest is just commercial process.

Of course, replicating this model has a barrier: there must be a mature platform base behind it, or you can't even stand up the environment in five days. For a team without a platform, the executable simplified version is the "two-week sprint validation": week one, arrive on site, connect the data, set the metric; week two, build a prototype that solves only a single point but runs on real business, demo to the business side at the weekend and decide go/no-go on the spot. The form can be trimmed; the iron rules cannot.

## 2.6 Should You Accommodate the Customer's Existing Environment Early On

The MVD stage has a disagreement almost every project runs into: the customer's existing tech environment — that pile of twenty-year-old legacy systems, the little tools departments built themselves, and the strict security-and-compliance perimeter — how far should our solution accommodate it?

Both camps have a point. The "accommodate" camp says: validating within the customer's real constraints is the only real validation. The "rebuild" camp says: doing deep adaptation for a legacy environment that's about to be replaced is wasting the precious validation window on engineering destined to be thrown away.

FDE practice offers a middle road: be compatible with the old systems at the data level, and never accommodate the old systems at the architecture level — I call it "read old, write new."

At the data level, be deeply compatible with the old environment. Read from wherever the customer's data is — even if it's in an old mainframe, in a spreadsheet on a shared drive, in some ancient system's proprietary interface. Finance's legacy mainframes and healthcare's heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics have always been the core battleground of deployment work. There's no shortcut to data-read compatibility, because data is the prerequisite for validating value, and data will never move house to accommodate your architecture. The good news is that this layer of work is being changed by AI itself: field mapping, cross-system transfer, and pulling data from interface-less legacy systems that used to require human interpretation can now be handed largely to agents — for example, using a browser agent to mimic human operation and pull data from a legacy system with no API. Integration costs are falling by orders of magnitude. (Source in Appendix C.)

At the architecture level, resolutely refuse to be a parasite of the old environment. The validation-stage system should run within a boundary you control, interacting with the old systems through interfaces, not writing your code into the old systems. Three reasons: a validation-stage solution itself has better than even odds of being overturned and rewritten, and the deeper the parasitism the greater the waste; writing into the old systems means going through the customer's change-management process, measured in months, which fundamentally clashes with the MVD's weekly cadence; and keeping a "can-withdraw" posture is itself a bargaining chip and an honest stance — the FDE finishes and leaves, but a parasite can never leave.

At the process level, yield to people's habits, not the systems' habits. This is the one technical teams most easily get backwards. You can be tough on the tech environment; you must yield to people's habits. If the business user's core actions happen in spreadsheets and email, the MVD's interface should appear in a spreadsheet plugin and in email, not demand that the user log in to a brand-new portal. OpenAI's deployment at BBVA cut in through the ChatGPT interface that 120,000 employees were already using, rather than building something new from scratch — a model of yielding to habit. Remember that cause of death from Section 2.1: employees unwilling to adopt the new tool ranks first among the five roadblocks. **A new system's biggest enemy is not the old system; it's the old habit.**

## 2.7 "Deeds Over Words" User Research

To close the chapter, pull the lens back to the source of the methodology and discuss the divide between FDE-style user research and traditional research.

The creed of traditional research is "ask": questionnaires, interviews, focus groups. The FDE's creed is "watch" and "do" — deeds over words. There are three reasons, each deeper than the last.

The first layer: the customer doesn't know what they want. This is not a put-down, it's a law of cognition. Facing a brand-new category — intelligence-analysis software in 2004, AI agents in 2025 — the user has no frame of reference to describe the need. Palantir's demo loop works precisely because it doesn't ask "what do you want" but says "here's what I built, tell me what's wrong with it." People's judgment about "what's wrong" is far stronger than their imagination about "what they want." Finding fault with a solution is a human gift; describing an ideal solution out of thin air is a human weakness.

The second layer: what the customer says and what they do are two different things. That group of lawyers in Section 2.1 is the best proof: survey them on "are you willing to use professional legal AI," and procurement will tell you the willingness is strong — after all, they just spent $50,000 on a tool; but observe the lawyers' actual behavior, and they're drafting contracts with ChatGPT. In an enterprise context, "saying" is polluted by too many factors: political correctness, courtesy toward the vendor, defense of one's own role. Only behavior doesn't lie. The shadowing method observes behavior; the MVD measures behavior; both are built on "doing is truer than saying."

The third layer: the highest-quality research happens in shared labor. In an interview, the customer is a "research subject," wary and performing; working side by side, the customer is a colleague, relaxed and real. The two or three days in the bootcamp of FDEs and customer technical staff writing code back to back exchange a density of information beyond any formal research — in the gaps between debugging, the customer will offhandedly say "honestly, we never trust this field," "this process nominally goes through the system, but really it's still a phone call." These lines never appear in a formal interview, because they look "informal." But they are precisely the intelligence that decides whether a deployment succeeds.

There's one new variable from 2026 worth adding here: the interview itself is being reshaped by AI. Ding Xindong, CTO of Tezign, shares this approach: instead of sending out a structured questionnaire, use an agent to autonomously interview the entire company — communicating differently according to different scenarios and different people's real pain points — and finally aggregate a global diagnostic map of "what stage each product line is at, what its pain points are, and what approach suits it." A questionnaire elicits answers; an interview draws out pain points — and AI has driven down the cost of the latter. (Source in Appendix C.)

The three layers together distill the FDE's research methodology into one sentence: **find the pain point with the shadowing method, validate the solution with the minimum viable deployment, and win the truth through shared labor.**

By here, the right problem is locked and value is preliminarily validated. The next battlefield is turning that validated single point of value into a real contract, and into a relationship that has truly begun — how to win the customer.
