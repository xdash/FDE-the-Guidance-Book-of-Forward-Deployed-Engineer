# Chapter 1 — The Rise of the FDE

> "The biggest challenge in building software for spies is: I don't know any spies."
> — Bob McGrew, early Palantir executive and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI

## 1.1 First, a Dead Nine-Million-Dollar Project

The beginning is always the same. In the conference room of a large enterprise, the vendor's demo has just wrapped up. The large model answers every question fluently, the data dashboards shimmer, and even the most demanding executive can't find fault. The CEO decides on the spot: sign it. The contract is worth several million dollars. Handshakes, a group photo, a press release.

Nine months later, the project was dead.

Not a dramatic death — a silent one. The system is still running, the servers are still on; it's just that not a single business unit is actually using it. The vendor delivered every feature in the contract, and the enterprise paid every dollar in the contract. The one thing that never showed up was "value."

If you think this was just bad luck, MIT's famous 2025 report will set you straight: this is the norm. They interviewed 52 organizations, collected 153 executive questionnaires, and reviewed more than 300 publicly disclosed enterprise AI projects. The conclusion was a single sentence: of the thirty to forty billion dollars burned, **95% produced no measurable financial return whatsoever**.

95%. In other words, when it comes to landing enterprise AI, the main theme is not success — it's failure at scale.

More interesting is *how* they fail. The report went out of its way to note: the problem is not the models. Those models that dazzled everyone in the demo are still just as smart in production — they simply "don't remember feedback, don't retain context, and don't enter the workflow." They look like products but behave like exhibits. Stanford's aggregate data that same year corroborates it: 88% of organizations were using AI, yet the share of agentic applications actually running in production was in the single-digit percentages. McKinsey delivered the final blow: only 6% of enterprises could say AI contributed more than 5% to their profits.

In the days after the report came out, a manufacturing COO's complaint made the rounds in the industry: "Online, everyone says everything has changed. Back on our shop floor, nothing has moved." (Source in Appendix C.) That line stings more than any statistic — his company doesn't lack budget or tools; what it lacks is someone to wedge those tools into the real processes on the floor.

Around the same time, the report's lead author told a story about a control group. Some startups run by nineteen- and twenty-year-olds were doing twenty million dollars a year in revenue off generative AI. Their playbook was the exact opposite of the incumbents': pick a single pain point, drill straight through it, and bind themselves tightly to the customers who actually use the product. Older companies always try to swallow the whole thing in one bite; the young ones take a small bite, chew through it, then take the next. (Source in Appendix C.)

Remember this contrast. It's practically a trailer for Chapter 2 of this book.

This chasm, by the way, wasn't dug in the AI era. China's enterprise software industry has been lying in it for well over a decade: big companies want customization, vendors lose money on every deal, the code is delivered and then abandoned when the team leaves, and everyone ends up reduced to "the client's outsourcing shop." One practitioner put it bluntly: customization is the natural enemy of SaaS, and this curse can only dissolve on its own when the market matures. Things are more genteel in the US, but the script is much the same: sales signs the deal, implementation moves in, and half a year later delivers a "fully featured system that nobody loves to use," followed by a long stretch of finger-pointing.

At bottom it's the same wall: **the place where software gets built and the place where value gets created are not the same place.** On this side of the wall, requirements are relayed through tickets, meeting minutes, and weekly reports, losing a layer of blood with each retelling. On the other side, the customer's real workflow hides in spreadsheets nobody wrote down, in conventions passed by word of mouth, in the tacit knowledge of "you'll have to ask Old Wang about that." The software industry has invented countless ladders for scaling that wall — requirement documents, user research, implementation methodologies, customer-success organizations — and the wall is still there.

Until one company decided: no more scaling the wall. Send the person over instead.

## 1.2 Palantir's Victory

In 2003, Silicon Valley had just clawed its way out of the wreckage of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel and a few young Stanford graduates founded a company named after *The Lord of the Rings* — Palantir, the seeing-stone that, in the legend, lets you see far away. What they set out to do sounded like science fiction: build data-analysis software for US intelligence agencies, stitch fragments scattered across countless classified databases into a coherent picture, and help analysts catch terrorists.

This business had a premise that would break any product manager on the spot. Years later, early executive Bob McGrew brought the era vividly to life on a podcast:

> "Our goal when we started the company was to build software for the intelligence community — to put it plainly, to build software for spies. And one challenge with building software for spies is: I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either. Even if you happen to find a spy and ask him, 'So how do you actually do your job day to day,' he usually won't tell you."

No user interviews, no requirement documents, no usability testing. The first lesson of internet startup methodology is void here. The method co-founder Stephen Cohen came up with was charmingly clumsy: build a demo, show it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. They didn't pull any punches: "This thing is terrible. It has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat; he pressed on: "Then how would you want it to be different?" He pulled out a notebook, wrote it down line by line, went back and revised, and brought the revised version back to their door.

That clumsy loop was the embryo of the FDE. Inside it hid two intuitions that would later prove worth a fortune: first, that customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something usable; and second, that the fastest path to knowing what a customer wants is to stand the person who builds the thing next to the person who uses it.

The one who upgraded these intuitions into a company strategy was employee number 13, Shyam Sankar. As Palantir moved from its first customer to its second and third, the team found a counterintuitive fact: what each customer wanted differed in small but critical ways. The standard solution is to extract the commonalities, build a general-purpose product, and say no to the differences. But Palantir's customers were the CIA, the FBI, and US forces on the battlefield — saying no meant being shown the door. Sankar went the other way: build a platform that could be flexibly customized, then send engineers to embed at the customer's site and finish that last mile.

His most crucial move was to change the accounting of the thing. In the software industry's ledger, "customizing for a single customer" is called services, the enemy of margins. Sankar flipped it over: **on-site customization is not a cost; it is product discovery.** Every pit an engineer falls into at a customer's site is a signpost for the platform's next evolution.

Sankar himself was the first Forward Deployed Engineer, and his earliest embedded stint reads like the archetypal scene for the role. Around 2007, the largest source of US casualties in Iraq was roadside bombs; the cross-agency operations center set up to fight them let Sankar bring a small team and a "still very rough" product into a SCIF for two weeks of joint work. A SCIF is a physically isolated classified space where even speakerphones are banned. Sankar came up with a brutal workaround: he strapped the phone to his own head with a rubber band, freeing both hands to write code — one ear on the analysts' feedback, the other on colleagues back at Silicon Valley HQ. For two weeks he worked nineteen hours a day: demo, ingest data, gather feedback, revise on the spot. At the end, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself was wrecked, and called CEO Karp: "This isn't sustainable. We're finished." Karp's answer would later become company culture: turn that very "unsustainability" into an institution. (Source in Appendix C.)

Years later a colleague recalled that Sankar could dress you down without any emotion at all. Once, while he and his colleague Mabry were chatting happily at an airport café, a sharply worded email of criticism landed in Mabry's inbox — written and sent on the spot from across the table. "There was no personal attack in it, only an implicit message: to win, I owe you these honest words." (Source in Appendix C.)

This playbook soon drew blood on the battlefield. Embedded Palantir engineers discovered that soldiers didn't need any fancy intelligence charts at all; they just needed a small tool to mark "this road looks suspicious" on a map — roadside bombs were the patrol's biggest killer. The engineers cobbled together a crude map tool on the spot: a soldier taps once to mark a dangerous stretch of road, and the whole squad sees it in real time. That tool saved lives, and later settled into a standard platform feature. Note this: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room — only in the moment an engineer and a soldier looked at the same road together.

On the commercial side, they died once first. Palantir's first enterprise-facing product, Metropolis, got a dismal market reception — only a few financial firms barely used it. The second attempt, Foundry, is what finally opened the account, and the turning point was Airbus. At the Toulouse plant, a fuel pump on the A380 kept failing, and Airbus's own engineers had spent two years without a clue. Palantir's people came in, wired the sensor data into the platform, and cracked it in two weeks — the fuel was sloshing away from the pump body as the aircraft climbed. A trivial fix preserved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars. Airbus's head of digitalization later remarked publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months to diagnose." (Source in Appendix C.) Airbus became Palantir's most loyal European champion from then on, building its entire Skywise data platform on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and more than fifty thousand users.

There's one piece of gossip worth recording. Legend has it that Palantir's software played a part in the 2011 operation that killed Osama bin Laden. The claim has never been confirmed and never been debunked — the journalists who reported the Palantir story deliberately left this ambiguous footnote in place. But true or not, the rumor itself was Palantir's best sales weapon. (Source in Appendix C.)

By some point before 2016, the number of Forward Deployed Engineers at Palantir had at one stage exceeded that of its traditional software engineers. A software company with more than half its engineers writing no product at HQ, scattered instead across customer sites worldwide. Wall Street couldn't make sense of it for years, sneering that it was a "human-wave tactic," "more like a consultancy."

Then time gave its answer. In 2023 it launched its AI platform, AIP, paired with a playbook called the "bootcamp" (detailed in Chapter 8), and compressed the enterprise-software sales cycle from nine-to-twelve months down to a few weeks. In Q4 2025, its "Rule of 40" score — revenue growth plus profit margin, the software industry's health metric, where 40 is a passing grade — hit 127%; in Q1 2026, 145%. It signed $4.26 billion in a single quarter, net revenue retention of 139%, $7.2 billion of cash on the books. On the earnings call, Karp said only one thing: "We are a species unto ourselves." Its market cap briefly broke through $400 billion. (Source in Appendix C.)

The people who once laughed at the human-wave tactic fell silent. In twenty years, Palantir proved one thing: that wall — a ladder can't get over it, but a person can. The people who got over it acquired a formal name: Forward Deployed Engineer.

## 1.3 What Is an FDE

### A one-sentence definition

The definition this book adopts comes from the best interpreter of the model, Bob McGrew — who was an engineer at PayPal early on, then an early Palantir executive, and later Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, where ChatGPT and GPT-4 both came out of the team he led:

> **A Forward Deployed Engineer is an engineer embedded at the customer's site who fills the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."**

Every word in that plain sentence is deliberate.

"Embedded at the site" means your work context is wired into the customer's: you're in the customer's chat groups, reading the customer's data, sitting in the customer's meetings, and getting to know the person who "knows why the process is the way it is" — not necessarily sitting in the customer's office every day. The "gap" is the reason this role exists: where the product works out of the box, you're not needed; the deeper the gap, the more you're needed — intelligence, finance, manufacturing, healthcare, law. "Engineer" is the most important qualifier: you write production code, not reports. When Palantir named the role, it deliberately kept the words "software engineer" to stress to the world that this is not a consulting job. As for "Forward Deployed," it's a military term for troops stationed at the front — putting your most capable people closest to the problem.

### What it is not

The fastest way to understand a new role is by elimination.

It is not pre-sales. Pre-sales work ends before the contract is signed; its goal is to win the deal, and its output is slides. The FDE's work only enters the deep water *after* the contract is signed; its goal is to win the outcome, and its output is a system running in production. Pre-sales makes the customer believe "this can work"; the FDE makes it actually work.

It is also not staff-augmentation outsourcing — a distinction that matters especially to Chinese readers, where "engineers on site" has too long and too unflattering a history. The first wave of service providers to fly the FDE flag in China drew the line against staffing in three sentences on their websites: staffing is paid by the hour, the FDE delivers by phase and is accepted by results; staffing writes from scratch on site, the FDE builds engineering on top of a product base; the longer staffing stays the longer it stays, and when the people leave the system stops, whereas the FDE finishes and leaves, with the capability staying behind in the system and the customer's team. In a sentence: staffing sells headcount, the FDE sells outcomes.

Jove, the FDE lead at the Silicon Valley AI-customer-service company Cresta, drew the boundary in even finer detail in a video conversation. His team is scaling from 30 to 100 people this year, and his judgment is: **an FDE only makes sense when bound to an AI platform** — if all you're doing is traditional data integration and system assembly, it's hard to tell you apart from a traditional implementation engineer or outsourcer. He also has one hard bar in hiring: in the age of agents, not being able to use code is like being illiterate. Another mechanism worth noting is the dual mandate: an FDE must not only make the deployment succeed but also carries a target for "making the product more mature" — what you learn on site must feed back into the platform. (Source in Appendix C.)

It is not a consultant. A consultant delivers advice per project and takes no responsibility for execution; the FDE is responsible for the system's ultimate operation, and the finish line is "the customer's team can use it independently." The collaboration between Anthropic and the fintech company FIS is a specimen: engineers embedded at FIS to co-build an anti-money-laundering agent, compressing investigations from hours to minutes, but the stated goal of the collaboration wasn't to hand over a system — it was to "transfer knowledge so that FIS could build agents on its own afterward." A consultant wants you to need him forever; the FDE wants you to stop needing him eventually.

It is not a traditional product engineer either. The product engineer faces abstract users — personas (what the user looks like), funnels (how many go from visit to payment), daily active users; the FDE faces concrete customers — a bank's risk department, a farm in Iowa, a patrol on the outskirts of Baghdad. Palantir has a famous internal split of roles: platform engineers are responsible for "one capability, serving many customers," and Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") are responsible for "one customer, marshaling many capabilities." What the platform engineer pursues is one feature usable everywhere; what the FDE pursues is thoroughly solving the problem of the one customer in front of him first.

### How this wind started blowing

A role invented back in 2003 — why did it only become a headliner in 2025?

The most direct trigger was generative AI. Large models created an unprecedented gap: anyone can produce a stunning demo in five minutes, but wiring that demo into an enterprise's real data, permissions, compliance, and workflow is an order of magnitude harder. The model companies figured it out one after another: **the next decisive factor is not model quality, it's deployment capability.**

The numbers sketch the steepness of the curve: in the first nine months of 2025, FDE job postings grew eightfold; on the YC job board, more than a hundred startups listed a role that had barely existed three years earlier. The venture firm a16z flatly called it "the hottest job in tech," and added a vivid metaphor: an enterprise buying AI is like your grandmother getting an iPhone — she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her.

A set of *Financial Times* reports in November 2025 is the best slice for observing this wave. Fournier, OpenAI's head of forward deployment for Europe, said the team had been formed only a year earlier and was about to scale to 50 people — "demand has exceeded our expectations." De Jong, Anthropic's applied-AI lead, put it more amusingly: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are two completely different species" — which is why her team was scaling fivefold in a year. Prettejohn, Palantir's head in the UK, distilled the company creed into one line: "Software only has value when it truly means something to the end customer." Even Gomez, CEO of the model company Cohere, came out in support: "We embed engineers at the very start of the contract, and pull back once the customer is running smoothly." (Source in Appendix C.)

The intensity of the talent grab has three more hard indicators: OpenAI's forward-deployment team started with 2 people and grew to 52 in a year; Salesforce, which wrote "don't do implementation yourself" into the textbook, publicly committed to hiring 1,000 FDEs; even Deloitte, which makes its living selling consulting, set up a dedicated FDE business line in December 2025. (Source in Appendix C.)

Then came the giants voting with their feet. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the formation of a "deployment company": majority-owned by itself, jointly backed by 19 top-tier capital firms including TPG, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, with an initial investment of over $4 billion and a reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion — and, along the way, acquired a consultancy with 150 deployment engineers. Hours later, Anthropic was reported to be forming a rival vehicle with Blackstone. The two largest model companies, on the same day, elevated "deployment" from a cost center to a strategic asset — the capital markets casting their vote for the FDE in the most expensive way possible.

The deepest reason of all is the one McGrew saw through: "AI agents are a category with no incumbent, so there's a vast amount of product discovery to do." What CRM software should look like has had a standard answer for twenty years; what an agent should look like, nobody knows — not even the customer. The answer can only be found at the customer's site.

Here history draws a full circle: in 2003, Palantir invented the FDE because it "didn't know how spies work"; in 2025, the whole industry embraced the FDE because it "doesn't know how agents inside enterprises should work." Twenty-two years, the same answer.

## 1.4 The FDE's Responsibilities and Traits

### A résumé born for this role

If you had to answer "what kind of profession is the FDE," McGrew's résumé is practically the model answer.

His first job was at PayPal, as an early engineer. That cohort would later be called the "PayPal Mafia" and would profoundly shape all of Silicon Valley. After leaving, he joined a startup-stage Palantir and rose all the way to executive, living through the entire process by which the FDE went from a stopgap to a company strategy. While running the product and engineering teams, he offered a famous metaphor: Forward Deployed Engineers pave "gravel roads" to value at customer sites one by one, and the product team decides which gravel roads are worth widening and hardening into "highways" that serve the next ten customers.

Later still, he served as OpenAI's Chief Research Officer, leading the development of ChatGPT, GPT-4, and the o1 reasoning model. In other words, this person built the platform on this side of the wall, crossed over to the site on the other side, and finally, with his own hands, built the very technology that made the wall higher than ever before.

An amusing scene played out at a 2025 YC AI conference. McGrew had assumed founders would crowd around asking "how did you invent ChatGPT," but every single one chased the same question: **how exactly does Palantir's FDE model work?** A man who invented ChatGPT, most pressed on a delivery methodology. That detail is the best footnote to which way the era's wind is blowing.

### Three layers of traits

Synthesizing more than twenty job postings from various companies and the firsthand accounts of practitioners, an FDE's traits can be grouped into three layers.

The first layer: a broad-enough technical generalist. An FDE doesn't need to be the deepest expert in any one domain, but must be able to solve full-stack problems independently at a customer's site: writes code, tunes APIs, understands data pipelines, can deploy to the cloud, has a feel for the temperament of large models, and understands the "utilities" of the enterprise environment — single sign-on (log in once, access everywhere), permissions, compliance certifications. The hiring market has a clear price for this combination: the 2026 compensation report shows that a mid-level FDE at a top AI lab has a median total annual compensation of about $385,000, a senior about $610,000, and a principal over a million — higher than most pure R&D roles at the same level, because the market knows how scarce such people are. (Source in Appendix C.)

The second layer: the ability to translate technology into business outcomes. This is the watershed between the FDE and the ordinary engineer. One frontline practitioner's line is widely quoted: "The model is usually the cleanest part. The hard part is finding the workflow nobody wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Palantir's hiring bar puts it more bluntly: "A candidate's articulacy, clarity, and ease of communication should make me happy to have them run a meeting with a customer." Interviews screen for this translation ability too. OpenAI's and Palantir's FDE interviews have a signature segment called "problem deconstruction": you're handed a huge, vague, real enterprise problem, and for sixty minutes you write not a single line of code — all they watch is how you probe, how you scope, how you build order out of chaos. The interviewers' advice: understand the problem before you jump in — slow is smooth, smooth is fast. In an ordinary interview, "I optimized the query by 40%" is a full-marks answer; in an FDE interview, the full-marks answer is: "I optimized the query by 40%, which let the customer's analysts get their reports two hours earlier every day and tripled the team's processing capacity." A technical achievement only counts as answered once it's converted into the customer's language.

The third layer: ownership, plus a touch of "rebellion." A line circulates in the practitioner community, describing exactly the accountability this role demands: "The deployment went down at 2 a.m. You don't file a ticket, don't blame another team, don't go back to sleep. You fix it. Period." Palantir has an even subtler expectation of its business-side roles: you need both deep industry knowledge and the nerve to be a "rebel" — to see the absurdities of the customer's status quo, and dare to push for a tenfold change, not a ten-percent one. The behavioral benchmark CEO Karp set back then was the "French waiter": embedded in the service process, sensitive to real needs, yet with enough confidence and taste to guide the customer from "what they think they want" to "what's actually good for them."

### How a day goes

Down at the daily level, an FDE's time roughly splits like this: forty to fifty percent immersed on the customer side writing code and tuning systems, twenty to thirty percent aligning direction with the customer's management, deconstructing problems, and making architecture decisions, ten to twenty percent settling the patterns learned on site back into the company's product line, and the rest on evaluation, optimization, and knowledge sharing — writing playbooks, evangelizing internally, training the customer's team.

Hidden in this schedule is an important message: the FDE is not "an engineer sent abroad" but "an engineer with a two-way mission" — delivering outcomes to the customer on one end, feeding intelligence to the company on the other. That is also the theme of the next section.

## 1.5 Let Everything Be Judged by Results

If you had to sum up the FDE's working creed in one sentence, it would be: let everything be judged by results.

First, separate "data" from "results." The history of enterprise software has never lacked projects with pretty data and terrible results: the feature checklist is a hundred percent ticked, but only five percent of people use it; the system has four nines of availability, yet the business unit would rather keep using spreadsheets. The vast majority of the 95% of failed projects in that MIT report did not lack data dashboards — the numbers were all there, the value never came, because no one was accountable for "that line of numbers on the financial statement."

The FDE model institutionally guarantees that "results" don't get diluted, and it does so through three specific things.

- **Payment moves toward results:** In its early days doing government projects, Palantir often adopted a "pay only if it works" arrangement. McGrew recalls it plainly: "Early on, it made sense for the startup to bear all the risk — you pay us once we've made it work." This logic evolved into something more refined in the AI era: Sierra charges by "resolved conversation" — no resolution, no charge; plenty of FDE service providers deliver by phase and are accepted by results. Once fees are bound to results, the delivery team's every behavior reorders itself — you'll no longer spend three weeks polishing a feature nobody uses, because "nobody uses it" is now on your own tab.

- **The measure of success is front-loaded to before work begins:** The first step of an FDE project isn't writing code, it's defining, together with the customer, "what counts as done." Palantir's bootcamp requires the customer to first lock onto an extremely focused core battleground — "cut scheduling conflicts on a certain production line by 30%," not "explore AI-enabled manufacturing" — precisely to keep the project from drifting, under the banner of "exploration," toward the unfalsifiable. OpenAI's collaboration with John Deere is a textbook demonstration: first, together with agronomy experts, they reviewed hundreds of real field cases and built a bespoke evaluation system, and only then began iterating the model. The final number — "chemical use reduced by up to 70%" — was not marketing spin dressed up after the fact; it was the target set before work began.

- **The final judge is behavioral change in the customer's organization:** That report had a biting finding: only about forty percent of enterprises provide employees an official AI-tool subscription, while as many as ninety percent of employees solve work problems day-to-day with personal consumer-grade products. This means a large number of "successfully launched" projects are actually in a state of "the official system spinning idle while employees route around it." In the FDE philosophy, the system going live is not the milestone; the customer's team changing how it works is. Sierra deliberately names the role internally "Agent Engineer," and its lead, Meurer, explains the selection criteria: they only take two kinds of problems — the genuinely hard, and the ones with genuine business impact, and both must hold at once.

"Let everything be judged by results" sounds like common sense, but executing it is an offense against the entire structure of interests: sales no longer dares to over-promise, because the delivery team is accountable for results; the customer's IT department can no longer settle up with a "feature checklist," because the business unit's usage rate has become the acceptance criterion; and the FDE can't exempt himself with "I built what was asked," because whether the requirement itself was right or wrong is on his tab too. This is exactly why the role is precious — and why it's expensive.

## 1.6 The FDE's Four Faces on the Team

An FDE lives in four worlds at once; he is the connector between the four.

- **To the customer, he is an "embedded product manager + full-stack engineer":** observing the customer's real work like an anthropologist — the most valuable discoveries usually come from "watching," not "asking" — and, like a founder, building things directly at the scene of that observation. Palantir institutionalized this two-person pairing: the deployment strategist, codenamed "Echo," is responsible for reading the customer's mission, stakeholders, and adoption path; the Forward Deployed Engineer, codenamed "Delta," is responsible for the technical implementation. Two to a team, one diagnoses, one builds, neither dispensable.

- **To the company's product line, he is a "scout and intelligence officer":** This is the most essential difference between the FDE and a traditional delivery team. The cost of traditional implementation is a sales cost — every person-day spent has to be earned back from the contract; a healthy FDE organization treats on-site work as R&D — three customers hitting the same integration gap isn't three headaches, it's a piece of product intelligence; five deployments all needing the same workflow means it should be abstracted into the platform's next standard capability. One former Palantir engineer recalled that key components of the Foundry platform were born at customer sites as far-flung as Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, and Toulouse, grew bottom-up, and were ultimately fed back into a product with billions in annual revenue. (Source in Appendix C.)

- **To sales, he is a "trust amplifier":** Enterprise customers have been let down too many times and are immune to any slide deck. The FDE rebuilds trust with two moves: first, get his hands dirty, building something that actually runs, on the customer's own data, in the customer's own environment, on the spot; second, be honest, daring to say no to the customer's mistaken premises. Palantir's bootcamp turned this trust production into a process: the customer brings real data, and in one to five days a deployable prototype is built, which executives click and use with their own hands. Early bootcamps had a paid-conversion rate of only 5% to 10%; the later conversion rate the company disclosed was already close to 75%. Trust can be manufactured, in an engineered way. (Source in Appendix C.)

- **To the organization itself, he is a "talent crucible":** An easily overlooked fact: Palantir produced an astonishingly dense group of founders. This is no surprise — the FDE's daily training is, in an environment of constrained resources, vague requirements, and complex relationships among parties, building something valuable end-to-end and getting people to use it, which is almost the complete dress rehearsal for founder training. Sreenivas, who later founded Decagon; Meurer, who built Sierra's agent-engineering team; the several authors who wrote this industry's most widely circulated methodology articles — all came out of Palantir's forward-deployment roles. One company's talent spillover became an entire industry's talent infrastructure. (Source in Appendix C.)

These four identities together point to the same conclusion: the FDE is not a box on the org chart, but an upgrade to how the organization learns — turning "understanding the customer" from secondhand information relayed layer by layer into an engineer's own hands-on muscle memory.

## 1.7 How to Hire an FDE

First, a bucket of cold water: the FDE is one of the hardest roles in the software industry to hire, because it demands that one person be excellent on two dimensions that usually trade off against each other.

Former Palantir engineer Barry laid this bare in a retrospective article: Palantir's bar for hiring FDEs was "engineers who could get into Google or Facebook" — because they were going to customer sites to **build** systems, not to tune parameters; but technical skill alone is nowhere near enough, and the forward-deployed person also needs creativity, judgment, and charisma facing customers. He added a stinging line: this is far more expensive and difficult than hiring a traditional pre-sales team.

Unpacking the practices of the hiring market, FDE hiring has three key elements.

- **Candidate profile:** Hire the "curious bulldozer," not the "refined craftsman." a16z's advice to startups used the phrase "curiosity-driven doer": strong initiative, little reverence for the status quo, a hunger for the customer's problem. McGrew is more specific: the FDE team needs two kinds of people — the "domain rebel," who knows the industry but doesn't worship its conventions, and the "prototype speedster," for whom speed beats perfection and who accepts that the first version will be thrown away and rewritten. Conversely, two kinds of people favored in traditional engineering culture are warning signs for the FDE role: the "craftsman" who places elegant code above customer outcomes, and the "loyal executor" who treats the customer's every word as gospel.

- **Interview:** Replace rote formulas with the "deconstruction round." The "problem deconstruction" mentioned earlier is the soul of the FDE interview: give the candidate a vague, enormous problem with the rough edges of real business — "a bank's compliance team manually reviews thirty thousand transaction alerts a day, nine in ten are false alarms, what do you do" — then observe for sixty minutes. What's assessed isn't the answer, it's the process: did they clarify constraints before acting, did they distinguish root cause from symptom, did they remember there's a real user sitting at the other end of the system, can they articulate the trade-offs clearly. Palantir also embeds about twenty minutes of behavioral questions in each technical round, and will explicitly reject a candidate who is technically strong but a cultural mismatch — the most important cultural item being a sense of order in the face of ambiguity.

- **Pricing:** Accept a hybrid structure of "engineer compensation + operating flex." The 2026 market data can serve as an anchor: Palantir Forward Deployed Engineers have a median total annual compensation of about $215,000; a mid-level FDE at a top AI lab is about $385,000, a senior about $610,000; Anthropic's roles have a base salary between $200,000 and $300,000. Another detail worth noting is bonus design: Palantir's bonuses are often tied to operating metrics like customer expansion, sitting between an engineering bonus and a sales commission; a16z's advice is to align incentives with the account manager, but don't saddle the FDE with a hard sales quota — that steers behavior toward signing deals rather than delivering results. The compensation structure is the last step in defining the role: the way you pay ultimately shapes how people behave. (Source in Appendix C.)

## 1.8 How to Become an FDE

Flip the perspective: if you're an engineer, product manager, or consultant who wants to enter this fast-growing market, how do you get there?

First, self-assess: the glamour and the cost of this role are two sides of one coin. In the forum practitioner community, the discussion of the FDE has a rare honesty. The upside: the best combination of technical substance and brand endorsement, one of the few roles where you can accumulate technical, business, and customer resources at the same time. The cost: a quarter to half your time traveling is the norm — OpenAI's job postings explicitly state travel can reach up to 50%; your work rhythm is defined by the customer's urgency, not your own schedule; and one recurring reminder — the risk of burnout is real.

One comment was sharp enough to copy down verbatim: "Some treat it as a brand springboard, some call it consulting with a cool title — both are right, and the difference is whether the company you're at feeds on-site learning back into the product, or sells you by the person-day." That line is both a criterion for choosing where to work and the theme of Chapter 7 of this book. (Source in Appendix C.)

Now the make-up class: what you have to make up isn't technology, it's the craft of "translation." A technical foundation is only the entry ticket, which most engineers have; what's truly scarce is three translation abilities — translating a business problem into a technical problem (Chapter 2), translating a technical solution into words an executive can understand (Chapter 3), and translating on-site experience into knowledge the team can reuse (Chapter 7). To practice these three, taking a class beats nothing like taking the field: shadow a pre-sales cycle, do a stint on site, run a training for a real user, then see which discomfort you grow fastest in.

On interview preparation, rewrite your résumé to be "customer-outcome-oriented." The principle was stated earlier, and it's worth stressing again: every technical achievement on the résumé must walk the last mile to the customer's language. "Built a retrieval-augmented system" is engineer's language; "the retrieval-augmented system I built cut first-response time in customer service from 4 hours to 8 minutes, and at renewal the customer proactively asked to expand it" is FDE language. Prepare two kinds of stories at once: one time you built order when requirements were unclear, and one honest failure — Palantir-lineage interviewers have an obsession with "tell me a real failure," because the essence of this job is advancing amid uncertainty, and a person who won't admit failure has no capacity to evolve.

When choosing a company, ask three questions in return. First, "what is your product platform" — an FDE without a platform base is pure staffing outsourcing. Second, "how does on-site learning flow back into the product" — ask them to tell you a recent instance of something settling from the field into a product feature; if they can't, that's a problem. Third, "who does the FDE report to" — reporting to the product or engineering line usually means the model is taken seriously; reporting to the sales line, be careful of becoming a human resource pool for pre-sales.

## 1.9 The FDE's Everyday Toolbox

To close the chapter, here is the full panorama of this role's current tooling. Tools go out of date, but the layering of capabilities behind the tools does not. Five layers, from underfoot to behind your back.

- **The platform-base layer | the company's weapon.** The FDE model holds together on the premise of "bringing a platform to the site," or it degenerates into custom development. The core of Palantir's Foundry and AIP is that thing called the "Ontology" — modeling the enterprise's data, logic, and actions into a semantic layer, so AI runs on a foundation that "understands the business." OpenAI's model APIs and agent toolchain; Sierra's agent platform. When evaluating any FDE opportunity, the thickness of this layer is the top priority.

- **The AI-engineering layer | the individual's craft.** The daily crafts after 2025 include: prompt engineering and context management; retrieval augmentation, letting the model look things up before it answers; evaluation systems, building a quantifiable ruler for vague business quality — this is the signature skill distinguishing the AI-era FDE from the traditional implementation engineer; agent architecture, tool calls, multi-agent collaboration, keeping a human in the loop at critical junctures; and the engineering optimization of cost and speed.

- **The data-and-integration layer | the first battle on arrival.** Almost every FDE project's first week is spent wrestling with this layer: data pipelines, enterprise-system connectors, permissions and identity authentication, vector databases for the AI to look things up in, data governance and de-identification (masking sensitive information). One practitioner's hard-won wisdom: seventy percent of a project's schedule gets stuck here, but you can't see it in the demo — it's the underwater part of the iceberg.

- **The delivery-and-collaboration layer | survival gear in the customer's environment.** Working inside the customer's security perimeter means a "double adaptation": you must be able to use your own modern toolchain, and also stoop to the customer's environment — which may be an intranet physically isolated from the internet, may only allow deployment inside the customer's cloud, may not even reach a code-hosting website. Containerization (the technology for packing up an environment and taking it with you), infrastructure-as-code (managing server environments with code), and a contingency plan for "getting the environment running even in a conference room with no network" all belong to this layer.

- **The knowledge-settling layer | the lever for scale.** This is the most easily overlooked layer, but it decides whether the team can escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount": playbooks, component libraries, deployment checklists, and the writing habit of rewriting "one customer's solution" into "a pattern for a class of customers." Chapter 7 will unpack it specifically.

The five layers of the toolbox together are the complete outline of this role: a platform underfoot, engineering in hand, results in your eyes, the product line at your back.

That is the FDE. In the seven chapters that follow, we enter the heartland of the methodology, starting from a project's most fundamental choice: how to make sure you're solving the right problem.
