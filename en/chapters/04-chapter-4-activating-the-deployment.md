## 4.1 Going Live Is Not Activation: The "First-Day Curse" of Enterprise Deployment

First, define this chapter's core concept. In consumer internet, "activation" means a new user completing a key action and experiencing the product's "aha moment." In enterprise deployment, activation means **the target user group forming a stable habit of using the system in their daily work** — not applause at the demo, but the system still being used frequently three months later with no one nagging.

In other words: "going live" for enterprise software is an administrative event; "activation" is a behavioral event. The former can be celebrated; only the latter counts.

A set of numbers from that MIT report deserves to be laid out again at the start of this chapter: only about forty percent of enterprises provide employees an official AI-tool subscription, while as many as ninety percent of employees do their work day-to-day with personal, consumer-grade AI tools. This means the real state of many enterprises is a "dual track": the official system spinning idle, shadow AI running rampant. The system went live; activation never happened.

Why do enterprise deployments so commonly die at activation? Because they have to clear three gates entirely unlike those of a consumer product. Consumer products die of "not fun"; enterprise deployments die in the users' hands: the user finds it clumsy — one step more than the old habit, and nobody uses it; the user finds it untrustworthy — wrong once, and trust resets to zero; the user finds it irrelevant to them — and then even fewer touch it. Not one of these three gates can be cleared at HQ; you can only clear them in the customer's conference rooms, on the shop floor, at the workstation. So activation is the FDE's home turf, and the biggest difference from traditional implementation that "delivers and leaves": traditional implementation treats the sign-off sheet as the finish line, the FDE treats the change in customer behavior as the finish line.

The seven sections of this chapter correspond to the seven weapons of activation.

## 4.2 Rapid Iteration: The "Hotfix" Culture of the Deployment Period

Internet products iterate through data experiments, approaching the optimum in fast small steps. You can't run rigorous data experiments in an enterprise-deployment setting — samples too small, too much interference — but its spiritual core, fast, small-step, evidence-based iteration, becomes a way of working during deployment: responding to every small user complaint as fast as you'd firefight a production incident — I call it the "hotfix."

Consumer products iterate on a cadence of "releases" — weekly, biweekly. The FDE deployment-period iteration cadence is measured in "days," even "hours": the business user says in the morning "this output is missing the supplier-code field," and by afternoon the field is added; the shop-floor supervisor says today "I can't tap this interface accurately with gloves on," and tomorrow the button is doubled in size. This response speed means far more to the user than the feature itself — **every overnight fix is a top-up of the user's trust.** In the first month, the user forms a judgment: is this team "for real," or "another one that delivers and leaves."

The hotfix culture has three execution points.

First, feedback must reach the person writing the code directly, with no relay in between. In the old way, a user's feedback went through customer success, the product manager, the schedule, and only then to the engineer — losing half the context at each hand-off, and by the time it got scheduled, a week had passed and the user's enthusiasm had cooled. Under the FDE model, feedback reaches the person writing the code directly, and ideally that person sits right next to the user. What Palantir shortened is exactly this loop: the Forward Deployed Engineer can build, test, learn, and feed back on site, without waiting for a formal product-requirement process to run its course.

Second, prioritize iteration by "blocking severity," not "feature importance." The trade-off logic of the deployment period is the reverse of the product period. In the product period you rank requirements by strategic value; in the deployment period you ask "what is blocking tomorrow's use" — a color problem, if it makes shop-floor workers feel "this was built for office white-collars," is the top priority; a powerful prediction feature the user can't currently use ranks after activation is done. Win usage first, then win depth.

Third, at the end of each day ask one question: which moment of the user's tomorrow did today's change make smoother? Fix things this way, and you're not just "answering every request" but proactively clearing every friction point between the user and the system. A fix isn't just closing a defect — every fix is a conscious act of activation design: you're dismantling, one by one, the friction points between the user and the system. The list of friction points hides in the on-arrival due diligence's process map and in daily observation.

OpenAI's FDE working method has a corresponding rhythm: early co-creation (on-site whiteboard alignment), validation (building the evaluation system), delivery (multi-day on-site building). Note that the delivery phase is still in units of "multi-day on-site" — the reason you can fix things this fast is that a person is standing next to the problem.

## 4.3 Iterating in the Customer's Environment: Evaluation-System-Driven Quality Improvement

If 4.2 was about the "speed" of iteration, this section is about its "sense of direction." In AI deployment, the vehicle for that sense of direction is a practice that only became mainstream after 2024: **the evaluation system** — continuously scoring the system's output against a set of standard cases.

Traditional software quality has only two states: the feature is right or wrong, the test passes or fails. AI-system quality is continuous, probabilistic, and scenario-dependent — the same answer that dazzles in the demo may be a disaster in a specific business context. Deadlier still, the power to define "good" belongs to the business side, not engineering: an answer the model thinks is perfect, a domain expert may spot as amateurish at a glance. Without this evaluation ruler, iterating an AI deployment is a blindfolded sprint — you think you're optimizing, but you're actually drifting at random.

The engineering practice of the evaluation system has, in leading teams, settled into three steps.

Step one: grow it from real cases. The eval set can't be made up by engineers; it must come from the customer's real business corpus. The essence of this step is making the domain expert's tacit judgment explicit as an executable ruler.

Step two: make the business side the judges. The evaluation system isn't an engineer's self-indulgent tool; the reviewers must include the business side. The best approach is to make the evaluation into a form domain experts can join: side-by-side output comparisons, simple better/worse labeling, regular review meetings. This process has a double payoff: the eval set gets more accurate, and the business side understands the system more deeply — watching the system answer their own cases better each time, the accumulation of trust is seen with their own eyes, not received in a report.

Step three: wire the evaluation system into the production loop. Going live isn't the end of evaluation. Continuously collect real inputs and outputs in production, sample and evaluate them regularly, and alert immediately when the score dips — this turns "quality" from a one-time pre-launch acceptance into continuous care across the lifecycle. What users are most sensitive to isn't average quality, it's the stability of quality: the collapse of trust from one system error takes ten correct answers to fill back in.

The John Deere collaboration is the most complete public sample of "evaluation-system first," worth slowing down to examine. What this company set out to solve was herbicide waste: traditional sprayers cover the whole field, while its "See & Spray" technology uses 36 cameras plus machine vision to spray only weeds while moving at 12 to 15 miles per hour — covering three football fields' worth of area a minute. The ideal of precision agriculture is grand: the US plants 12 trillion corn and soybean plants a year, the best farmland yields 200 bushels per acre, and top growers can hit 600 — "if every single plant could be tended to individually, yields could be transformed." Those are the words of John Deere technology executive Justin Rose.

But what farmers want isn't technology, it's trustworthy advice. OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineers flew to Iowa, went into the fields with agronomists, first reviewed hundreds of real field cases with experts and built a bespoke evaluation system, then rapidly iterated the model — and had to keep pace with the growing season, because missing the planting window means missing a whole year. The final result: chemical use reduced by up to 70%, and farmer interaction frequency up 6×. Note the order of these two numbers: **first the "good" defined by the evaluation system, then the "good" the model delivers.** (Source in Appendix C.)

The deeper meaning of the evaluation system: put plainly, it turns the old master's mental "this is what good looks like" into a scoring standard the system executes automatically every day. This is exactly the micro-miniature of the FDE model — the customer's knowledge is no longer just words in a requirements doc, but a ruler living inside the system.

## 4.4 Finding Another Way to Lower the Barrier to Use

Getting the user to value with the fewest actions is common sense in product design. But an enterprise system's barrier to use is often an obstacle outside the system. Four "barrier-lowering" techniques repeatedly validated in deployment practice.

Technique one: parasitize the user's existing interface. Wherever the user's main battlefield is, that's where your system should appear: he works in a spreadsheet, you build a spreadsheet plugin; he approves in email, you let the approval happen in email; he works in a ticketing system, you embed the AI recommendation into the ticket card. Requiring him to log in to a new system is like adding a wall between you and him that he has to climb every day. OpenAI cutting in through the ChatGPT interface 120,000 employees already used at BBVA, and Anthropic letting the model enter the customer's existing workflow tools via an open protocol, are the same logic. **Each additional login sheds a chunk of activation rate.**

BBVA is the best sample of "entering the organization along the old habit." It began collaborating with OpenAI in May 2024, and the first step was to hand out just 3,300 ChatGPT Enterprise accounts — no all-hands campaign, let the seed users play first. Employees quickly created more than 20,000 custom mini-assistants on their own, about 4,000 of them used frequently; management didn't ban "shadow AI," but did the opposite: "We give everyone a safe platform so they can experiment with confidence." Structured training was paired with it: 250 executives (including the chairman himself) took the course first, a "network of AI pioneers" was built across the bank, and a cohort of advanced users internally called "AI geeks" was cultivated. The data over a year later: employees saved on average about 3 hours a week, and 83% used it actively every week. In December 2025, the two announced a bank-wide rollout: 25 countries, 120,000 employees, and launched an end-to-end transformation roadmap called "the eight things." From 3,300 to 11,000 to 120,000, each step of expansion happened after the usage data from the previous step was published — this is activation by "letting the habit walk on its own." (Source in Appendix C.)

Technique two: activation rate hides in the defaults. A new user's first reaction to a blank system is "now what," and most churn happens in those three seconds. The FDE's solution is to pre-load the "first use": pre-built templates, pre-filled examples (based on the customer's own data), preset guidance (on first open, there's already a to-do that belongs to him). On day one, the system should understand what he might want to do better than he does — the things you observed by staking out on site those first few days now come into play.

Technique three: translate "ask the AI" into "click a button." Enterprise users' fluency with "conversing with AI" is far more uneven than imagined. Making users compose their own questions offloads the engineering burden onto the person least suited to bear it. The mature approach is to package high-frequency scenarios into one-click actions: "generate last week's anomaly report," "reconcile this batch of invoices," "draft this customer letter" — behind the button is a complete prompt and workflow tuned through evaluation. Leave conversational interaction for exploration, button interaction for the everyday.

Technique four: be a "copilot" first, talk "autopilot" later. Facing a high-risk, high-resistance process, don't push automation all at once. Let the system exist first as a "suggester" — AI drafts, a human confirms; AI flags, a human adjudicates. Through confirmation after confirmation, the user builds a trust calibration for the system's judgment; only when the confirmation-pass rate is high enough does automation get on the agenda. John Deere's solution to this day retains the agronomist's final decision; agent designs in financial-compliance scenarios generally retain a human check. The copilot strategy isn't conservative, it's the optimal path between activation rate and risk.

## 4.5 The Protracted Integration War

Every FDE veteran carries scars from the same war: the integration war against the customer's "legacy systems" — those old systems that have run for years inside the enterprise that no one dares touch.

The war's cruelty exceeds the imagination of anyone who hasn't been on the front line. a16z's description is incisive: the context an AI application needs — history, business logic, permission systems — is all locked in the enterprise's internal databases, interfaces, and workflows, and the work of connecting them "was never optional, it's mandatory." Finance's legacy mainframes, healthcare's heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics, manufacturing's dozens of self-governing shop-floor systems — the common thread of these environments is: outdated docs, broken interfaces, and half the people who understood them already retired.

This war has four strategic points.

- **Fight integration as a campaign, not a chore.** In most project plans, integration is written as a single line "system integration: 2 weeks," and then swells in execution to four months. The root of the error is treating integration as a technical chore — it's technical archaeology *and* organizational politics *and* data governance, an out-and-out hard battle. The right posture: draw the system map during on-arrival due diligence, tier and schedule the integration risks, and gnaw the hardest bones earliest — integration has the greatest uncertainty, and every day you delay its start, the whole project's timeline runs naked one more day.

- **Second, solve the data problem before the model problem.** That report has a widely cited insight: the underlying cause of many AI-project failures is "garbage in, garbage out" — AI connected to an ungoverned data source, ten versions of the same document hit at random, and the output is naturally untrustworthy. Palantir's solution is the Ontology: first model the enterprise's data assets into a layer with business semantics, clarifying "which field is authoritative, which version is valid, who has the right to see what," and run AI on this foundation. The universal lesson of this path is: **data governance isn't the preliminary engineering of an AI project, it *is* the AI project itself.**

The US Navy's "shipbuilding operating system" is the most spectacular footnote to this lesson. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Palantir's CEO jointly announced a $448 million contract: initially covering two large shipyards, three naval dockyards, and a hundred suppliers. Shipbuilding's data environment is a textbook-level disaster: ERP systems, decades-old databases, and paper drawings coexisting. And two numbers from the pilot stage shut everyone up: at nuclear-submarine maker General Dynamics Electric Boat, **submarine scheduling was compressed from 160 person-hours to under 10 minutes**; at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, **material review time was compressed from weeks to under an hour**.

The Secretary of the Navy stressed: "This isn't a concept, isn't a pilot, isn't research — this is already being done." (Source in Appendix C.) Note the order of this case: first put in the heavy effort to connect the data into a unified semantic layer, and only then can the efficiency miracle happen — not the other way around.

The same logic recurs in three completely different industries. The fast-food chain Wendy's: syrup-shortage dispatch across 6,450 North American stores used to take 15 employees a whole day to check, and after connecting to a data platform, a plan comes out in five minutes. The mortgage giant Fannie Mae: uses AI to identify mortgage fraud with a detection rate over 99%, far surpassing its former rule-based system. Citi: customer credit approval compressed from hours to minutes — AI reads through credit history, transaction habits, industry risk, and affiliated companies in one go. Three industries, one common premise: first arrange the scattered data into a semantic layer a machine can understand, and only then does intelligence have somewhere to land. (Source in Appendix C.)

- **Third, use AI to fight AI's integration war.** An important variable after 2025: integration work itself is starting to be automated by AI. The scenario a16z envisioned has partly become reality — for a legacy system with no interface, use a browser agent to mimic a human and pull data; field mapping, format conversion, interface-doc interpretation are largely handed to the model. Leading teams' self-requirement is worth quoting: "Automate the integration process as much as possible — process mining, data pipelines, system integration, interface-doc mapping — because this speed advantage compounds." Using AI to do the work of AI deployment may be the most wonderful thing about this role.

- **Fourth, know when to bypass rather than conquer.** Not every legacy system is worth integrating head-on. For some systems the right solution is "shadow reading" (read-only snapshots, nightly sync), for some it's "human ferrying" (keeping a manual step during the transition), and for some it's simply "declared isolation" (the process's data is out of this project's scope, tell the customer clearly). An engineer's ego always wants to conquer every fortress; the FDE's judgment shows in choosing the battlefield — remember your goal isn't total technical victory, it's the customer's outcome.

## 4.6 Change Management: Getting the Customer's Organization to Champion You

This section handles activation's biggest soft resistance: the organization. Of the report's five roadblocks, "employee resistance" and "change management" together take up nearly half. Bringing tech live needs only engineers; changing behavior needs the whole organization.

In the FDE context, change management centers on cultivating three groups of people.

- **The champion | your internal ally:** Behind every successful deployment stands a champion inside the customer: he genuinely believes in this, willing to stake his own reputation to clear the way for you. David Wakeling of Linklaters is the archetype — as head of the firm's market innovation, he was Harvey's counterpart, advocate, and umbrella within the firm. Key points for cultivating a champion: give him material he can tell (a story worth telling), give him victories (turn his vision into a highlight of his career), give him a sense of safety (you take the front when things fail). One well-treated champion beats ten product pitches.

- **The influencer | the informal opinion leader:** Every organization has a set of uncrowned kings: senior analysts, the old master on the shop floor, the person in the department you "just ask." They hold no power, but they hold trust. Their one line, "this thing actually works," outweighs three all-hands emails from management; their one line, "all flash," is enough to kill the system silently at the grassroots. During activation you must deliberately cultivate this group: invite them to be the first testers, take every one of their complaints seriously, and make your adoption of their suggestions visible — "this field was added on Master Wang's advice." Making the influencer a co-author is the shortest path to breaking the "I don't use what I didn't build" mentality.

- **The losers | the interest groups the solution disturbs:** Automation inevitably redistributes work, and redistributing work inevitably creates losers: the approval role whose sense of existence has been compressed, the department whose information wall has been pierced, the veteran whose "exclusive craft" has been replaced. Ignore them, and they become the system's most stubborn underground resistance — passively uncooperative, spreading incident stories, voting no at acceptance. Mature change management designs "a way out for the losers" in advance: steer the freed-up labor toward higher-value work (and publicly commit to no layoffs), transform "gatekeepers" into "coaches" (the old master's experience used to train the system), let the losers see their place in the future. This is both humane and purely utilitarian — the cost of resistance is far higher than the cost of appeasement.

The collaboration between the ticketing platform Vivid Seats and Sierra shows what "mobilizing the whole customer side" during activation looks like. After deciding to bring in an agent, this company's product, customer-experience, and engineering teams all went all-in — "once we make a decision, we commit 100%, everyone re-tests." The result: less than four weeks from launch to go-live, and after go-live the customer self-resolution rate rose 40% and customer satisfaction rose 35%.

But more valuable is what changed afterward: with routine questions taken over by the agent, the customer-experience team shifted from "firefighting in a queue" to fixing process problems at the root, even with spare capacity for fan-delight projects like "surprise upgrades"; and the agent's conversation data began feeding back into the product — "if ten thousand people ask about the same feature in a month, we can immediately move it up in priority. The best moment is: a product improvement driven by a pattern, so the user never needs to ask for help again." (Source in Appendix C.) **The highest state of activation is the system making itself unnecessary** — this case also foreshadows Chapter 7's "feeding the product from the field."

A corresponding sample in Chinese companies is appearing too. Ren Xiliang, head of efficiency engineering at the e-commerce company Dewu, breaks a ten-thousand-person company's AI transformation into three steps: first reach consensus with everyone, lower the tool barrier, get everyone using it first; second, split enterprise scenarios into four quadrants by fault tolerance, and hand scenarios with large fault tolerance (like business analysis) to AI first; third, set up a dedicated knowledge-operations group that burrows into the business teams and moves experts' tacit experience out of individual heads into a space AI can see. Consensus, scenario, knowledge — the order can't be reversed: first people willing to use it, then pick the right places to use it, and finally give AI the rice to cook with. (Source in Appendix C.)

Change management's ultimate test has only one standard: **after your team withdraws, is the system still used?** If it's blazing while you're there and cools fast once you leave, that wasn't activation, it was a backing dance. A truly activated organization keeps moving forward on its own.

## 4.7 I, Robot — Automating the Delivery Work Itself

Delivering automation to the customer is the product itself; automating your own delivery work is efficiency. This section covers the latter — it's the first lever for an FDE team to escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount."

In an FDE's daily work, a startling proportion of the actions are templatable repetitive labor: initializing a new customer's deployment environment, the standard pipeline for data ingestion, answering the security-review questionnaire, the pre-launch checklist, the format of periodic customer reports. Every action done a second time should trigger a question: "Can this become a script, a template, or a checklist?"

Leading teams' practices have settled into four kinds of assets.

- **Deployment templates:** package environment setup, permission configuration, and monitoring integration into one-click infrastructure-as-code. When a new customer arrives, you can stand up a standardized environment in their cloud on day one, instead of configuring from scratch for a week. Decagon can compress a simple-scenario deployment to 15 days, backed by a highly templated integration layer. (Source in Appendix C.)

- **Integration component library:** connectors for mainstream enterprise systems (pre-built integration pieces), written once, reused everywhere. Palantir's Ontology model essentially takes this logic to the extreme: the product of data ingestion isn't a one-off pipeline but a reusable data asset carrying business meaning.

- **Checklist culture:** the security-review checklist, the go-live admission checklist, the withdrawal-handover checklist. The checklist is humanity's oldest tool against complexity, and the bedrock of an FDE team's quality stability — it turns "reliance on individual experience" into "reliance on organizational memory."

- **Automated reporting:** both the weekly report to the customer and the field intelligence to the company should be semi-automatically generated. An FDE's time is too expensive per unit to spend on copy-pasting — and certainly shouldn't let "forgot to report" sever the loop between field and product that Chapter 7 will cover.

How considerable the compounding of automation is can be corroborated from one angle: a16z lists "build or buy tools to automate service delivery" as a key piece of advice for building a forward-deployment team, and judges it the key variable that lets this generation of AI companies run faster and clear lower per-deal thresholds than the last generation of enterprise-software companies. Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live at four weeks, and Palantir compressing the sales cycle from nine months to weeks, are not powered by engineers working overtime, but by turning yesterday's delivery into today's scaffolding.

This section also answers the most common challenge — "isn't the FDE just a human-wave tactic?" The essence of the human wave isn't a lot of people, it's everyone doing non-reusable, one-off labor. **When every delivery by an FDE team paves the road for the next delivery, it's no longer a human wave but a machine accelerating itself.**

The seven weapons of activating the deployment are covered: the speed of the hotfix, the direction of the evaluation system, the finesse of lowering the barrier, the patience of the integration war, the soft skill of change management, the lever of automation. The system is alive, and the people are moving. But the cruelty of enterprise business is: activation only buys time; renewal is survival. Next chapter — defending the renewal.
