# Chapter 7 — Scaling by Replication

> "If you don't turn on-site learning into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects."
> — Barry, former Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer

## 7.1 Leveraging Delivery with Reproducibility

The highest state of internet growth is having one user bring more users — the growth engine turning from bought-in to homegrown. The question the FDE must answer is the same: **each customer you finish should make the next customer easier — not by adding people, but by banking the experience of the last one.**

This proposition is the book's "last mile," because it's the final line of defense between the FDE model and the consulting and outsourcing industries. If you strip out this chapter, what becomes of the plays in the previous six? An elite team that can fight hard battles takes on a customer, delivers to that customer, earns enough to cover the cost, then takes on the next — revenue growth perfectly linear with headcount growth. Congratulations, you've reinvented the consultancy.

Barry drew this dividing line most sharply in his retrospective: "If you don't turn on-site learning into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects." McGrew's formulation is from another angle: the mark that the FDE model holds is "the amount of customization decreasing with each subsequent customer" — if the customization work you do doesn't decrease with the number of customers, you're not doing FDE, you're doing a person-day business.

The lever of reproducibility has three tiers, each harder than the last and each more valuable.

- **Tier-one lever | knowledge replication (the playbook):** write "how to do it" into documents — playbook templates, checklists, training materials. It turns reliance on individual experience into reliance on organizational memory: a new hire's ramp-up shrinks from a year to three months. This is the most basic lever, and most teams stop here.

- **Tier-two lever | component replication (tools and code):** turn "what's been done" into "what can be used directly" — integration connectors, evaluation frameworks, deployment templates, industry data models. The next team's starting point is no longer zero, but the shoulders of the last project. The automation assets in Section 4.7 belong to this tier.

- **Tier-three lever | product replication (platform capability):** abstract "recurring customization" into a standard platform capability — from then on, this need no longer requires any engineer on site. Palantir's Ontology, Decagon's agent-flow configuration, Sierra's agent platform are all products of the tier-three lever. This tier changes the whole business's cost structure: **the first two tiers lower delivery cost; the third eliminates delivery cost.**

And the progression of these three tiers is the full version of McGrew's "gravel roads and highways": FDEs pave gravel roads (solving the problem at hand), the component team lays the crushed-stone roadbed (making the next car's ride smoother), and the platform team pours the highway (so from then on anyone can pass). The remaining seven sections of this chapter cover exactly this complete engineering, from paving to laying road.

## 7.2 Bad News Travels Far — Spreading the Postmortems of Failed Deployments

Turning an incident into an asset is a scarce organizational capability. An FDE organization's attitude toward failure decides whether it can possess replication capability: **failure is the raw material of replication, and an organization that hides failure is burying a gold mine as garbage.**

First, get one thing straight: in the FDE model, failure isn't an accident, it's an inevitability. Barry's recollection is merciless: "There was plenty of failure — heaps of time, money, and travel expenses, burned spectacularly." Palantir simply wrote accepting failure into its institution: run pilots like venture bets, most will die, and after they die you must do a postmortem. He specifically noted: to the projects that crashed, the teams whose effort came to nothing, the burned-out engineers, you also owe a thank-you — the lessons were bought and paid for by them. This point is the hardest to learn, because engineers' nature is to celebrate success and hide failure.

Turning failure into an organizational asset takes three mechanisms.

- **Decriminalizing the postmortem:** the postmortem meeting's only rule: address the issue, not the person. Once the postmortem is tied to performance reviews, people start dressing up failure — and a dressed-up failure has no teaching value. Decriminalizing isn't refusing accountability; it's redefining "accountability" as "whether the lessons were extracted honestly and completely." A project lead who runs an honest postmortem of a failure may contribute more to the organization than one who succeeded by luck.

- **Structuring the lessons:** the postmortem's output can't be "be careful next time"-style musings; it must be searchable, executable knowledge: what are the high-risk signals of this customer type? Which integration assumption was overturned? Which evaluation metric was designed wrong? These conclusions should go into the "minefield" chapter of the playbook and into the due-diligence checklist for new projects — so the next team's "on-arrival due diligence" automatically carries the historical lessons. **A failure is only truly absorbed once it's written into the process.**

- **Sharing failure externally, in moderation:** this layer is the most counterintuitive: sharing failure lessons externally (after de-identification) is extremely efficient trust marketing. As Section 3.5 said, Barry's "airing of dirty laundry" article became the best evangelism for the Palantir model — the reader can tell who's speaking trade-show lines and who's speaking trench truth. A team that dares to dissect failure publicly conveys the signal "we've already paid the tuition for this industry's wrong turns" — exactly what enterprise customers most want to hear.

## 7.3 Riding the Momentum: Standing at the Industry's Tuyère

Borrowing the attention of an external event for your own use is a classic marketing move. Teams in the FDE space stand at an epic tuyère, and the window of a tuyère won't stay open forever.

The 2025-to-2026 industry tuyère was formed by several airflows converging: that report made the "95% failure rate" the heartache of every enterprise information leader — the customer's heartache is your opportunity; OpenAI and Anthropic forming deployment companies on the same day pushed "deployment" onto the financial headlines — the money the giants spend educating the market benefits the whole industry; the 800% growth in FDE postings made this role a regular in the tech press — even "should we hire an FDE" became a kind of corporate anxiety. These three airflows together created a rare situation: **the customer is proactively looking for you, discussing the very thing you want to sell.**

The first move of riding momentum is "defining the agenda." A tuyère's attention is a public resource, and whoever's content defines the agenda harvests the attention. a16z set the tone for the whole space with one industry manifesto; communities like OpenFDE, while aggregating practitioners, also aggregate the power to define. For a specific company, the best way to seize the agenda's defining power is to make public the things you keep at the bottom of the chest: the playbook, the failure postmortems, the value data — exclusive firsthand cognition is the only currency of agenda-defining power.

The second move is "binding to the industry agenda." The biggest driver of enterprise-AI procurement is upgrading from "efficiency" to "survival" — CEOs get pressed in the boardroom on "what's our AI strategy," and this anxiety conducts into budget. Translate your solution into "boardroom language": not "we can help you optimize the customer-service process," but "we can let you show, at your next board meeting, a quantifiable AI report card." The same delivery, anchored to a different agenda level, reaches a different budget pool.

The third move is "building assets during the tuyère, not consuming the tuyère." A tuyère stops. The discussion the report stirred up will cool; the media's buzzwords will go out of fashion. The most precious thing during a tuyère isn't signing a few more deals, but converting the attention the tuyère brings into long-term assets: lighthouse cases, a voice in industry methodology, a reproducible delivery system. **After the tuyère passes, the skinny-dippers are exposed by the receding tide, and those with assets reach the shore.**

## 7.4 Building a Propagation Loop Outside the Customer

Propagation that relies not on ads but on word of mouth happens in this line of work mainly through three circles.

Circle one: the practitioner community. The FDE is a professional community in formation — in 2025 it was still scattered inside various companies; by 2026 there was already a dedicated community like OpenFDE, a dedicated compensation report, a dedicated interview guide. The formation of a professional community is a heaven-sent dividend for early movers: contribute content to the community (methodology, tools, data), and your name gets bound to this emerging profession. The propagation path of a concept has always been thus: the early evangelist ends up as the concept's synonym.

Circle two: the customer's industry community. Every industry has its own small circle — the bankers' forum, the law-firm partners' annual meeting, the hospital administrators' summit. The trust dividend of Section 3.3 flows mainly within these closed circles. The ticket into these circles isn't an ad, it's the customer champion invited to speak — your job is to give him something worth telling and told well. A ten-minute share by your customer at his industry summit beats a hundred days at your booth at a trade show.

Circle three: the ecosystem-partner network. Section 3.7 covered the acquisition value of ecosystem bundling; here we stress its propagation property: cloud vendors' solution architects, consultancies' consultants, integrators' project managers — these people circulate among different customer sites every day, and the list of "reliable teams" in their mouths is the fastest, most accurate promotion channel. The key to cultivating this circle is to let them "win": let ecosystem partners gain results, cases, and reputation from working with you. **Someone who helps others succeed gets remembered by the whole network.**

The flywheel the three circles form together is: delivery creates stories, stories propagate in the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, new opportunities create new stories. What distinguishes this flywheel from traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "value already delivered" — the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is letting every ounce of past effort pull in future customers.

## 7.5 The Built-In Fission Factors of the Product

A good product propagates itself — the line "sent from such-and-such mailbox" in an email signature was the earliest self-propagation design. The system an FDE delivers can also pre-embed a "built-in diffusion mechanism," letting the system itself become the salesperson of expansion.

Factor one: visibility design. The system's outputs are naturally passed around — weekly reports, analysis results, approval flows. Let every output carry its "origin": a "generated by such-and-such system" in the report footer, a link to the handling entry in an anomaly alert. When a beautiful analysis report is forwarded to the next-door department head, whoever sees it naturally asks "what made this?" The essence of visibility design is to let the system's value exhibit itself in the daily workflow, rather than relying on a dedicated report.

Factor two: collaborative admission. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: the case an investigator flags needs a supervisor's review, the analyst's report needs the business side's confirmation — every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. New users brought by collaboration aren't "promoted to," they're "brought by the work itself," and their acceptance is entirely different.

Factor three: a self-serve exploration layer. Beyond the core delivery, leave a low-risk practice area (a sandbox): the customer's employees can try "have the AI do an analysis for me" in it themselves. The uses that grow in the sandbox are often the most vital expansion leads — a scenario an employee in some department played their way into has deeper roots than a scenario sales pushed ten times. **Let everyone help you do discovery, and let the FDE do the hard sieges.**

Two new 2026 products laid this factor out in the open. In March, Sierra launched "Ghostwriter": the customer uploads their standard operating procedures, historical conversation logs, even a photo of a whiteboard, or describes a goal in plain words, and the system automatically generates a production-ready agent covering voice, chat, and email across three channels and thirty-plus languages — the customer's business staff can "build an agent self-serve" for the first time, while the forward-deployment team shifts from "building each one by hand" to "acceptance and backstop." Decagon walked this path even earlier: the customer's customer-service manager writes the flow in natural language ("for returns over 30 days, first check the membership tier, and process it if it meets policy") without waiting for engineers to schedule it — reportedly, Chime's self-resolution rate reached 70%, Duolingo's ticket-deflection rate reached 80%, and the fitness platform ClassPass's customer-service cost dropped 95%. (Source in Appendix C.) **When the customer starts building for themselves, your role shifts from construction crew to design institute** — this is exactly the ultimate form of factor three.

Factor four: cross-boundary value evidence. Automatically generate a "this quarter's value report" each quarter and let it flow naturally to the customer's executive level. This report is both Chapter 5's renewal engineering and a knock on the door for vertical expansion — every number an executive sees plants a seed for "where else this system is worth using."

The common principle of the built-in factors is: **if you want others to help you propagate, you first have to make them genuinely realize value.** Any design that propagates for propagation's sake will be recognized in an enterprise setting as "a vendor's little trick" and resented. The enterprise market has no viral propagation, only "the chain reaction of word of mouth" — slow, but every step solid.

## 7.6 Handling Organizational Politics at Scale

The biggest obstacle to scaling was never technology, but people's turf.

When the system expands from one department to ten, you're actually rewriting the map of power inside the organization: information monopolies are broken (data one department hoarded is now visible company-wide), approval authority is compressed (what AI answers in a second no longer needs three days of layered signatures), professional walls are leveled (a veteran's "exclusive experience" is encoded into the system). Every change manufactures silent enemies.

For handling organizational politics, FDE veterans have settled three mental methods.

- **Method one: expansion must bring a "local."** Each time you enter a new department, first find that department's "local boss" — the most prestigious veteran — and invite him into the adaptation process: "For this scenario, what's different about your department?" His answers make the solution more accurate, and his participation makes the solution flow unobstructed on his turf. **People don't oppose what they helped build** — the most reliable lever in organizational behavior.

Harvey's expansion in top law firms is a classic lesson in reading organizational politics. Three tiers of people exist in a firm at once: partners — those who pay, but don't do the work themselves; associates — those who do the work, but don't sign; knowledge-management lawyers — touching neither side, yet seeing both sides' blind spots. A generic questionnaire would compress the three tiers into the same drop-down options and get a pile of distorted averages; Harvey's forward-deployment team instead designed interviews and demos for each tier separately: show partners "how much this deal earns," show associates "three fewer late nights a week," show KM lawyers "the firm's tacit experience finally has somewhere to settle." One deployment has to make all three tiers feel they are beneficiaries — miss any tier and the system gets quietly shelved at the corresponding step. (Source in Appendix C.)

- **Method two: give every step of expansion a "beneficiary."** Before expanding, work out clearly what each involved department gains: the department whose data wall was pierced gains a globally visible analytical capability; the department whose approval authority was compressed gains the power to focus on exceptions (AI handles the routine, humans focus on the tricky). Expansion without beneficiaries is invasion; expansion with beneficiaries is liberation — the only difference is whether you've thought through and clearly stated "what he gains."

- **Method three: always leave the "old order" its dignity.** The process the system replaces was once designed by someone capable; the experience being encoded was once some expert's life's work. In the rollout messaging, respect for the old order is respect for the old order's maintainers: "This process supported the company for the past ten years, and now we're going to take its ten years of experience and amplify it a hundredfold." Only an old world treated well won't make trouble in the new one.

## 7.7 Settling Replication Efficiency into the Playbook

The start of this chapter listed the playbook as a tier-one lever; this section unpacks its engineering details — because most teams' playbooks are written as "dead on arrival" document graveyards.

- **The playbook's first property: it lives in the process, not in the knowledge base.** A playbook that can only be found in the document system might as well not exist. The effective approach is to embed it in the workflow: at a new project's due-diligence stage, the system automatically pops up the due-diligence checklist for that customer type; at the deployment stage, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; at the postmortem stage, updating the playbook is a required output of the postmortem. **Knowledge not embedded in the process is just archaeological material.**

- **The playbook's second property: organized by "scenario," not "function."** A failed playbook is written by the company's org structure ("delivery process," "technical specs"); a successful playbook is written by customer scenario ("the financial anti-money-laundering scenario play," "the manufacturing scheduling scenario play," "the law-firm knowledge-base scenario play"). Each scenario playbook contains a fixed set of seven: typical pain points and fit-test key points, a due-diligence checklist (the high-risk signals specific to this scenario), the known minefields of data and integration, the metric templates for evaluation and acceptance, the role map for change management, the reference structure for pricing and expansion, and the lesson library from historical postmortems. Harvey's law-firm-scenario play could be replicated to PwC and Cleary because it wrote the experience of the first customer (Linklaters) into an inheritable scenario asset.

- **The playbook's third property: it must be "alive" — with an owner, a version, and depreciation.** Each scenario playbook assigns an owner (usually the most senior delivery team), is mandatorily reviewed and updated after each project ends, and gets a full overhaul every six months — deleting the outdated, merging the duplicated. A playbook with no owner will rot within a year into a trap that misleads new hires, worse than no playbook at all.

The relationship of the playbook to the tier-two lever (component library) and tier-three lever (platform) is: the playbook records "judgment," the component library settles "craft," the platform solidifies "capability." With all three in place, a newly formed FDE squad can reach eighty percent of a veteran team's combat power within two weeks — that is the real meaning of replication.

## 7.8 Planning and Polishing Productization

The final section covers the crowning work of the tier-three lever: crystallizing on-site experience into a product — the alchemy of the entire FDE model.

First, the most spectacular success: Palantir's Foundry. Today this platform, with billions in annual revenue supporting the company's hundreds-of-billions market cap, had its core components born at customer sites as far-flung as Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku — tools each Forward Deployed Engineer built separately to solve a local problem, which through years of natural selection and platform absorption finally grew into a unified product. Barry recalled this process as "entirely bottom-up": in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held," letting the field bloom in a hundred ways and taking the survivors into the core.

This process isn't an idyll; it has a rigorous inner logic that can be distilled into "the four questions of productization."

- **Question one:** is this on-site solution "particular" or "common"? The criterion: has a similar problem arisen independently at three or more customers? The same pain point at three customers is a product signal; a special request from one customer may just be a quirk. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let "multiple forward-deployment teams reinventing the wheel" naturally expose the commonality — when three teams have each built something similar, the platform team knows the time to absorb it has come.

- **Question two:** is the cost of generalizing smaller than the benefit? McGrew warned that on-site code is "fast and rough," and the cost of generalizing it is "often higher than writing the first version." The productization decision must do this math: the generalization investment versus the customization cost saved across some number of future customers. If it doesn't pencil out, leave it at the component tier and don't force platformization — every capability in the platform is a lifetime maintenance commitment.

- **Question three:** can it be used by "people who can't write code"? On-site tools are used by engineers; platform capabilities are used by everyone. The hardest step in productization is often not technology but interaction: turning an engineer's script into a feature business staff can configure. Decagon's agent-flow configuration (letting operations define flows in natural language) is the model for this step — pick the right abstraction layer and the capability's user base grows a hundredfold.

- **Question four:** after absorption, is there still room in the field? This is the subtlest balance: absorb too much into the platform and the field team degenerates into configurators, killing the soul of the FDE model (on-site creativity); absorb too little and the lever of scale never lifts. Palantir's solution is to maintain "radical delegation from base to front line" — the top sets only the goal, and the play belongs to the field. **The platform is responsible for making "common things easy"; the field is responsible for making "impossible things happen."**

For those who doubt this flywheel, the most forceful rebuttal is always gross margin: send people to the field, how can margin possibly be high? History has answered three times. ServiceNow's gross margin at IPO was 63.2%, dismissed as "too much like a services company" — ten years later 79%, with a market cap near $200 billion; Workday's at IPO was 54.1% — now 76%; Palantir's at IPO was 79%, reaching 82% by 2025, prettier than the vast majority of "pure software" companies.

The venture firm Insight Partners' summary is incisive: **these companies that bet heavily on implementation and services during a platform shift are exactly the ones that held on while everyone else ran the other way.** The secret is that the gross margin can climb year over year — as the platform's accumulation thickens, the customer can do more and more of the implementation work themselves (the bootcamp model has a large amount of integration work done on the customer side), and the service cost is continuously amortized down. Ugly first, then beautiful; heavy first, then light. (Source in Appendix C.)

To close the chapter, a story of "betting on the right direction." At the end of 2022, ChatGPT burst onto the scene, and everyone was guessing what Palantir would do. Sankar placed a bet that would later be quoted over and over: large models will quickly commoditize, and value will gather at the two ends — compute chips at one end, the Ontology (the semantic layer that lets a model work safely on enterprise data) at the other. He pushed most of the company's engineers onto the AI platform AIP. The result: Palantir's stock rose from single digits when he took over as CTO all the way to around $200; US commercial revenue grew triple digits for multiple consecutive quarters. The logic of this bet mirrors Chapter 1 of this book: **the stronger and cheaper the model, the more valuable the stretch of engineering that "wires the model into reality."** (Source in Appendix C.)

The endpoint of the four questions of productization is a gathering-up of the entire FDE business model: **the field is the probe, the platform is the lever, the product is the vehicle of compounding.** The more real the world the probe detects, the more solid the capability the platform settles; the more solid the platform, the farther the probe can go. Once this flywheel starts turning, you're no longer a company that "sends people to do work," but a company that "continuously converts the world's complexity into software assets" — the highest-margin, deepest-moat species since the birth of the software industry.
