## 4.1 Going Online Does Not Equal Activation: The "First Day Curse" of Enterprise Deployment

Let's define the core concept of this chapter first. In consumer internet, "activation" refers to new users completing key behaviors and experiencing the product's "Aha moment". In enterprise deployment, activation means **the target user group forming a stable habit of using the system in their daily work** — not applauding at the demo presentation, but the system still being used at high frequency three months later with no one pushing them.

In other words: "going online" for enterprise software is an administrative event; "activation" is a behavioral event. The former can be celebrated, but only the latter counts.

A set of numbers from that MIT report is worth presenting again at the beginning of this chapter: Only about 40% of enterprises provide official AI tool subscriptions for their employees, while up to 90% of employees use personal consumer-grade AI tools for work on a daily basis. This means the actual state of many enterprises is a "dual-track system": official systems idle, shadow AI runs rampant. The system went online, but activation never happened.

Why do enterprise deployments commonly die at the activation stage? Because it has to pass three hurdles, which are completely different from consumer products. Consumer products die from "not being fun", while enterprise deployments die at the hands of users: users find it clunky — if it takes one more step than the old habit, no one will use it; users feel it's untrustworthy — if it makes a mistake once, trust drops to zero; users feel it has nothing to do with them — then even fewer people will touch it. None of these three hurdles can be solved at headquarters; you can only overcome them by going to the customer's meeting rooms, workshops, and workstations. So activation is the FDE's home turf, and it is also the biggest difference from the traditional "deliver and leave" implementation: traditional implementation treats the acceptance form as the finish line, while FDE treats the change in customer behavior as the finish line.

The seven sections of this chapter correspond to the seven weapons of activation.

## 4.2 Rapid Iteration: The "Hotfix" Culture of the Deployment Phase

Internet product iteration relies on data experiments, approaching the optimum with small, quick steps. In enterprise deployment scenarios, strict data experiments cannot be conducted — sample size is too small, interference is too high — but its core spirit, rapid, small-step, evidence-based iteration, becomes a way of working during the deployment phase: responding quickly to every small user complaint just like repairing an online incident — I call this "hotfixing".

The iteration rhythm of consumer products is measured by "versions", updated weekly or bi-weekly. The iteration rhythm of the FDE deployment phase is measured in "days" or even "hours": in the morning, a business user says "this output is missing the supplier code field", and by the afternoon the field is added; today the workshop director says "this interface is hard to click accurately with gloves on", tomorrow the button is enlarged by a factor of two. The significance of this response speed to users far exceeds the feature itself — **every overnight fix is a recharge of user trust**. Users will form a judgment in the first month: "this team is for real" or "just another one that leaves after delivery."

The hotfix culture has three execution key points.

First, feedback must go directly to the person writing the code, without a broken telephone in between. In the past, user feedback had to go through customer success, product managers, scheduling, and finally to the engineer — half the context was lost with each handoff, and by the time it was scheduled, a week had passed and the user's heart had grown cold. Under the FDE model, feedback goes directly to the person writing the code, and ideally, the person writing the code sits right next to the user. What Palantir shortens is exactly this loop: frontline deployed engineers can build, test, learn, and send back right on site, without waiting for an official product requirement to go through the process.

Second, iteration priority is sorted by "usage blocking degree" rather than "feature importance". The logic of trade-offs during the deployment phase is opposite to that of the product phase. The product phase prioritizes requirements by strategic value, while the deployment phase needs to ask "what is blocking tomorrow's usage" — if a color scheme issue makes workshop workers feel "this is made for office white-collars", it is the highest priority; if a powerful predictive feature is something users cannot use right now, it is ranked after activation is completed. Win the usage first, then win the depth.

Third, ask one question when wrapping up work every day: which moment of the user's tomorrow did today's change make smoother? Fixing like this is not just "responding to every request", but proactively clearing every friction point between the user and the system. Fixing is not just defect elimination — every fix is a conscious activation design: you are dismantling the friction points between the user and the system one by one. The list of friction points is hidden in the process map from the entry due diligence and daily observations.

There is a corresponding rhythm division in OpenAI's FDE methodology: early co-creation (on-site whiteboard alignment), validation (building the evaluation system), delivery (multi-day on-site building). Note that the delivery phase still uses "multi-day on-site" as a unit — being able to fix things this quickly relies on people guarding right next to the problem.

## 4.3 Iterating in the Customer's Environment: Quality Improvement Driven by the Evaluation System

If 4.2 is about the "speed" of iteration, this section is about the "sense of direction" of iteration. In AI deployment, the carrier of the sense of direction is a practice that only became mainstream after 2024: the **evaluation system (evals)** — continuously scoring the system's output using a batch of standard cases.

The quality of traditional software has only two states: the function is right or wrong, the test passes or fails. The quality of AI systems is continuous, probabilistic, and scenario-dependent — the same answer that amazes in a demo might be a disaster in a specific business context. What's more fatal is that the right to define "good" lies with the business side, not the engineering side: an answer the model considers perfect might be recognized as amateurish by business experts at a glance. Without this evaluation yardstick, AI deployment iteration is just running blindfolded — you think you are optimizing, but you are actually randomly drifting.

The engineering practice of the evaluation system has been fixed into three steps among leading teams.

Step 1: Grow out of real cases. The evaluation set cannot be fabricated by engineers; it must come from the customer's real business corpus. The essence of this step is to make the implicit judgment of business experts explicit as an executable yardstick.

Step 2: Let the business side be the judges. The evaluation system is not a self-entertainment tool for engineers; the reviewers must include the business side. The best practice is to make the evaluation into a form that business experts can participate in: side-by-side output comparison, simple pros and cons annotation, regular review meetings. This process has dual benefits: the evaluation set becomes more and more accurate, and the business side's understanding of the system deepens — they see the system answer better and better on their own cases each time, and the accumulation of trust is seen with their own eyes rather than derived from reports.

Step 3: Connect the evaluation system to the production loop. Going online is not the end of evaluation. Continuously collecting real inputs and outputs in the production environment, conducting regular sampling evaluations, and triggering alerts immediately when scores drop — this turns "quality" from a one-time pre-launch acceptance into continuous care throughout the life cycle. What users are most sensitive to is not average quality, but the stability of quality: a collapse in trust caused by one mistake of the system takes ten correct actions to fill back up.

The John Deere partnership is the most complete public sample of "evaluation system first", which is worth slowing down to look at closely. What this company wants to solve is herbicide waste: traditional sprayers cover the whole field, while its "See & Spray" technology uses 36 cameras plus machine vision to spray only weeds while moving at 12 to 15 miles per hour — covering the area of three football fields in one minute. The ideal of precision agriculture is huge: the US plants 12 trillion corn and soybean plants every year; the best farmland yields 200 bushels per acre, and top growers can do 600 — "If every single plant could be taken care of individually, the yield would be earth-shattering." These are the exact words of Justin Rose, a tech executive at John Deere.

But what farmers want is not technology, but trustworthy advice. OpenAI's frontline deployed engineers flew to Iowa, went down to the fields with agronomists, first reviewed hundreds of real operational cases with experts, built a customized evaluation system, and then rapidly iterated the model — and they had to catch the farming season; missing the planting season meant missing a year. The final result: chemical usage was reduced by up to 70%, and the frequency of farmer interaction increased by 6 times. Please note the order of these two numbers: **first came the "good" defined by the evaluation system, then came the "good" delivered by the model**. (See Appendix C for sources)

The deep meaning of the evaluation system is this: to put it plainly, it is turning the "what is considered good" in the master worker's head into a scoring standard that the system automatically executes every day. This is exactly a microscopic epitome of the FDE model — the customer's knowledge is no longer just words in a requirements document, but a yardstick living within the system.

## 4.4 Finding Alternative Paths to Lower the Barrier to Usage

Allowing users to reach value with the minimum number of actions is common sense in product design. But the barrier to using enterprise systems is often obstacles outside the system itself. There are four "barrier-lowering" methods that have been repeatedly verified in deployment practices.

Method 1: Parasitize on the user's existing interfaces. Wherever the user's main battlefield is, that is where your system should appear: if they work in spreadsheets, you make a spreadsheet plugin; if they approve things in emails, you let the approval be completed within the email; if they work in a ticketing system, you embed AI suggestions into the ticket cards. Asking them to log into a new system is equal to adding a wall between you and them that must be climbed every day. OpenAI entering from the existing ChatGPT interfaces of BBVA's 120,000 employees, and Anthropic letting models enter customers' existing workflow tools via open protocols, share the same logic. **With every extra login, the activation rate drops a notch**.

BBVA is the best sample of "entering the organization by going along with old habits". It started cooperating with OpenAI in May 2024. The first step was issuing only 3,300 ChatGPT Enterprise accounts — not doing a company-wide movement, but letting seed users play with it themselves first. Employees quickly and spontaneously created more than 20,000 custom GPTs, of which about 4,000 are used at high frequency; management did not ban "shadow AI", but did the opposite: "We give everyone a secure platform and let them try it out with peace of mind." At the same time, structured training was provided: 250 executives (including the chairman himself) took classes first, and an "AI Pioneer Network" was established throughout the bank, cultivating a group of advanced users internally referred to as "AI Geeks". Data over a year later: employees save an average of about 3 hours per week, and 83% of them actively use it every week. In December 2025, the two sides took advantage of the momentum to officially announce a bank-wide rollout: 25 countries, 120,000 employees, and launched an end-to-end transformation roadmap called the "Eight Things". From 3,300 to 11,000 to 120,000, every step of expansion occurred after the usage data of the previous step was published — this is activation that "lets habits walk by themselves". (See Appendix C for sources)

Method 2: Activation rates are hidden in default values. New users' first reaction to a blank system is "and then what?", and most churn happens in those three seconds. The FDE's solution is to pre-install the "first use": pre-set templates, pre-filled examples (based on the customer's own data), and pre-configured guidance (when opening it for the first time, there is already a to-do list belonging to them). On the first day, the system should know better than the user what they might want to do — the things observed during the stakeout in the previous few days come in handy here.

Method 3: Translate "asking AI" into "clicking buttons". Enterprise users' proficiency in "talking to AI" is far more uneven than imagined. Letting users construct prompts themselves is equal to shifting the engineering burden onto the people who should bear it the least. The mature practice is to encapsulate high-frequency scenarios into one-click actions: "generate last week's anomaly report," "verify this batch of invoices," "draft this client letter" — behind the buttons are complete prompts and workflows that have been evaluated and tuned. Conversational interactions are left for exploration, while button interactions are left for daily routines.

Method 4: Be a "Copilot" first, then talk about "Autopilot". In the face of high-risk, high-resistance processes, do not push automation all at once. Let the system exist as an "advisor" first — AI drafts, human confirms; AI annotates, human adjudicates. Through repeated confirmations, users establish a trust calibration with the system's judgment; when the confirmation pass rate is high enough, automation is then put on the agenda. John Deere's solution still retains the agronomist's final decision-making power; agent designs in financial compliance scenarios generally retain manual gatekeeping. The Copilot strategy is not conservative; it is the optimal path between activation rate and risk.

## 4.5 The Protracted War of Integration

Every FDE veteran has a body full of scars, all coming from the same war: the integration war with customers' "legacy systems" — those old systems that have been running in enterprises for years and no one dares to touch.

The cruelty of this war is beyond the imagination of anyone who hasn't stayed on the front lines. a16z's description hits the nail on the head: the context needed by AI applications — historical records, business logic, permission systems — are all locked within the enterprise's internal databases, APIs, and workflows, and the job of connecting them is "never a multiple-choice question, but a required course". Old mainframes in the financial industry, heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in the healthcare industry, dozens of siloed shop floor systems in the manufacturing industry — the commonalities of these environments are: outdated documentation, incomplete APIs, and half the people who understand them have already retired.

There are four strategic key points in this war.

- **Treat integration as a battle, not as chores**. In most project plans, integration work is written as a single line "System docking: 2 weeks", and then inflates to four months during execution. The root cause of the error is treating integration as technical chores — it is both technical archaeology and organizational politics, and it also has to manage data governance — it is an out-and-out tough battle. The correct posture: draw out the system map during entry due diligence, schedule integration risks by grading them, and gnaw the hardest bones earliest — integration has the biggest uncertainty; every day it is delayed is a day the entire project's timeline runs naked.

- **Second, solve data problems before model problems.** There is a widely cited insight in that report: the underlying cause of many AI project failures is "garbage in, garbage out" — AI connects to ungoverned data sources, hits ten random versions of the same document, and the output is naturally untrustworthy. Palantir's solution is ontology: first model the enterprise's data assets into a layer with business semantics, clarifying "which field is authoritative, which version is valid, who has the right to view what", and AI runs on this layer of foundation. The universal enlightenment of this path is: **Data governance is not the pre-requisite engineering of an AI project; it IS the AI project itself**.

The US Navy's "Shipbuilding Operating System" is the most spectacular footnote to this enlightenment. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Palantir's CEO jointly announced a $448 million contract: first covering two large shipyards, three naval docks, and a hundred suppliers. The data environment in the shipbuilding industry is a textbook-level disaster: ERP systems, decades-old databases, and paper blueprints coexist. But two numbers from the pilot phase made everyone shut up: at nuclear submarine manufacturer General Dynamics Electric Boat, **the submarine production scheduling plan was compressed from 160 man-hours to less than 10 minutes**; at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, **material review time was compressed from weeks to less than an hour**.

The Secretary of the Navy specifically emphasized: "This is not a concept, not a pilot, not a study — this is already being done." (See Appendix C for sources) Note the order in this case: only after exerting great effort to connect data into a unified semantic layer could the miracle of efficiency happen — not the other way around.

The same logic is replicated across three completely different industries. Fast-food chain Wendy's: scheduling for syrup stockouts across 6,450 stores in North America used to take 15 employees a whole day to look up, but after connecting to the data platform, a plan is produced in five minutes. Mortgage giant Fannie Mae: using AI to identify mortgage fraud, the detection rate exceeds 99%, far surpassing the original rule-based system. Citibank: customer credit approval compressed from hours to minutes — AI reads credit history, trading habits, industry risks, and associated enterprises in one breath. Three industries, one common premise: only by first organizing scattered data into a semantic layer that machines can understand can intelligence have a place to land. (See Appendix C for sources)

- **Third, fight the integration war of AI with AI.** An important variable after 2025: the integration work itself is beginning to be automated by AI. The scenario envisioned by a16z has partially become reality — for old systems without APIs, browser agents simulate humans to fetch data; field mapping, format conversion, and API documentation interpretation are largely handed over to models to process. The self-requirements of leading teams are worth quoting: "Automate the integration process to the greatest extent possible — process mining, data pipelines, system docking, API documentation grooming — this speed advantage will compound." Using AI to do the work of AI deployment, this might be the most wonderful part of this role.

- **Fourth, know when to bypass instead of conquer.** Not all legacy systems are worth head-on integration. The correct solution for some systems is "shadow reading" (read-only snapshots, nightly syncs), for some it is "manual ferrying" (retaining manual steps during the transition period), and for some it is simply "declaring isolation" (the data of this process is not within the scope of this project, clearly informing the customer). An engineer's pride always wants to conquer every fortress, but an FDE's judgment is reflected in choosing the battlefield — remember that your goal is not a total technical victory, but customer outcomes.

## 4.6 Change Management: Let the Customer Organization Stand Up for You

This section deals with the biggest soft resistance to activation: the organization. Among the five major roadblocks in the report, "employee resistance" and "change management" together account for nearly half. Going online with technology only requires engineers; behavior change requires the entire organization.

Change management in the context of FDE centers on managing three groups of people.

- **Sponsors | Your internal allies:** Behind every successful deployment stands an internal sponsor within the customer: they truly believe in this, and are willing to bet their reputation to pave the way for you. David Wakeling of the law firm Allen & Overy is a typical example — as the head of the firm's Markets Innovation Group, he was Harvey's counterpart, advocate, and umbrella within the firm. The key to managing sponsors: give them materials they can present (stories they can tell outside), give them battle merits (turning their vision into a highlight of their career), and give them a sense of security (when things fail, you stand in the front). One well-treated sponsor is better than ten product pitch meetings.

- **Influencers | Informal opinion leaders:** Every organization has a group of uncrowned kings: senior analysts, master workers in the workshop, the "just ask them" person in the department. They don't hold power, but they hold trust. Their one sentence "this thing actually works" is worth three all-staff emails from management; their one sentence "it's all flash and no substance" is enough to make the system die silently at the grassroots level. During the activation phase, you must deliberately manage this group of people: invite them to be the first batch of trial users, treat every one of their complaints seriously, and make the adoption of their suggestions explicit — "this field was added according to Master Wang's advice." Letting influencers become co-authors is the shortest path to cracking the "I won't use what I didn't make" mentality.

- **The Disadvantaged | Interest groups touched by the solution:** Automation inevitably redistributes work, and redistributing work inevitably creates the disadvantaged: approval roles whose sense of existence is compressed, departments whose information barriers are pierced, senior employees whose "unique crafts" are replaced. Ignore them, and they will become the system's most stubborn underground resistance — passively uncooperative, spreading stories of accidents, and voting against it during acceptance. Mature change management designs a "way out for the disadvantaged" in advance: redirecting freed-up manpower to higher-value work (and publicly promising no layoffs), transforming "gatekeepers" into "coaches" (using the experienced workers' expertise to train the system), letting the disadvantaged see their place in the future. This is both humane and purely utilitarian — the cost of resistance is far higher than the cost of pacification.

The partnership between ticketing platform Vivid Seats and Sierra demonstrates what "company-wide mobilization on the customer side" looks like during the activation phase. After deciding to introduce agents, the company's product, customer experience, and engineering teams all pushed in — "Once a decision was made, we were 100% committed, with everyone re-testing." Result: less than four weeks from kickoff to launch, self-service resolution rate for post-launch issues increased by 40%, and customer satisfaction increased by 35%.

But what's more valuable is the subsequent change: after routine problems were taken over by agents, the customer experience team shifted from "lining up to put out fires" to radically curing process issues, and even had spare capacity to do "surprise upgrade" fan-pampering projects; meanwhile, agent dialogue data began feeding back into the product — "If ten thousand people ask for the same feature in a month, we can immediately prioritize it. The best moment is: a product improvement brought by a pattern makes it so users simply don't need to ask for help anymore." (See Appendix C for sources) **The highest realm of activation is the system making itself unnecessary** — this case also previews Chapter 7's "The Frontline Feeding Back to the Product".

Corresponding samples in Chinese companies are also emerging. Ren Xiliang, head of efficiency engineering at e-commerce company Dewu, broke down the AI transformation of the tens of thousands-strong company into three steps: the first step is to reach a consensus with everyone, lower the tool barrier, and let everyone start using it first; the second step is to divide enterprise scenarios into four quadrants based on error tolerance space, prioritizing AI for scenarios with large error tolerance (like business analysis); the third step is to set up a dedicated knowledge operation group to dive into business teams and move experts' tacit experience from individual brains to spaces visible to AI. Consensus, scenarios, knowledge — the order cannot be reversed: first, have people willing to use it, then pick the right places to use it, and finally give AI the ingredients to cook with. (See Appendix C for sources)

The ultimate test standard for change management is only one: **when your team withdraws, is the system still being used?** If it's bustling while you're there and rapidly cools down after you withdraw, that's not activation, that's backup dancing. A truly activated organization will walk forward on its own.

## 4.7 I, Robot — The Automation of Delivery Work Itself

Delivering automation to customers is the product itself; automating your own delivery work is efficiency. This section talks about the latter — it is the first lever for FDE teams to break away from "revenue growing linearly with headcount".

In the daily work of FDEs, a staggering proportion of actions is templateable repetitive labor: deployment environment initialization for new customers, standard pipelines for data access, questionnaire responses for security reviews, pre-launch checklists, and periodic reporting formats to customers. Every action done for a second time should trigger a question: "Can this become a script, template, or checklist?"

The practices of leading teams have already crystallized into four types of assets.

- **Deployment Templates:** Packaging environment setup, permission configuration, and monitoring integration into one-click infrastructure as code. When a new customer comes in, a standardized environment can be spun up in the customer's cloud environment on day one, instead of spending a week configuring from scratch. Decagon can compress deployment for simple scenarios to 15 days, backed by a highly templated integration layer. (See Appendix C for sources)

- **Integration Component Library:** Connectors (pre-built system docking parts) for mainstream enterprise systems, write once, reuse everywhere. Palantir's ontology model essentially pushes this logic to the extreme: the product of data access is not a one-time pipeline, but reusable data assets with business meanings.

- **Checklist Culture:** Security review checklists, launch admission checklists, withdrawal handover checklists. Checklists are humanity's oldest tool against complexity, and they are also the cornerstone of the quality stability of FDE teams — they turn "relying on personal experience" into "relying on organizational memory".

- **Automated Reporting:** Weekly reports to customers and field intelligence back to the company should all be semi-automatically generated. The unit price of FDE time is too high to be spent on copy-pasting — and even less should "forgetting to report" cut off the loop between the frontline and the product that will be discussed in Chapter 7.

How substantial the compounding interest of automation is can be corroborated from one aspect: a16z listed "building or buying tools to automate service delivery" as a key recommendation for assembling a frontline deployment team, and judged this to be the key variable for this generation of AI companies to run faster and have lower ticket size barriers than the previous generation of enterprise software companies. Sierra's publicly reported fastest launch case is four weeks, and Palantir compressing the sales cycle from nine months to a few weeks — neither relies on engineers working overtime, but on turning yesterday's delivery into today's scaffolding.

This section also responds to the most common question — "Isn't FDE just a human wave strategy?" The essence of the human wave is not the number of people, but that everyone is doing non-reusable one-time labor. **When every delivery by an FDE team is paving the road for the next delivery, it is no longer a human wave, but a self-accelerating machine**.

The seven weapons of activating deployment have been covered: the speed of hotfixes, the direction of the evaluation system, the ingenuity of lowering barriers, the patience of integration wars, the soft skills of change management, and the leverage of automation. The system is alive, and the people are moving. But the cruelty of the enterprise business lies in: activation is only a lifeline extension, renewal is survival.
