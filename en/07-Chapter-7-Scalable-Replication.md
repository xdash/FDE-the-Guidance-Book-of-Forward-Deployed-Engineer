# Chapter 7: Scalable Replication

> "If you don't turn field learnings into product assets, all you get is a bunch of custom projects."
> —— Barry, former Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer

## 7.1 Leveraging Delivery Through Replicability

The highest state of internet growth is letting one user bring in more users—the growth engine shifts from external acquisition to internal generation. The question FDEs must answer is exactly the same: **For every customer completed, the next customer should be easier to do—not by adding headcount, but by accumulating the experience from the last time.**

This proposition is the "last mile" of this entire book, because it is the final line of defense between the FDE model and the consulting or outsourcing industries. If we remove the contents of this chapter from the strategies discussed in the previous six chapters, what is the outcome? An elite team capable of fighting hard battles takes on a customer, delivers to a customer, earns money to cover costs, and then takes on the next—revenue growth and headcount growth are completely linear. Congratulations, you have reinvented the consulting firm.

Barry described this boundary most ruthlessly in his memoir: "If you don't turn field learnings into product assets, all you get is a bunch of custom projects." McGrew's formulation comes from another angle: the hallmark of a viable FDE model is "the decreasing amount of customization for each subsequent customer"—if your customization work does not decrease as the number of customers grows, you are not doing FDE; you are in the man-day business.

The leverage of replicability has three levels, each more difficult than the last, and each more valuable than the last.

- **Level 1 Leverage | Knowledge Replication (Playbooks):** Documenting "how to do it"—playbook templates, checklists, training materials. It transforms the reliance on individual experience into a reliance on organizational memory: reducing the onboarding time for newcomers from a year to three months. This is the most basic leverage, and most teams stop here.

- **Level 2 Leverage | Component Replication (Tools and Code):** Turning "what has been done" into "what can be directly used"—integration connectors, evaluation frameworks, deployment templates, industry data models. The starting point for the next team is no longer zero, but standing on the shoulders of the previous project. The automation assets discussed in Section 4.7 belong to this level.

- **Level 3 Leverage | Product Replication (Platform Capabilities):** Abstracting "recurring customizations" into standard platform capabilities—from then on, this requirement no longer needs any engineers on-site. Palantir's Ontology, Decagon's agent workflow configuration, and Sierra's agent platform are all products of level 3 leverage. This level of leverage changes the cost structure of the entire business: **the first two levels reduce delivery costs; the third level eliminates delivery costs**.

The progression through these three levels of leverage is the complete version of McGrew's phrase "gravel roads and highways": FDEs build gravel roads (solving immediate problems), component teams lay crushed stone foundations (making it easier for the next vehicle), and platform teams pour highways (so everyone can pass through from then on). The remaining seven sections of this chapter will discuss this complete engineering process from building roads to paving them.

## 7.2 Bad News Travels Fast—The Dissemination of Post-Mortems for Failed Deployments

Turning accidents into assets is a rare organizational capability. An FDE organization's attitude towards failure determines whether it can possess replication capabilities: **Failure is the raw material for replication; an organization that covers up failure is equivalent to burying a gold mine like garbage.**

Let's make one thing clear first: in the FDE model, failure is not an accident; it is inevitable. Barry's recollections are merciless: "There were plenty of failures—vast amounts of time, money, and travel expenses burned spectacularly." Palantir simply formalized the acceptance of failure into its system: pilots are treated like venture capital investments; most will die, and those that die must be reviewed (post-mortemed). He specifically reminded: for those crashed projects, teams that worked for nothing, and exhausted engineers, we must also say thank you—the lessons were bought and paid for by them. This is the hardest part to learn, because an engineer's nature is to celebrate success and hide failure.

Translating failure into organizational assets requires three mechanisms.

- **Blameless Post-Mortems:** The only rule for post-mortem meetings: focus on the issue, not the person. Once post-mortems are linked to performance, people will start to embellish failures—and embellished failures have zero instructional value. Blamelessness does not mean a lack of accountability, but redefining "accountability" as "whether the lessons were extracted honestly and completely." The contribution of an honest post-mortem by the leader of a failed project to the organization may be greater than that of a lucky success.

- **Structuring Lessons Learned:** Post-mortem outputs cannot be "pay attention next time" sighs; they must be retrievable, actionable knowledge: What is the high-risk signal for this customer type? Which integration assumption was overturned? Which evaluation metric was designed incorrectly? These conclusions should enter the "minefield" section of the playbook and the due diligence checklist of new projects—so that the next team's "entry due diligence" automatically carries historical lessons. **Failures are only truly absorbed when they are written into processes.**

- **Moderate Externalization of Failures:** This layer is the most counter-intuitive: sharing failure lessons externally (after desensitization) is a highly efficient trust marketing tactic. As discussed in Section 3.5, Barry's article "exposing family scandals" became the best evangelism for the Palantir model—readers can distinguish who is giving a booth pitch and who is telling the truth from the trenches. A team that dares to publicly dissect its failures sends the signal that "we have already paid the tuition for this industry's detours"—which is exactly what enterprise customers want to hear most.

## 7.3 Riding the Momentum: Standing at the Industry's Tipping Point

Leveraging the attention from external events for your own use is a classic marketing technique. Teams in the FDE track are currently standing at an epic tipping point (wind outlet), and the window for this tipping point will not remain open forever.

The industry tipping point from 2025 to 2026 is a confluence of several currents: that report made the "95% failure rate" the headache of every enterprise CIO—the customer's headache is your opportunity; OpenAI and Anthropic established deployment companies on the same day, pushing "deployment" into financial headlines—the money giants spend educating the market benefits the entire industry; the 800% hiring growth for FDE roles makes this position a frequent guest in tech media—even the question of "whether to hire an FDE" has become a form of corporate anxiety. These three currents together create a rare situation: **customers are actively looking for you to discuss what you want to sell**.

The first action in riding this momentum is "defining the agenda." Attention at the tipping point is a public resource; whoever's content defines the agenda harvests the attention. a16z set the tone for the entire track with an industry manifesto; communities like OpenFDE aggregate definition power while aggregating practitioners. For specific companies, the best way to seize the power to define the agenda is to publish their closely guarded secrets: playbooks, failure post-mortems, value data—exclusive first-hand insights are the only currency for the power to define agendas.

The second action is "aligning with the industry agenda." The biggest driver for enterprise AI procurement is upgrading from "efficiency" to "survival"—CEOs are being grilled at board meetings with "What is our AI strategy?", and this anxiety will translate into budgets. Translate your solutions into "boardroom language": not "we can help you optimize customer service processes," but "we can enable you to present quantifiable AI scorecards at the next board meeting." The same delivery, anchored at different agenda levels, reaches different budget pools.

The third action is "building assets during the tipping point, not consuming the tipping point." The wind will stop. The discussions sparked by reports will cool down, and media buzzwords will become outdated. The most precious thing during a tipping point is not signing a few more contracts, but converting the attention brought by the wind into long-term assets: lighthouse cases, the right to speak on industry methodologies, and replicable delivery systems. **When the wind passes and the tide recedes, the naked swimmers will be exposed, while those with assets will land ashore.**

## 7.4 Building a Customer External Dissemination Loop

Propagation that relies on word of mouth rather than advertising mainly occurs through three circles in the FDE industry.

Circle 1: Practitioner Communities. FDE is a professional community in the making—in 2025 it was still scattered within various companies, but by 2026 there were dedicated communities like OpenFDE, dedicated compensation reports, and dedicated interview guides. The formation of a professional community is a godsend for pioneers: contribute content (methodologies, tools, data) to the community, and your name will be bound to this emerging profession. The transmission path of concepts has always been this way: early evangelists eventually become synonymous with the concept itself.

Circle 2: Customers' Industry Communities. Every industry has its own small circles—forums for bankers, annual meetings for law firm partners, summits for hospital administrators. The trust dividends discussed in Section 3.3 mainly flow within these closed circles. The ticket to entering these circles is not advertising, but customer champions who are invited to speak—your job is to give them something worth talking about and something they can talk about well. A ten-minute presentation by a customer on your behalf at their industry summit beats a hundred days at a booth in an industry exhibition.

Circle 3: Ecological Partner Networks. Section 3.7 discussed the customer acquisition value of ecological bundling; here we emphasize its propagation attribute: cloud vendor solution architects, consulting firm consultants, and system integrator project managers are people who move between different customer sites every day—their list of "reliable teams" is the fastest and most accurate promotional channel. The key to managing this circle is to let them "win": let ecological partners gain performance, cases, and reputation in their collaboration with you. **Those who help others succeed will be remembered by the entire network.**

The flywheel combining these three circles is: delivery creates stories, stories propagate within the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, and new opportunities create new stories. The difference between this flywheel and traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "delivered value"—the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is making every bit of past effort pull in customers for the future.

## 7.5 Built-In Virality Factors in Products

Good products propagate themselves—a line like "Sent from my..." in an email signature was the earliest design for self-propagation. The systems delivered by FDEs can also embed "built-in diffusion mechanisms," allowing the system itself to become a salesperson for expansion.

Factor 1: Visibility Design. The outputs of the system will naturally be circulated—weekly reports, analysis results, approval workflows. Make every output carry its "origin": a "Generated by XYZ System" in the footer of a report, or a processing portal link in an anomaly alert. When a beautiful analysis report is forwarded to the director of the neighboring department, the person who sees it will naturally ask, "What was this made with?" The essence of visibility design is letting the system's value exhibit itself in daily workflows, rather than relying on dedicated reporting.

Factor 2: Collaborative Onboarding. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: cases annotated by investigators need supervisor review, analysts' reports need business confirmation—every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. New users brought in by collaboration are not "promoted to," they are "brought in by the work itself," leading to a completely different level of acceptance.

Factor 3: Self-Service Exploration Layer. Outside the core delivery, leave a low-risk practice area (sandbox): customer employees can try "letting AI do an analysis for me" in there. Usage patterns that grow out of the sandbox are often the most vital expansion clues—a scenario played out by a department employee themselves has deeper roots than a scenario pushed by sales ten times. **Let the whole company help you discover, and let the FDE tackle the hardest parts.**

Two new products in 2026 made this factor plain to see. Sierra launched "Ghostwriter" in March: customers upload their standard operating procedures, historical chat logs, or even a photo of a whiteboard, or describe a goal in plain language, and the system automatically generates a production-ready agent covering voice, chat, and email channels in over 30 languages—for the first time, customer business personnel can "build Agents self-service," and the forward deployed team shifted from "building each one by hand" to "acceptance and fallbacks." Decagon's agent operating procedures walked this path even earlier: customer service supervisors write procedures in natural language ("For returns over 30 days, check membership level first; if it meets the policy, process it"), without waiting for engineers to schedule it—reportedly, Chime achieved a 70% self-service resolution rate, Duolingo's issue deflection rate reached 80%, and fitness platform ClassPass's customer service costs dropped by 95%. (Sources in Appendix C) **When customers start building themselves, your role shifts from a construction crew to a design institute**—this is the ultimate form of Factor 3.

Factor 4: Cross-Boundary Value Evidence. Automatically generating a "Quarterly Value Report" every quarter allows it to naturally flow to the customer's executive level. This report is both the renewal engineering of Chapter 5 and a stepping stone for vertical expansion—every number the executives see lays the groundwork for "where else is this system worth using."

The common principle of built-in factors is: **If you want others to help you spread the word, you first have to let them get real value out of it.** Any design meant just for the sake of propagation will be identified as "vendor gimmicks" in enterprise scenarios and will be met with aversion. There is no viral marketing in the enterprise market, only "chain reactions of word-of-mouth"—slow, but every step is solid.

## 7.6 Navigating Organizational Politics During Scaling

The biggest obstacle to scaling is never technology, but people's territories.

When a system expands from one department to ten, you are actually rewriting the internal power map of the organization: information monopolies are broken (data exclusively held by one department is now visible company-wide), approval authority is compressed (things AI replies to in seconds no longer need three days of layered signatures), and professional barriers are leveled (the "exclusive experience" of senior employees is encoded into the system). Every change creates silent enemies.

To handle organizational politics, FDE veterans have crystallized three core principles.

- **Principle 1: Expansion Must Include "Locals".** Every time you enter a new department, first find the "local boss" in that department—the most prestigious senior employee—and invite them to participate in the adaptation process: "How is this scenario different for your department?" Their answers will make the solution more accurate, and their participation will ensure the solution passes unimpeded in their territory. **People do not oppose what they helped build**—this is the most reliable leverage in organizational behavior.

Harvey's expansion in top law firms is a classic textbook case of reading organizational politics. There are three tiers of people simultaneously existing within the firm: Partners—those who pay, but don't do the hands-on work; Paralegals/Associates—those who do the work, but don't sign off; Knowledge Management Lawyers—those who don't belong to either side but see the blind spots of both. Generic questionnaire surveys compress these three tiers into the same set of dropdown options, resulting in distorted average scores; Harvey's forward deployed team, however, designed separate interviews and demos for the three tiers: showing partners "how much money this business makes," showing associates "saving three days of overtime this week," and showing knowledge management lawyers "the entire firm's tacit experience finally has a place to accumulate." A deployment must simultaneously make all three tiers feel like beneficiaries—missing any single tier will result in the system being quietly shelved at the corresponding step. (Sources in Appendix C)

- **Principle 2: Ensure Every Step of Expansion Has "Beneficiaries".** Before expanding, calculate clearly what each relevant department gets: a department whose data barriers were pierced gets global visibility and analysis capabilities; a department whose approval authority was compressed gets the right to focus on exceptions (AI handles the routine, humans focus on the difficult). Expansion without beneficiaries is an invasion; expansion with beneficiaries is liberation—the only difference lies in whether you have thought through and clearly articulated "what they get."

- **Principle 3: Always Leave Dignity for the "Old Order".** The processes replaced by the system were once designed by someone capable; the encoded experience was once the life's blood of an expert. In promotional pitches, respecting the old order is respecting the defenders of the old order: "This process supported the company for the past ten years; now we are going to amplify its ten years of experience a hundredfold." A well-treated old world will not make trouble in the new world.

## 7.7 Solidifying Replication Efficiency with Playbooks

At the beginning of this chapter, playbooks were listed as level 1 leverage. This section expands on their engineering details—because the playbooks of most teams are written as "write-and-die" document graveyards.

- **The First Attribute of a Playbook: Living in the Workflow, Not Lying in a Knowledge Base.** A playbook that can only be found in a document system might as well not exist. The effective approach is to embed it into the workflow: during the due diligence phase of a new project, the system automatically pops up the due diligence checklist for that customer type; during the deployment phase, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; during the post-mortem phase, updating the playbook is a mandatory output. **Knowledge that is not embedded in the workflow is merely archaeological material.**

- **The Second Attribute of a Playbook: Organized by "Scenario" Rather Than "Function".** Failed playbooks are written according to the company's organizational structure ("Delivery Process," "Technical Specifications"); successful playbooks are written according to customer scenarios ("Financial Anti-Money Laundering Scenario Playbook," "Manufacturing Scheduling Scenario Playbook," "Law Firm Knowledge Base Scenario Playbook"). Each scenario playbook contains a fixed set of seven items: typical pain points and fit-check essentials, due diligence checklist (high-risk signals specific to this scenario), known minefields of data and integration, metric templates for evaluation and acceptance, a role map for change management, reference structures for pricing and scaling, and a lesson library from historical post-mortems. Harvey's ability to replicate its playbook in the law firm scenario to PwC and Cleary Gottlieb relied precisely on turning the experience of the first customer (Allen & Overy) into inheritable scenario assets.

- **The Third Attribute of a Playbook: It Must be "Alive"—Having Owners, Versions, and Depreciation.** Assign an owner to each scenario playbook (usually the most senior delivery team). Require a mandatory review and update after each project ends, and perform a comprehensive overhaul every six months—deleting the outdated and merging the duplicate. A playbook without an owner will rot into a trap that misleads newcomers within a year, which is worse than having no playbook at all.

The relationship between playbooks, Level 2 Leverage (Component Libraries), and Level 3 Leverage (Platforms) is: playbooks record "judgments," component libraries accumulate "craftsmanship," and platforms solidify "capabilities." Only when all three are complete can a newly assembled FDE squad reach 80% of a veteran team's combat effectiveness within two weeks—this is the true meaning of replication.

## 7.8 The Planning and Polishing of Productization

The final section discusses the crowning achievement of Level 3 Leverage: crystallizing field experience into products—this is the alchemy of the entire FDE model.

Let's first look at the most spectacular success story: Palantir's Foundry. Today, this platform with billions of dollars in annual revenue, supporting hundreds of billions in the company's market value, had its core components born in customer sites far and wide—Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, Baku. The tools built independently by forward deployed engineers in various locations to solve local problems went through years of natural selection and platform assimilation, eventually growing into a unified product. Barry recalls this process as "completely bottom-up": in 2014, the company's product strategy literally was "strong opinions, loosely held," letting a hundred flowers bloom in the field, and incorporating the survivors into the core.

This process was not a pastoral idyll; it had rigorous internal logic, which can be distilled into the "Four Questions of Productization."

- **Question 1:** Is this field solution a "particularity" or a "commonality"? The judgment criterion is: has the same problem appeared independently at more than three customers? The identical pain point of three customers is a product signal; the special request of a single customer might just be a quirk. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let the "repeated reinvention of the wheel by multiple forward deployed teams" naturally expose commonalities—when three teams independently build similar things, the platform team knows: the time for assimilation has arrived.

- **Question 2:** Is the cost of generalization less than the benefit? McGrew warned: field code is "fast and dirty," and the cost of generalizing it "is often higher than writing the first version." Productization decisions must calculate this equation: the investment in generalization versus the customization costs saved across several future customers. If the math doesn't work out, leave it at the component layer and don't force it into the platform—every capability in the platform is a lifelong maintenance commitment.

- **Question 3:** Can it be used by "people who don't write code"? The users of field tools are engineers; the users of platform capabilities are everyone. The hardest step in productization is often not technical but interactive: turning an engineer's script into a feature that business personnel can configure. Decagon's agent workflow configuration (letting operations use natural language to define workflows) is the template for this step—if the abstraction layer is chosen correctly, the user base of the capability expands a hundredfold.

- **Question 4:** After assimilation, is there still room left for the field? This is the most delicate balance: if the platform assimilates too much, the field team degenerates into configurators, and the soul of the FDE model (field creativity) dies; if it assimilates too little, the leverage of scaling cannot be realized. Palantir's solution is to maintain "radical authorization of the field by the base"—top management only sets goals; the tactics are up to the field. **The platform is responsible for making "common things easy," while the field is responsible for making "impossible things happen."**

The most powerful rebuttal from those who doubt this flywheel will always be gross margin: if you send people to the site, how can gross margins possibly be high? History has given three answers. When ServiceNow went public, its gross margin was 63.2%, and it was dismissed as "too much like a services company"—ten years later, it is 79%, with a market cap of nearly $200 billion; Workday was at 54.1% when it went public—now it's 76%; Palantir was at 79% when it went public, and reached 82% in 2025, outperforming the vast majority of "pure software" companies.

The summary by the venture capital firm Insight Partners hits the nail on the head: **These companies that heavily invested in implementation and services during the platform transition period are exactly the ones that persevered when everyone else ran in the opposite direction.** The secret is that gross margins can climb year over year—as the platform's accumulated capabilities thicken, more and more implementation work can be done by the customers themselves (the boot camp model allows a massive amount of integration work to be completed on the customer side), and service costs are continuously diluted. Ugly first, beautiful later; heavy first, light later. (Sources in Appendix C)

At the end of this chapter, let's tell a story of "betting on the right direction." At the end of 2022, ChatGPT came out of nowhere, and everyone was guessing what Palantir would do. Sankar made a bet that would later be repeatedly quoted: large models will rapidly commoditize, and value will cluster at the two ends—compute chips on one end, and the ontology (the semantic layer that allows models to safely work on enterprise data). He bet more than half of the company's engineers on the AI platform AIP. The result: Palantir's stock price surged from single digits when he took over as CTO to around $200; U.S. commercial revenue saw triple-digit growth for multiple consecutive quarters. The logic of this bet and Chapter 1 of this book are two sides of the same coin: **The stronger and cheaper the models get, the more valuable the engineering required to "connect the model to reality" becomes.** (Sources in Appendix C)

The culmination of the Four Questions of Productization is a convergence of the entire FDE business model: **The field is the probe, the platform is the lever, and the product is the carrier of compound interest.** The more real the world detected by the probe, the more solid the capabilities accumulated by the platform; the more solid the platform, the further the probe can reach. Once this flywheel starts turning, you are no longer a company that "sends people to do work," but a company that "continuously transforms the complexity of the world into software assets"—that is the species with the highest profit margins and the deepest moats since the birth of the software industry.
