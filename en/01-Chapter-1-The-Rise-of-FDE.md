# Chapter 1 The Rise of FDE

> "The biggest challenge in building software for spies is: I don't know any spies."
> —— Bob McGrew, early executive at Palantir, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI

## 1.1 First, a Story of Nine Million Dead Dollars

The beginning of the story is always the same. In the conference room of a large enterprise, the vendor's presentation has just ended. The large model answers fluently, the data dashboards are dazzling, and even the most critical executives can't find a flaw. The CEO makes an immediate decision: sign. The contract is worth millions of dollars, hands are shaken, photos are taken, and a press release is issued.

Nine months later, the project is dead.

Not dead with a bang, but quietly. The system is still running, the servers are still on, but no business unit is actually using it. The vendor delivered every feature in the contract, and the enterprise paid every penny of it. The only thing that didn't arrive was "value."

If you think this is just bad luck, MIT's famous 2025 report will tell you: this is the norm. They interviewed 52 organizations, collected 153 executive questionnaires, and reviewed over 300 public enterprise AI projects. The conclusion was a single sentence: out of the tens of billions of dollars burned, **95% produced no measurable financial return**.

95%. In other words, in the realm of enterprise AI implementation, the main theme is not success, but systemic failure.

What's even more interesting is how they failed. The report explicitly noted: the problem wasn't the models. Those models that amazed everyone in the demo were still smart when deployed to production, but they "don't remember feedback, don't store context, and don't integrate into workflows"—they looked like products but operated like exhibits. Stanford University's broader market data from the same year corroborated this: 88% of organizations were using AI, but the percentage of agent applications that actually made it into production was only in the single digits. McKinsey added the final blow: only 6% of enterprises could claim that AI contributed more than 5% to their profits.

Around the time the report was published, a manufacturing COO's complaint circulated in the industry: "The internet says everything has changed, but back on our shop floor, nothing has moved." (Source in Appendix C). These words cut deeper than any data—his company didn't lack budget or tools; they lacked someone to integrate the tools into the actual workflows of the shop floor.

During the same period, the report's lead author shared a control group story. Some 19- or 20-year-old youngsters running startups achieved $20 million in revenue in a year using generative AI. Their approach was the exact opposite of established enterprises: they picked just one pain point, drilled through it, and tightly bound themselves to the customers who would actually use their products. Older companies always want to eat the whole elephant at once; the youngsters only take a small bite, chew through it, and then take the next. (Source in Appendix C)

Remember this contrast. It is practically a trailer for Chapter 2 of this book.

Actually, this gap wasn't newly dug in the AI era. China's enterprise software industry has been lying in this gap for over a decade: large companies demand customization, vendors lose money on every deal, code is delivered and then abandoned, and ultimately they all degrade into "outsourced companies for the client." One practitioner put it harshly: customization is the natural enemy of SaaS; this curse can only dissipate itself when the market matures. The situation in the US is a bit more dignified, but the script is similar: sales signs the deal, implementation moves in, half a year later a "fully-featured but unloved" system is delivered, followed by endless bickering.

Ultimately, it's the same wall: **the place where software is built and the place where value is created are not the same place**. On this side of the wall, requirements are translated through layers of tickets, minutes, and weekly reports, losing a layer of blood with each translation; on the other side, the customer's real workflow is hidden in spreadsheets nobody documents, in word-of-mouth conventions, and in the tacit knowledge of "you have to ask old Wang about this." The software industry invented countless ladders to climb the wall—PRDs (Product Requirement Documents), user research, implementation methodologies, customer success systems—but the wall remains.

Until one company decided: no more climbing the wall, let's send people over.

## 1.2 Palantir's Victory

In 2003, Silicon Valley had just crawled out of the ruins of the dot-com bubble. Peter Thiel and a few young Stanford alumni founded a company named after a "Lord of the Rings" reference—Palantir, the seeing stone that can look far away. What they wanted to do sounded like science fiction: build data analysis software for US intelligence agencies, connecting the fragments scattered across countless classified databases into a cohesive picture to help analysts catch terrorists.

This business had a premise that would make any product manager instantly break down. Years later, early executive Bob McGrew brought this history to life on a podcast:

> "Our goal when we started was to build software for the intelligence community, which basically meant building software for spies. And one challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either. Even if you happen to find one and ask, 'How do you actually work?', they usually won't tell you."

No user interviews, no requirement documents, no usability testing. The first lesson of internet startup methodology was entirely voided here. The solution devised by co-founder Stephen Cohen was endearingly clumsy: build a demo first, show it to folks at intelligence agencies, and ask what they think. They were blunt: "This is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat, but asked: "Then how would you like it to be different?" He then pulled out a notebook, wrote down their points one by one, went back to change it, and brought it back.

This clumsy loop was the embryo of the FDE. Hidden within were two intuitions later proven to be worth their weight in gold: first, customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something that works; second, the fastest way to know what customers want is to put the people who build things right next to the people who use them.

The person who upgraded these intuitions into a corporate strategy was employee number 13, Shyam Sankar. As Palantir moved from its first customer to its second and third, the team discovered a counterintuitive fact: what each customer wanted had subtle but critical differences. The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a generic product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's customers were the CIA, the FBI, and the US military on the battlefield—saying no meant getting kicked out. Sankar did the opposite: build a flexible, customizable platform, then deploy engineers on-site at the customer's location to finish the last mile.

His most critical move was changing the accounting for this. In the software industry's ledger, "customizing for a single customer" is called services, the enemy of profit margins. Sankar flipped it: **on-site customization is not a cost; it is product discovery**. Every pitfall engineers encountered on-site was a signpost for the platform's next evolution.

Sankar himself was the first forward deployed engineer, and his earliest on-site experience serves as the archetype for the role. Around 2007, the biggest cause of US military casualties in Iraq was IEDs (Improvised Explosive Devices). A cross-agency operations center set up for this allowed Sankar to bring a small team and a "still rough" product to co-locate in a SCIF (Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility) for two weeks. A SCIF is a physically isolated, classified space where even speakerphones are banned. Sankar came up with a wild solution: he strapped the phone to his head with an elastic band to free his hands for coding—listening to analyst feedback in one ear, and talking to colleagues at Silicon Valley headquarters in the other. For two weeks, he worked 19 hours a day: demoing, connecting data, taking feedback, and changing it on the spot. By the end, the analysts said: this is useful. Sankar himself, exhausted, called CEO Alex Karp: "This isn't sustainable, we're done." Karp's answer later became company culture: make this "unsustainability" institutional. (Source in Appendix C)

Years later, a colleague recalled that Sankar scolded people without emotion. Once, he and his colleague Mabry were happily chatting at an airport cafe when Mabry suddenly received a sternly worded criticism email from him—written and sent right there while sitting across the table. "There was no personal attack in it, just an underlying message: To win, I owe you this truth." (Source in Appendix C)

This playbook soon drew blood on the battlefield. Palantir engineers deployed on-site found that soldiers didn't need fancy intelligence charts at all; they just needed a small tool to mark "this road is suspicious" on a map—IEDs were the biggest killer of patrols. The engineers cobbled together a simple map tool on the spot; soldiers could click to mark dangerous segments, visible to the whole team in real-time. This tool saved lives and later solidified into a standard platform feature. Note: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room; it could only be born in the moment when an engineer and a soldier looked at the same road together.

On the commercial side, they had to die once first. Palantir's first enterprise-facing product was called Metropolis; the market response was dismal, with only a few financial companies barely using it. Their second attempt, Foundry, finally gained traction, with the turning point at Airbus. In the Toulouse factory, a fuel pump fault on the A380 kept recurring, and Airbus's own engineers had investigated for two years without a clue. Palantir's people moved in, connected the sensor data into the platform, and solved the case in two weeks—the fuel was sloshing away from the pump body during climb. A trivial fix saved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars. Airbus's digital chief later publicly sighed: "For the same problem, we would have taken 24 months to investigate before." (Source in Appendix C). Airbus has since become Palantir's most hardcore supporter in Europe, building its entire Skywise data platform on top of it, connecting over 10,000 aircraft and 50,000 users.

There is also a piece of gossip worth noting. Rumor has it that Palantir's software was involved in the operation that killed Osama bin Laden in 2011. This claim has never been confirmed, nor falsified—journalists writing profiles on Palantir deliberately left this ambiguous footnote. But true or false, the rumor itself is Palantir's best sales weapon. (Source in Appendix C)

Before 2016, the number of Forward Deployed Engineers at Palantir once exceeded that of traditional software engineers. A software company where over half of the engineers weren't at headquarters building products, but scattered globally at customer sites. For years, Wall Street couldn't understand it, dismissing it as "human wave tactics" and "more like a consulting firm."

Then, time gave the answer. In 2023, they launched their AI platform, AIP, paired with a playbook called "Bootcamps" (detailed in Chapter 8), compressing the enterprise software sales cycle from 9-12 months down to a few weeks. In Q4 2025, their "Rule of 40"—revenue growth rate plus profit margin, a health metric for the software industry where 40 is a passing grade—hit 127%; in Q1 2026, 145%. Single-quarter contract signings were $4.26 billion, net revenue retention was 139%, and they had $7.2 billion in cash. Karp simply said on the earnings call: "We are a species unto ourselves." The market cap once surged past $400 billion. (Source in Appendix C)

Those who mocked its human wave tactics back then were left speechless. Palantir took twenty years to prove one thing: ladders can't climb that wall, but people can. The group of people climbing the wall got an official name—Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE).

## 1.3 What is an FDE

### A One-Sentence Definition

The definition adopted in this book comes from Bob McGrew, the best expositor of this model—he was an early engineer at PayPal, later an early executive at Palantir, and then Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, where ChatGPT and GPT-4 came from teams he led:

> **A Forward Deployed Engineer is an engineer stationed at the customer site, bridging the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."**

In this plain statement, every word matters.

"Stationed at the site" means your working context is embedded within the customer's: joining their chat groups, reading their data, attending their meetings, getting to know the person who "knows why the process is this way"—not necessarily sitting in the customer's office every day. "The gap" is the reason this role exists: where the product works out of the box, you are not needed; the deeper the gap, the more you are needed—intelligence, finance, manufacturing, healthcare, law. "Engineer" is the most crucial qualifier: you write production code, not reports. Palantir deliberately kept the words "software engineer" in the title to emphasize to the world: this is not a consulting role. As for "Forward Deployed," it's a military term referring to troops deployed to the front lines—putting the most combat-capable people closest to the problem.

### What It Is Not

To understand a new role, the process of elimination is fastest.

It is not pre-sales. Pre-sales work ends before signing; the goal is to win the deal, the output is slides; FDE work enters deep waters after signing; the goal is to win results, the output is a system running in production. Pre-sales is responsible for making the customer believe "this can work"; FDE is responsible for making it actually work.

It is not on-site outsourcing either—this distinction is especially crucial for Chinese readers, as "on-site engineers" have a very long, and quite unsavory, history in China. The first batch of domestic service providers flying the FDE banner drew the line with outsourcing in three sentences on their websites: Outsourcing charges by the hour, FDE delivers by milestones and accepts results; outsourcing codes from scratch, FDE brings a product foundation to engineer; outsourcing stays longer and the system stops when people leave, FDE leaves when finished, leaving capabilities in the system and the customer's team. In short, outsourcing sells headcounts, FDE sells results.

Jove, head of FDE at Silicon Valley AI customer service company Cresta, detailed this boundary in a video chat. His team is expanding from 30 to 100 people this year, and his assessment is: **FDE only makes sense when tied to an AI platform**—if it's just doing traditional data integration and system building, it's hard to distinguish from a traditional implementation engineer or outsourcer. He also has a strict hiring rule: in the Agent era, if you can't code, you're illiterate. Another notable mechanism is dual responsibility: an FDE must not only make the deployment successful but also carry the metric of "making the product more mature"—what is learned on-site must feed back into the platform. (Source in Appendix C)

It is not a consultant. Consultants deliver advice by project and are not responsible for execution; an FDE is responsible for the ultimate operation of the system, and the endpoint is "the customer team can use it independently." Anthropic's collaboration with fintech company FIS is a prime example: engineers embedded with FIS to co-build an anti-money laundering agent, reducing investigations from hours to minutes, but the stated goal of the partnership wasn't to hand over a system, but to "transfer knowledge so FIS can build agents themselves later." A consultant hopes you will always need them; an FDE hopes you eventually won't.

It is not a traditional product engineer. Product engineers face abstract users—personas (what users look like), funnels (how many go from visit to pay), DAUs (Daily Active Users); FDEs face specific customers—a bank's risk control department, a farm in Iowa, a patrol outside Baghdad. Inside Palantir, there is a famous role division: platform engineers are responsible for "one capability serving many customers," while forward deployed engineers (internal codename "Delta") are responsible for "one customer, mobilizing many capabilities." A platform engineer strives for a feature that can be used everywhere; an FDE strives to completely solve the problem of the specific customer in front of them first.

### How Did This Trend Start

Why did a role invented in 2003 become top-tier only in 2025?

The most direct catalyst is generative AI. Large models created an unprecedented disparity: anyone can make a stunning demo in five minutes, but connecting that demo to a real enterprise's data, permissions, compliance, and workflows is an order of magnitude harder. Model companies gradually figured out: **the next decisive factor is not model quality, but deployment capability**.

Numbers outline the steepness of the curve: in the first nine months of 2025, FDE job postings surged eightfold; on the YC job board, over a hundred startups listed this position that barely existed three years prior. Venture capital firm a16z straightforwardly called it "tech's hottest job" and provided a vivid analogy: an enterprise buying AI is like your grandma getting an iPhone—she wants to use it, but needs you to set it up for her.

A series of reports by the Financial Times in November 2025 serves as the best cross-section of this trend. Fournier, OpenAI's head of forward deployment in Europe, said the team was formed just a year ago and was about to expand to 50 people, with "demand exceeding our expectations"; DeJong, head of applied AI at Anthropic, put it more amusingly: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are completely different species"—so her team expanded fivefold in a year. Palantir UK head Pretejohn condensed the company credo into one sentence: "Software is only valuable if it actually means something to the end customer." Even Cohere CEO Gomez, a model company, came out to endorse it: "We embed engineers right at the start of the contract and pull back once the customer is running smoothly." (Source in Appendix C)

The intensity of the talent war has three hard indicators: OpenAI's forward deployment team started with 2 people and grew to 52 in a year; Salesforce, which wrote "we don't do implementation ourselves" into its textbooks, publicly committed to hiring 1,000 FDEs; even Deloitte, which makes a living selling consulting, set up a dedicated FDE business line in December 2025. (Source in Appendix C)

Then came the giants voting with their feet. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the formation of a "deployment company": self-controlled, joined by 19 top-tier capital firms like TPG, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, with initial investment exceeding $4 billion, a reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion, and casually acquired a consulting firm with 150 deployment engineers. Hours later, Anthropic was exposed to be forming a counter-entity with Blackstone Group. The two largest model companies, on the same day, elevated "deployment" from a cost center to a strategic asset—the capital markets cast their vote for FDEs in the most expensive way possible.

The deepest reason, however, was seen clearly by McGrew: "AI agents are a category with no incumbents, so there is a massive amount of product discovery to do." What CRM software should look like had a standard answer twenty years ago; what an agent should look like, nobody knows, not even the customer. The answer can only be found on-site at the customer's.

History has drawn a circle here: in 2003, Palantir invented the FDE because they "didn't know how spies worked"; in 2025, the whole industry collectively embraced the FDE because they "don't know how agents in enterprises should work." Twenty-two years later, the same answer.

## 1.4 Responsibilities and Traits of an FDE

### A Resume Born for the Role

If you want to answer "What kind of profession is an FDE," McGrew's resume is practically the textbook answer.

His first job was at PayPal as an early engineer. That group was later known as the "PayPal Mafia," profoundly shaping all of Silicon Valley. After leaving, he joined Palantir in its startup phase, working his way up to executive, and witnessing the whole process of FDE turning from an emergency measure into corporate strategy. When managing the product and engineering teams, he made a famous analogy: Forward Deployed Engineers build "gravel roads" to value at customer sites, and the product team is responsible for judging which gravel roads are worth widening and paving to become "highways" serving the next ten customers.

Later, he became Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, leading the development of ChatGPT, GPT-4, and the o1 reasoning models. In other words, this is someone who built the platform on this side of the wall, climbed over to the field on the other side, and finally built the very technology that made the wall unprecedentedly high.

An interesting scene occurred at a 2025 YC AI conference. McGrew thought founders would crowd him to ask "How did you invent ChatGPT," but the question everyone chased him down for was the same: **How exactly does Palantir's FDE model work?** A person who invented ChatGPT was asked most about deployment methodology. This detail is the best footnote for the shifting winds of the era.

### Three Layers of Traits

Synthesizing over twenty job postings from various companies and testimonies from practitioners, the traits of an FDE can be categorized into three layers.

First layer: Broad enough technical generalists. An FDE doesn't need to be the deepest expert in a single field, but they must be able to independently solve full-stack problems on-site: they can write code, call APIs, understand data pipelines, deploy to the cloud, grasp the temperaments of large models, and must understand enterprise environment "utilities"—Single Sign-On (login once, access everywhere), permissions, and compliance certifications. The job market has a clear price tag for this combination: a 2026 compensation report showed that mid-level FDEs at top AI labs had a median total compensation of around $385,000, senior levels around $610,000, and principals over a million—higher than most pure R&D roles at the same level, because the market knows how scarce such people are. (Source in Appendix C)

Second layer: The ability to translate technology into business results. This is the watershed between an FDE and an ordinary engineer. A frontline practitioner's quote is widely cited: "The models are usually the cleanest part. The hard part is finding the workflow no one wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is that way." Palantir's hiring standard puts it even more bluntly: "The candidate's articulation, clarity, and comfort communicating should make me glad to have them run a meeting with a client." Interviews also screen for this translation ability. The signature part of OpenAI and Palantir's FDE interviews is called "problem deconstruction": throwing a huge, vague, real enterprise problem at you, without writing a single line of code for sixty minutes, just watching how you probe, how you define the scope, and how you establish order in chaos. The interviewer's advice is: understand the problem before jumping in—slow is smooth, smooth is fast. In a normal interview, "I optimized the query by 40%" is a perfect answer; the perfect answer in an FDE interview is: "I optimized the query by 40%, which allowed the client's analysts to get their reports two hours earlier every day, tripling the team's processing capacity." Technical achievements only count when translated into the customer's language.

Third layer: Extreme ownership, plus a little "rebelliousness." A phrase circulates in practitioner circles specifically describing the accountability required for this role: "A deployment goes down at 2 AM. You don't file a ticket, you don't blame another team, you don't go back to sleep. You fix it. Period." Palantir has an even more subtle expectation for business-side roles: to have deep industry knowledge while daring to be a "rebel"—able to see the absurdity of the customer's status quo, and daring to push for 10x, rather than 10%, changes. The behavioral benchmark set by CEO Karp back then was the "French waiter": embedded in the service process, sensitive to real needs, yet confident and tasteful enough to guide the customer from "what they think they want" to "what is actually good for them."

### A Day in the Life

In day-to-day terms, an FDE's time is roughly split like this: 40-50% immersed on the customer side coding and tuning systems; 20-30% aligning direction with customer management, breaking down problems, and making architectural decisions; 10-20% distilling patterns learned in the field back into the company's product line; and the rest evaluating, optimizing, and knowledge sharing—writing playbooks, internal evangelism, and training customer teams.

This schedule hides a vital piece of information: an FDE is not "an outsourced engineer," but "an engineer with a two-way mission"—delivering results to the customer on one end, and feeding intelligence back to the company on the other. This is also the theme of the next section.

## 1.5 Everything Speaks with Results

If one sentence were to summarize the FDE work ethic, it would be: Everything speaks with results.

First, clarify the difference between "data" and "results." Enterprise software history has never lacked projects with great data and terrible results: feature lists 100% checked off, but usage at only 5%; system availability at four nines, yet business units prefer to keep using spreadsheets. The vast majority of the 95% failed projects in the MIT report didn't lack data dashboards—the numbers were there, but the value didn't come, because no one was responsible for "that line of numbers on the financial statements."

The FDE model institutionally ensures that "results" are not diluted, specifically through three things.

- **Aligning payment with results:** When doing government projects in the early years, Palantir heavily used "pay upon success" arrangements. McGrew recalled bluntly: "In the early days, it made sense for startups to take all the risk—you pay us after it's done." This logic has evolved more finely in the AI era: Sierra charges per "resolved conversation," not charging if it's unresolved; many FDE service providers deliver by phase and accept payment by results. Once payment is tied to results, the delivery team's behavior is entirely re-prioritized—you won't spend three weeks polishing a feature no one uses, because you'll have to pay for "no one using it" yourself.

- **Measuring success upfront before starting:** The first step in an FDE project is not writing code, but defining "what success looks like" with the customer. Palantir's Bootcamps require customers to first lock down a highly focused core battlefield—"reduce scheduling conflicts on a certain production line by 30%" instead of "explore AI-empowered manufacturing"—precisely to prevent the project from drifting into unfalsifiability under the guise of "exploration." OpenAI's collaboration with John Deere is a textbook example: they first reviewed hundreds of real-world farming cases with agronomy experts, built a custom evaluation system, and only then started iterating models. Ultimately, the figure "chemical usage reduced by up to 70%" wasn't a post-hoc PR spin; it was a target set before work began.

- **The ultimate judge is behavioral change in the customer's organization:** The report had a sharp finding: only about 40% of enterprises provided official AI tool subscriptions for employees, while up to 90% of employees used personal consumer products daily to solve work problems. This means a large number of "successfully launched" projects actually exist in a state where "official systems idle while employees detour." In FDE philosophy, a system launch is not the milestone; the customer team changing how they work is. Sierra deliberately named the role "Agent Engineer," and when head Moeller explained the selection criteria, he said: we only take two kinds of problems—those that are truly hard, and those with real business impact, and both must be true at the same time.

"Everything speaks with results" sounds like common sense, but executing it is an offense against the entire interest structure: sales dare not over-promise because the delivery team is accountable for results; the customer's IT department can't sign off using a "feature list" because business unit usage is the acceptance criteria; the FDE themselves can't use "I built to spec" as a shield because the correctness of the spec itself is also on their ledger. This is precisely why this role is precious, and also why it is expensive.

## 1.6 The Four Faces of an FDE in a Team

An FDE lives in four worlds simultaneously; they are the connector of these four worlds.

- **To the customer, they are an "Embedded Product Manager + Full-Stack Engineer":** Acting like an anthropologist observing the customer's real work—the most valuable discoveries often come from "looking," not "asking"—and like a founder rolling up their sleeves to build it on the spot. Palantir institutionalized this duo: a Deployment Strategist (codename "Echo") responsible for understanding the customer's mission, stakeholders, and adoption path, and a Forward Deployed Engineer (codename "Delta") responsible for technical realization. A two-person team, one diagnosing, one building, neither dispensable.

- **To the company's product line, they are an "Outpost and Intelligence Officer":** This is the most fundamental difference between FDE and traditional delivery teams. The cost of traditional implementation is a sales cost; every man-day spent must be earned back from the contract; a healthy FDE organization treats field work as R&D—if three customers hit the same integration gap, that's not three instances of trouble, it's a piece of product intelligence; if five deployments all need the same workflow, it should be abstracted into the platform's next standard capability. A former Palantir engineer recalled that Foundry's key components were born in customer sites across Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, and Toulouse, growing from the bottom up, and finally feeding back into a product making billions of dollars in annual revenue. (Source in Appendix C)

- **To sales, they are a "Trust Amplifier":** Enterprise customers have been let down too many times and are immune to all slide decks. FDEs rebuild trust through two actions: first, doing it, building something that runs on the customer's own data and environment right then and there; second, honesty, daring to say no to the customer's wrong premises. Palantir's bootcamps processualized this trust production: customers bring real data, and within one to five days a deployable prototype is made, which executives click and use themselves. Early bootcamps had a paid conversion rate of only 5% to 10%, but the company disclosed that later conversion rates neared 75%. Trust can be manufactured through engineering. (Source in Appendix C)

- **To the organization itself, they are a "Talent Crucible":** An easily overlooked fact: Palantir has produced an incredibly dense group of founders. This is no surprise—the daily training of an FDE involves building something valuable end-to-end and getting people to use it in environments with limited resources, vague requirements, and complex stakeholder relationships. This is practically a complete rehearsal for founder training. Srinivas, who later founded Decagon; Moeller, who built Sierra's agent engineering team; and the authors of the most widely circulated methodology articles in this industry all came out of Palantir's forward deployment roles. The talent spillover from one company became the talent infrastructure for an entire industry. (Source in Appendix C)

These four identities combined point to the same conclusion: FDE is not a box on an org chart, but an upgrade in organizational learning—transforming "understanding the customer" from second-hand information passed through layers, into the first-hand muscle memory of engineers.

## 1.7 How to Hire an FDE

Let's pour some cold water first: FDE is one of the hardest roles to hire for in the software industry, because it requires a person to excel simultaneously in two dimensions that usually trade off against each other.

Former Palantir engineer Barry explained this thoroughly in a retrospective article: Palantir's standard for hiring FDEs is "engineers who could get into Google or Facebook"—because they are going to the customer's site to **build** systems, not to tweak parameters; but pure technical skill is far from enough, forward deployed people also need creativity, judgment, and customer-facing charisma. He added a hard truth: this is much more expensive and difficult than hiring a traditional pre-sales team.

Deconstructing the market's hiring practices reveals three key elements in hiring FDEs.

- **Candidate Profile:** Hire "curious bulldozers," not "refined craftsmen." a16z's advice to startups uses the phrase "insatiably curious go-getters": highly self-motivated, lacking reverence for the status quo, and hungry for customer problems. McGrew was more specific: FDE teams need two kinds of people—"domain rebels" who understand the industry but don't blindly follow its conventions; and "prototype sprinters" who prioritize speed over perfection and accept throwing away the first version to rewrite. Conversely, two types of people favored in traditional engineering culture are red flags for FDE roles: "craftsmen" who put elegant code above customer results, and "loyal executors" who treat the customer's every word as a decree.

- **Interviews:** Use the "Deconstruction Round" instead of standardized tests. The "problem deconstruction" mentioned earlier is the soul of an FDE interview: giving the candidate a fuzzy, massive, raw real-world business problem—"A bank's compliance team manually checks 30,000 transaction alerts every day, 90% are false alarms, what do you do?"—and watching for sixty minutes. What's tested is not the answer, but the process: did they clarify constraints before jumping in, did they distinguish root causes from symptoms, did they remember there's a real user on the other end of the system, and can they clearly explain their trade-offs. Palantir also embeds about twenty minutes of behavioral questions into every technical round, explicitly rejecting candidates with strong tech but poor cultural fit—the most important cultural item being a sense of order when facing ambiguity.

- **Pricing:** Accept a mixed structure of "Engineer Salary + Business Elasticity." Market data from 2026 can serve as an anchor: the median total compensation for Palantir forward deployed engineers is about $215,000; for mid-level FDEs at top AI labs, it's about $385,000, and for seniors, about $610,000; Anthropic's base salary for the role ranges between $200,000 and $300,000. Another detail worth noting is bonus design: Palantir's bonuses are often tied to operational metrics like customer expansion, falling somewhere between an engineering bonus and a sales commission; a16z's advice is to align incentives with Account Executives, but not to give FDEs hard sales quotas—that would drive behavior toward closing deals rather than results. Compensation structure is the final step in role definition: how you pay ultimately shapes behavior. (Source in Appendix C)

## 1.8 How to Become an FDE

Changing perspective: if you are an engineer, product manager, or consultant looking to enter this rapidly growing market, what path should you take?

First, self-assess: the glamour and the toll of this role are two sides of the same coin. In practitioner forum communities, there's a rare honesty in discussions about FDEs. The positives: the best combination of technical substance and brand endorsement, and one of the few roles where you can simultaneously build technical, business, and client networks. The toll: 25% to 50% travel is the norm; OpenAI's job postings explicitly state travel up to 50%; the pace of work is defined by the customer's urgency, not your own schedule; and a recurring warning—the risk of burnout is real.

One comment was sharp enough to be quoted directly: "Some treat it as a brand springboard, some say it's just consulting with a cool title, both are true—the difference is whether your company feeds field learning back into the product, or treats you as a man-day to sell." This statement is both a criterion for choosing a job and the subject of Chapter 7 of this book. (Source in Appendix C)

Next, catch up: what you need to study is not technology, but the craft of "translation." A technical foundation is just the ticket for entry, which most engineers have; what's truly scarce are three translation skills—translating business problems into technical problems (Chapter 2), translating technical solutions into words executives understand (Chapter 3), and translating field experience into reusable knowledge for the team (Chapter 7). To practice these three, going on the field beats going to class: shadow a pre-sales meeting, do an on-site stint, give a training session to a real user, and see which kind of discomfort you grow fastest in.

For interview prep, rewrite your resume to be "customer result-oriented." The principle was stated earlier, let's emphasize it again: every technical achievement on a resume must go the last mile into customer language. "Built a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system" is engineer language; "Built a Retrieval-Augmented Generation system that reduced customer service initial response from 4 hours to 8 minutes, leading the customer to proactively request capacity expansion upon renewal" is FDE language. Also prepare two types of stories: one time you established order when requirements were unclear, and one honest failure—Palantir alumni interviewers have an obsession with "tell me about a real failure" because the essence of the job is moving forward in uncertainty, and those who don't admit failure lack the ability to evolve.

When choosing a company, ask three questions back. First, "What is your product platform?"—an FDE without a platform foundation is pure manual outsourcing. Second, "How does field learning flow back into the product?"—if they can't give a recent example of a field pattern crystalizing into a product feature, there's a problem. Third, "Who does the FDE report to?"—reporting to the product or engineering line usually means the model is taken seriously; reporting to the sales line means you need to be careful about becoming a headcount pool for pre-sales.

## 1.9 The FDE's Common Toolbox

At the end of this chapter, we present the current tool landscape for this role. Tools become outdated, but the capability layers behind the tools do not. Five layers, from beneath your feet to behind your back.

- **Platform Foundation Layer | The Company's Weapon.** The premise of the FDE model working is "bringing the platform to the field"; otherwise, it devolves into custom development. Palantir's Foundry and AIP have at their core something called the "Ontology"—modeling an enterprise's data, logic, and actions into a semantic layer, allowing AI to run on a foundation that "understands the business"; OpenAI's model API and agent toolchain; Sierra's agent platform. When evaluating any FDE opportunity, the thickness of this layer is the top priority.

- **AI Engineering Layer | Personal Craft.** Daily skills post-2025 include: prompt engineering and context management; Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), letting the model look up information before answering; evaluation systems, establishing quantifiable yardsticks for vague business quality—this is the signature skill distinguishing an AI-era FDE from a traditional implementation engineer; agent architecture, tool calling, multi-agent collaboration, and keeping humans in the loop at critical junctures; as well as engineering optimization for cost and speed.

- **Data and Integration Layer | The First Battle Upon Entry.** Almost all FDE projects spend their first week wrestling with this layer: data pipelines, enterprise system connectors, permissions and identity authentication, vector databases for AI lookups, data governance and desensitization (redacting sensitive info). A practitioner's rule of thumb: 70% of project delays happen here, but it's invisible in a demo—it's the underwater part of the iceberg.

- **Delivery and Collaboration Layer | Survival Gear in the Customer's Environment.** Working within a customer's security perimeter means "dual adaptation": you must know how to use your own modern toolchain, but also be able to stoop to the customer's environment—which might be an intranet physically isolated from the internet, might only allow deployment in the customer's cloud, or might block access to code hosting websites. Containerization (technology to pack up environments and take them), Infrastructure as Code (managing server environments with code), and contingency plans for "running the environment in a conference room with no internet" all belong to this layer.

- **Knowledge Distillation Layer | The Lever for Scale.** This is the most easily overlooked layer, yet it determines whether a team can escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount": playbooks, component libraries, deployment checklists, and the writing habit of turning "a solution for a specific customer" into "a pattern for a class of customers." Chapter 7 will expand on this specifically.

The five layers of the toolbox together form the complete outline of this role: standing on a platform, holding engineering tools, keeping eyes on results, and connected to the product line behind.

This is the FDE. In the following seven chapters, we enter the heartland of methodology, starting from the very origin of a project's choice—how to ensure you are solving the right problem.
