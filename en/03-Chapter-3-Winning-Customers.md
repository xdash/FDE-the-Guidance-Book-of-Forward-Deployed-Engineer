# Chapter 3: Winning Customers

> "Enterprises buying AI is like your grandmother getting an iPhone—she wants to use it, but she needs you to help her set it up."
> — a16z (Andreessen Horowitz)

## 3.1 Screening Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson in Internet product customer acquisition is filtering seed users: 100 users who love you are better than 10,000 who think you're just okay. The counterpart in the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) world is lighthouse customers—the kind of customers who not only bring you revenue but also send a signal to the entire industry.

The strategic value of lighthouses is exponentially amplified in the FDE model, for three reasons.

First, a lighthouse is the strongest sales asset. Enterprise customers have long decision-making chains and high risk aversion, making peer endorsement the shortest path to persuasion. The early history of legal AI company Harvey is a textbook example: the first major client officially announced in February 2023 was Allen & Overy, a top-tier global law firm with 3,500 lawyers and 43 offices. Once this lighthouse was lit, major clients like PwC and Cleary Gottlieb followed—the legal industry values pedigree above all else, and winning Allen & Overy meant obtaining a pass to the entire top-tier law firm market. Palantir's early history is isomorphic: the CIA was its most demanding and most endorsement-valuable client; the trust of the intelligence community later became the key to opening the doors to Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

The process by which Harvey lit this lighthouse was itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Allen & Overy set up a dedicated group—the Markets Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling—to secretly trial the product of this then-unknown startup. Note their method of validation: not watching demonstrations, but firm-wide practical combat. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had asked the system about 40,000 real work questions, covering 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling's conclusion was a quote repeatedly cited by the legal community: "I have been working in legal tech for 15 years and I have never seen anything like this that can change the rules of the game." Another partner demonstrated a specific scenario to the media: asking the system to prepare a ten-page document for an American client on "how to open a bank in Luxembourg," "and it actually did it." (Source in Appendix C)

Second, a lighthouse determines your product DNA. In the FDE model, on-site learning flows back to the product—meaning your first ten customers essentially participate in shaping your product. Pick the wrong lighthouse, and the product will be driven in a non-universal direction. The first piece of advice a16z gives startups, "sell smart," is exactly about this: young companies cannot satisfy everyone; they must choose an ideal customer profile that has at least some commonalities in system environments and usage scenarios, so that the learning from each delivery can accumulate rather than cancel each other out.

Third, the quality of lighthouses is an order of magnitude more important than quantity. Your capacity is inherently scarce—an elite team can deeply serve a single-digit number of customers concurrently. Taking on the wrong one doesn't mean "earning one less deal," but rather an elite team getting bogged down in a quagmire for half a year. Therefore, the three types of high-risk signals from Chapter 2 (no business owner, refusal to provide real data, universe-scale requirements) must also be checked here, plus a lighthouse-specific test: negotiate early on whether they are willing to step up after success—jointly publishing case studies, speaking out at industry conferences, and hosting visits from your potential clients. A lighthouse unwilling to speak for you publicly has its value halved at least.

### Beware of "Requirement Locusts"

When making Internet products, beware of "product locusts"—early users who swarm in, use it and leave, and mislead the product direction. The corresponding species in the FDE world is "requirement locusts": customers with ample budgets and strong demands, but who will drain your team dry without generating any compounding returns. Three identifying features: their requirements deviate significantly from your company's strategic direction, leaving no reusable capabilities after completion; they treat FDEs as cheap outsourced labor, assigning tasks by headcount rather than aligning by outcomes; and internal political friction is huge, meaning your primary job becomes helping one department prove another wrong. Saying no to requirement locusts is hard—their contract values are often tempting. But remember Barry's accounting: a wrong pilot burns not only immediate costs but also team time, morale, and the compounding product returns that could have grown from the right customer.

## 3.2 Start by Doing the Dumbest Things

The famous incubator YC has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." The founders of the earliest homestay platforms took photos of hosts' houses door-to-door; the founders of the online payment company Stripe manually installed software for users on the spot.

When McGrew was asked in a podcast about the relationship between FDE and this maxim, he gave a precise formulation: **The FDE model is doing unscalable things at scale**.

This sentence lays bare the philosophical undertone of FDE customer acquisition: in the enterprise market, there is no scalable shortcut to acquiring trust, only "dumb" methods. There are three progressive levels.

Level One: People must be present. The last item on a16z's checklist for forward deployed teams is just a few words: be there in person. The reasoning is, "It's a cliché, but a cliché is a cliché because it's true"—being on-site not only improves sales but also exponentially increases the success rate when unraveling the client's internal power dynamics and driving the adoption of new tools. Enterprise customers price trust by "number of meetings" and "shared experiences." Remote meetings build familiarity; only fighting side by side builds trust.

Level Two: Get your hands dirty. OpenAI's FDEs go into the fields in Iowa with agronomists; Palantir engineers live on oil drilling rigs and airplane final assembly lines for weeks; Harvey's FDEs do adoption demos for partners one by one in law firms. None of these scenarios is "efficient," but each produces something remote communication never could: a firsthand feeling of the customer's situation. This visceral sense translates directly into solution quality—only after stepping in the mud of a farmland will you understand why that seemingly perfect mobile interface is completely unusable under strong outdoor glare.

Level Three: Be a servant first, then a mentor. The most common mistake FDEs make in the early stages of entry is carrying a "we are here to save you" posture. With the wrong posture, all intelligence channels will close. The correct sequence is to first perform the most inconspicuous services: helping the client's analysts fix a data issue, helping the IT department fill in an API document, or automating a weekly report for the business team. These "dumb tasks" are exchanged for three strategic assets: the real map of the internal organization (who has the final say, who is trusted, who are the hidden key nodes), the truth of the data environment (which data is trusted, which is just for show), and most importantly—the identity certification that "this outsider is one of us." Only after this identity is established will your words carry weight.

The end of dumb methods isn't staying dumb forever. Chapter 7 will discuss how to distill these grueling efforts into replicable playbooks—but before scaling, you must first wade through the mud to carve out that path worth replicating.

## 3.3 Trust Dividend: The Endorsement Goldmine of Benchmark Customers

Enterprise procurement is a market with "extreme information asymmetry and extremely high costs of failure," and decision-makers have an extremely steep trust hierarchy for information: vendor self-promotion is inferior to analyst reports; analyst reports are inferior to public peer case studies; public peer case studies are inferior to private peer recommendations. The last tier—"Someone I know used it and said it really works"—crushes all other channels in conversion efficiency. And the FDE model is precisely the best machine for producing "private recommendation material": what you deliver isn't a software license, it's an executive client saying "they really know their stuff" at a dinner with peers.

Mining this goldmine involves three levels of action.

Level One: Turn delivery into a "story that can be told." The prerequisite for client executives to spread the word for you is that your delivery can be narrated by them as a story that makes them look good in front of their peers. This requires the deliverables to have three narrative elements: a specific number ("chemical use reduced by 70%," "anti-money laundering investigation went from hours to minutes"), a specific person ("our agronomists worked in the field with their team"), and a specific contrast ("it used to take three months, this time it took five days"). At the end of the delivery, proactively prepare this narrative for the internal sponsor—a one-pager, three charts, and a version that can be told in 30 seconds. How detailed the materials are determines whether the story gets distorted when retold.

Level Two: Turn customer success into customer social currency. After deploying 4,000 stores in eight months via a boot camp model, the pharmacy chain Walgreens became a star at industry conferences; market research firm J.D. Power went even further—after attending Palantir's boot camp as a client, it started hosting boot camps for its own clients. (Source in Appendix C) This is the ideal state: the customer displays your methodology as its own industry leadership. Endorsement upgrades from "thank you" to "proud to use you."

Level Three: Beware of the "backfire mechanism" of endorsements. The enterprise market holds grudges. A failed delivery to a highly exposed client spreads much faster than ten successes—because failure stories make better drinking conversation. This is why the "refusal mechanism" from Chapter 2 and the "activation discipline" from Chapter 4 are so important: the fatal flaw in a lighthouse strategy isn't failing to find a lighthouse, but letting the lighthouse extinguish in your hands.

## 3.4 Using Data to Understand the Customer's Foundation

Before and after FDEs enter the field, there is an action called "entry due diligence": before writing the first line of code, use a structured approach to fully grasp the customer's foundation. A complete due diligence checklist contains five maps.

- **Data Map**: What data sources the client has, who owns each, what the quality is, who manages permissions, and whether there is dark data you wouldn't even think of—a spreadsheet hoarded by a veteran employee, a report that only circulates in emails, paper ledgers. The point is not "what exists," but "which is trusted." In almost every organization, the official data source and the data source genuinely trusted by employees are not the same; the latter is the one you need to connect to.

- **Process Map:** The real operational map of the target business process—not the version in the process documents, but the version observed through shadowing, including all undocumented steps, exceptions, and workarounds. Specially mark three points: the step consuming the most time, the step with the highest cost of error, and the step causing the most intense emotions. These three points are usually the motherlode of value.

- **Organization Map:** Who initiates, who pays, who uses, who can veto, and who is the uncrowned king. The most common cause of death for enterprise projects isn't technology, but miscalculating the organizational math—your internal sponsor's position is not high enough, or too high (so high they have no time for you), or the right people were not included. Specifically find those "knowledge hubs": people whose titles aren't high, but who everyone goes to when facing practical problems. They are both the best source of requirements and the seed nodes for future rollout.

- **System Map:** The true face of the technical environment—the list of systems to integrate and their interface status, security and compliance boundaries, and change management processes. The system map determines your deployment architecture and schedule. Many project timelines are doomed to bankruptcy on day one because no one asked clearly: releasing a version on the client's side takes a six-week process.

- **Political Map:** The most sensitive and critical one. Whose cheese does this project move? Is the automated target process a source of power for a certain department? Whose job will your solution make seem redundant? That report listed "employee reluctance to adopt new tools" as the number one roadblock, and the root of resistance is mostly not laziness, but fear—fear of being replaced, fear of being proven incompetent, fear of losing their reason to exist. The function of the political map is to identify the carriers of these fears in advance, and arrange "new paths of life" rather than "dead ends" for them in the solution design. Chapter 5's renewal topic will return to this point: the people you design to be "victims" will become the fiercest opponents during contract renewal.

Only when all five maps are complete are you truly "in the field." This due diligence usually takes one to two weeks, conducted separately by "Echoes" and "Deltas" with daily alignment. It looks like pure cost, but the math goes like this: the two weeks of due diligence saves you from three months of sprinting on the wrong battlefield.

## 3.5 Technical Content Marketing: Building a Trust Engine with Continuous Output

Content marketing is a classic weapon for customer acquisition in Internet companies. The customer acquisition logic of FDE companies determines a unique positioning for their content marketing: **not chasing traffic, but chasing the "pre-sale of professional trust."**

The way enterprise customers find you is not by scrolling past your ads, but by: encountering a tough problem, searching, asking around, and discovering that a certain company has exceptionally professional public discourse on this issue, and then concluding—"They get it, find them." The role of content here is the "pre-qualification" at the very front of the customer decision chain. Palantir constantly publishes scenario-based technical articles; OpenAI and Anthropic turn enterprise customer cases into detailed technical narratives; an industry-defining magnum opus from a16z sets the tone for the entire sector—these are not brand promotions; they are meticulously curated trust assets.

FDE companies' content marketing has three golden rules distinguishing it from conventional enterprise content.

- **Rule One: Write from the "Trench Perspective," Not the "Booth Perspective."** Conventional enterprise content talks about how strong the product is and how big the vision is; FDE content talks about how hard the problem is and how we waded through it. Why did the article on forward deployed engineering by former Palantir engineer Barry go viral in the industry? Because it was all about things you can't see at exhibition booths: the waste of reinventing the wheel, negative margin pilots, exhausted engineers—as a result, this article that "aired dirty laundry" became the best evangelism for the Palantir model. The truth from the trenches inherently carries penetrating power because readers can distinguish who is reciting marketing pitches and who is recounting the reality they live in every day.

- **Rule Two: Open Source Methodologies to Create "Quotation Eligibility."** Making public your methodologies for discovery, validation, and delivery may seem like teaching peers in the short term, but in the long term, it defines industry standards—when customers across the industry start asking questions using your frameworks ("How is your evaluation system built?" "What does your deployment checklist look like?"), you evolve from a vendor to the examiner. The emergence and rapid popularity of practitioner open-source communities like OpenFDE in 2026 proves that this industry's thirst for knowledge is far from satisfied; whoever systematically satisfies it first grasps the power to define. The writing of this book is, to some extent, an application of the same logic.

- **Rule Three: Make the Client's Internal Sponsor the Hero in the Content.** The byline logic of case studies is subtle: the protagonist should be the visionary manager on the client's side, and your team is the "partner helping them succeed." Giving sponsors the stage is equivalent to handing a similar persona at the next potential client an invitation card to "become them."

## 3.6 Tactics for Clearing Procurement, Legal, and Security Reviews

The "floodgates" of the enterprise market are the Procurement Department, the Legal Department, and the Security Review Committee. A large number of technically successful FDE projects die at these three gates, dying with absolutely no technical dignity: contract terms break down, data processing agreements get stuck, and security questionnaires drag on into the fourth month.

The key to clearing them lies in how you view these three gates: **treat them as obstacles, and you'll flip over in the last mile; treat them as part of the delivery, and you can build an advantage**—because most tech companies are careless about these three things; if you are serious, you win.

Procurement Gate: First, understand that procurement KPIs are not your enemy. The procurement department's duty is to control prices, control risks, and ensure compliance. They push down prices not out of spite, but because it's their job. The countermeasure is not locking horns, but giving them tools "they can report back with": clear phased pricing (giving them stepping stones for price reduction), comparable market benchmarks (giving basis for approval), and most importantly—measurable outcome commitments (making accusations of "overpaying" groundless). The outcome-based pricing model has an unexpected advantage here: what procurement struggles to approve most is "expenses where it's unclear what will be obtained," whereas quotes like "pay $X for every ticket resolved" are understood by procurement at a glance.

Legal Gate: Polish your data processing agreement like a product. The legal focus of enterprise AI projects is highly concentrated: Will the data be used to train models? Where is the data stored, and who can access it? Who pays if there is a security incident? Who is responsible for AI output errors?

Smart teams productize the standard answers to these questions—pre-configured agreement templates, clear declarations of model usage, and tiered accident liability frameworks. Palantir survived in the intelligence community precisely by building "permissions and auditing" into the core of its product (who viewed what data is fully traceable); Anthropic, in its collaboration with financial clients, made "traceable and auditable" a selling point in its agent design. **Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.**

Security Review Gate: Buy yourself two months with a "Pre-answered Whitepaper." The standard motion for enterprise security reviews is a questionnaire with hundreds of questions. Waiting to answer them when the questionnaire arrives requires cross-departmental confirmation for each item, which means waiting for months. Efficient teams proactively maintain a "Security Whitepaper": deployment architecture diagrams, data flow diagrams, encryption and permission schemes, compliance certifications, and standard answers to every question asked in past reviews. Eighty percent of most questionnaires can be copied and pasted directly from this whitepaper. Your response speed itself is the most intuitive signal of security maturity—security teams have seen it all, and they know what a frantic scramble for answers implies.

## 3.7 Ecosystem Bundling: Standing on the Shoulders of Channels

The ecosystem strategy in the FDE era is to borrow the shoulders of three types of partners.

Type One: Cloud Providers and Large Platforms. AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud are themselves major gateways for enterprise AI procurement. Entering their joint sales systems equates to obtaining a fast pass straight to enterprise procurement lists. The application marketplaces of cloud providers also solve a practical pain point: customers can tap into their existing cloud budget commitments to procure your services, bypassing the procurement process for onboarding new vendors.

Type Two: Consultancies and System Integrators. This is the most dramatic ecosystem upheaval of 2026. In the list of founding partners of OpenAI's deployment arm, names like Bain & Company, Capgemini, and McKinsey stand prominently side-by-side—the world's largest consulting and integration giants have shifted from "potential competitors" to "equity-holding allies." The logic is crystal clear: model companies have technology, consultancies have client relationships and industry depth, and integrators have implementation manpower—only through this tripartite convergence can the massive market of "AI transformation" be swallowed whole. For startups, the takeaway is dual: while you must beware of consulting giants devouring your delivery layer under the guise of the ecosystem, you must also recognize the genuine dividends of allying with regional and industry-specific integrators—they hold in their hands client relationships you couldn't build in three years.

The same script is playing out almost simultaneously in China. In July 2026, ByteDance's Volcano Engine signed a strategic partnership with EY, one of the Big Four accounting firms: they will jointly build solutions around core scenarios like data governance, financial management, and marketing growth. Most notably, the two parties plan to build a 1,000-person-level FDE team to "create an AI-native delivery team." Volcano Engine President Tan Dai's words were very straightforward: the FDE team must "place engineers with dual technical and industry backgrounds forward at the client site, deeply participating in the entire process of solution implementation." On one side is a consulting giant rich in client relationships; on the other is a model platform lacking industry depth—FDE has become the welding point between the two. (Source in Appendix C)

Type Three: The Customer's Customers. The highest level of bundling is embedding yourself into the customer's own value chain. After attending Palantir's boot camp, J.D. Power started organizing boot camps for its own clients—Palantir's capabilities spilled over through J.D. Power's client relationships, bringing the customer acquisition cost near zero. When designing your delivery, you can pre-embed this "re-deliverability": can this solution be used by the customer to serve their own customers? If so, you transition from a vendor into a part of the customer's business model—and that is the deepest moat.

## 3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is a Strategy in Itself

Scarcity can manufacture attraction—invite-only and queuing mechanisms are classic tactics in Internet products. The scheduling issue in FDE is opposite in shape but identical in principle: **Your capacity is always smaller than demand, thus "who goes first and who goes next" inherently becomes a strategic tool.**

Let's first talk about cruel realistic constraints. A qualified forward deployment squad (one on the business side plus two or three on the technical side) can generally deliver no more than two projects concurrently with high quality. OpenAI started with 2 engineers in its early years, Sierra's deployment cycles are measured in months, and Harvey's deployment for a single law firm takes six to nine months—elite delivery capacity is a naturally scarce resource. Scarcity isn't terrifying; what's terrifying is failing to price scarcity.

The first principle of scheduling is to prioritize by strategic value, not by contract amount. The decision matrix has only two dimensions: the lighthouse value of the customer (industry signal strength) and the learning value of the customer (how much reusable capability can be accumulated). Customers scoring high on both dimensions could, and even should, be prioritized at a loss—Barry recalls Palantir doing free pilots and burning millions of dollars in its early days, betting exactly on this matrix. Large contracts scoring low on both dimensions are actually the most dangerous: the money looks big, but it traps an elite team for half a year.

The second principle is to productize "waiting." If a customer must wait three months in the queue before entry, don't let those three months go to waste: give them a checklist for data preparation (so entry equals acceleration), suggestions for organizational warm-up (which key people to secure first), and lightweight remote diagnostics (to maintain warmth and sweep for mines in advance). Manage the waiting period well, and the delivery cycle after entry can be shortened by a third—queuing transforms from a burden on customer experience into a component of delivery quality.

The third principle is to never promise concurrency that exceeds capacity. Your delivery quality relies on the continuous focus of the same team—each project carries an entire set of client context; switching requires reloading context each time, making the friction of multi-project concurrency far greater than imagined. It's better to let sales market "we're booked until next quarter" as scarcity (which often even drives up prices) than to let the team run themselves ragged among three customers. When business is booming, it's easy to succumb to the temptation of taking more orders; but the cost of crashing from over-committing is often far greater than the cost of taking one less deal.

## 3.9 Making Your Brand Ubiquitous with Open Source Tools and Trial Kits

There is a unique customer acquisition tactic in the FDE world: turn what you do well into open-source tools and demos, and let the target customer's engineers start using them first.

The logic is this: the initiator of enterprise procurement is an executive, but technical veto power lies in the hands of engineers. Getting target customers' engineers to use your tools in their daily work is equivalent to pre-planting trust votes on the technical end of the decision chain. There are three typical mediums.

Open-source tools and components. Open source the generic modules accumulated during the delivery process—evaluation frameworks, connectors, deployment templates. Anthropic turned the Model Context Protocol (an open standard enabling models to connect to external tools) into a public specification, and the artifacts FDEs deliver at client sites are built on this protocol—the first lesson the client engineers learn is Anthropic's tech stack. **The popularization process of a standard is the accumulation process of trust.**

Self-service "Tech Showcases." Enterprise products typically lack self-service trials, but they can have tech showcases: public demo environments, sandboxes (isolated practice environments), and interactive tutorials. Palantir's boot camp essentially turns "trials" into a ritual; for resource-constrained teams, a quick-start kit that allows a client engineer to plug in sample data and run through it in an afternoon is the best sales engineer.

Presence in engineering communities. Keep the presence of your engineers consistently visible in the technical communities of target industries—answering questions, sharing pitfalls, committing code. The conversion path for this presence is long, but it reaches exactly the people who hold technical veto power. For FDE companies, recruitment and customer acquisition become one in this action: the best candidate pool and the best client leads often come from the same community.

## 3.10 Writing Proposals and Proof of Concept Plans

The most expensive text in the enterprise market, in terms of unit price, is the proposal and the proof of concept (PoC) plan—saying they're worth their weight in gold is no exaggeration.

The bad news is that most proposals written by technical teams make the same mistake: talking endlessly about "what we will do" rather than "what you will get." A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted pyramid structure.

Layer One: Business outcomes, stated in one paragraph. The first paragraph of the proposal must be the business outcome in the customer's language: "Within eight weeks, reduce the average processing time for your bank's anti-money laundering investigations from 4 hours to 15 minutes, freeing up investigative capacity equivalent to 2.5 times your current team." Without this sentence, customer executives simply won't read the rest.

Layer Two: Value validation path, how to prove it was achieved. Specify acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, and exit mechanisms for each phase. The signal conveyed by this layer is "we dare to be tested"—in an enterprise market scarred by over-promising, daring to be tested is the rarest form of sincerity.

Layer Three: Delivery method, how we will do it. This layer is where the technical solution finally comes in, and it should be written so that non-technical readers can follow: architecture diagrams with business annotations, and milestones with decision points. It's especially important to specify matters requiring customer cooperation—data access, the time commitment of key personnel. Clearly stating what the customer needs to do serves both to sweep for mines in advance and to filter customers.

Layer Four: Risks and countermeasures, we've thought about how we might die. Most proposals evade risks as if mentioning them is bad luck. On the contrary, mature buyers trust suppliers the most when they "clearly lay out the ways to die": what happens if data quality is substandard, what happens if key personnel change, what happens if the accuracy rate misses the threshold? This layer is the proposal's trust amplifier, and also the textual embodiment of the "Reject Expensive Failures" concept from Chapter 2.

The Proof of Concept plan has a particular uniqueness: it is the carrier of the "graduation criteria." The plan must specify a maximum duration, acceptance metrics, and two continuations—graduation and parting ways. A vaguely written PoC plan is equivalent to issuing a permit to break ground on a PoC graveyard.

## 3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundaries of Hybrid Delivery

Over the past few years, FDE delivery has undergone a significant evolution: moving from purely on-site to a hybrid model of "primarily remote + on-site at key nodes." Palantir's official narrative also confirms that today many projects are executed mostly remotely, with on-site presence concentrated at critical milestones.

Designing the rhythm of the hybrid model is a craft. Which tasks physically require presence? Experience boils it down to three categories: early relationship building (the first meeting, shadowing, building trust with executives—trust cannot be built across a screen; it requires meeting in person); high-intensity co-creation (boot camp-style joint building, whiteboarding tough critical architectural decisions); and politically sensitive periods (solution rollout, inter-departmental coordination, change management—during these moments, you need to read the room). Which tasks are actually better done remotely? Deep coding, document distillation, routine iteration—tasks that require an uninterrupted flow state.

The hybrid model also has two hidden dividends. One is the cost structure: on-site presence is one of the largest variable items in FDE costs, and a reasonable hybrid ratio can expand delivery profit margins by more than ten percentage points. The other is talent sustainability: a team traveling half the year year-round has a significantly higher burnout rate—practitioners' complaints about travel rank highest on forums. The hybrid model is not about slacking off; it's a guarantee for organizational endurance.

But the bottom line must be drawn clearly: **a "never-meet" FDE is a false proposition**. If a project is fully remote from launch to delivery, you lose not only the relationship, but the very thing repeatedly argued in this chapter and Chapter 2—firsthand cognition of the site. Remote work sustains existing trust and context, but those must first be created in the physical world.

## 3.12 Overseas Expansion

The proposition of expanding the FDE model overseas presented two interesting camps in 2026.

The globalization of American companies is demonstrated by giants: OpenAI's FDE recruitment map covers San Francisco, New York, and Seattle, as well as London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi—the geographic distribution of the FDE team precisely traces the global map of enterprise AI purchasing power: North America, Western Europe, developed economies in East Asia, and Middle Eastern sovereign wealth.

FDE overseas expansion in the Chinese context follows a different logic. The past dilemma was "product DNA": domestic vendors had long done highly customized project-based businesses, and their organizational capabilities misaligned with the standardization and productization demanded by overseas markets. But the FDE model provides a reverse perspective: **The delivery culture of Chinese engineers—able to endure hardship, quick to respond, proficient across the full stack, and accustomed to solving all problems on the client site—is exactly what most closely aligns with FDE requirements**. So the question has never been whether Chinese engineers are suited to be FDEs, but whether the platform foundation behind them is thick enough. Among big tech companies, ByteDance's Volcano Engine established an FDE team for the Doubao large model; among startups, some local teams have written "FDE does not equal on-site outsourcing" onto their official websites—both paths are being explored.

On specific strategies for overseas expansion, FDE has an innate advantage and an innate constraint. Advantage: the "lighthouse to endorsement" logic is equally effective in global markets, and the willingness to pay and spirit of contract of enterprise customers in developed countries are more mature. Constraint: FDE is a heavily localized business—language, time zone, compliance, local trust networks; each requires a local team, not remote support. This means the cost structure of FDE going overseas is naturally higher than that of online software going overseas, and the pace must be more restrained: first secure a foothold with products that can be delivered remotely, then assemble local delivery teams in key markets, doing it one country at a time.

The twelve things discussed in this chapter boil down to one sentence: **The essence of winning customers is becoming the exception worthy of trust in a market that has been let down countless times.** Signing the contract is just an admission ticket—next, the real hard battle begins: bringing the system alive and getting people to use it.
