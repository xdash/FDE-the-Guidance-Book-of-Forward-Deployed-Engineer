# Chapter 8: Complete Case Studies

The methodologies of the previous seven chapters were all distilled from real-world practice sites. This chapter fully restores these sites: five sets of case studies, from the inventors of the model to the latest inheritors, from Silicon Valley to China. When reading the cases, it is recommended to look back at the first seven chapters with a question: how were these methods intuitively created before the day they were named?

## 8.1 Palantir: Forging a "Dumb Method" into a Moat Over Twenty Years

### Entrepreneurship: Building Software for "Users You Can't Ask"

2003 in Silicon Valley, still lying in the ruins of the dot-com bubble. Peter Thiel pulled together a group of Stanford-educated young people to do something that sounded crazy: build data analysis software for US intelligence agencies. The company's name was taken from the Palantiri in "The Lord of the Rings"—seeing stones that look into the distance.

The crazy part wasn't the technology, but the users. Bob McGrew's widely circulated memory years later explains the predicament most clearly: "One challenge of building software for spies is that I don't know any spies. You probably don't either. Even if you happen to find a spy and ask him 'how do you actually work normally', he usually won't tell you."

The entire methodology of the software industry—user interviews, questionnaires, usability testing—collectively failed in front of this user group. Co-founder Stephen Cohen's response was endearingly clumsy: build a demo, show it to the intelligence agency people, and ask what they think. The answer was merciless: "This is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat, but followed up: "Then how would you like it to be different?" Then he wrote down every piece of feedback, went back to change it, and delivered it to their door again once changed. The cycle repeated.

This cycle is the genetic fragment of the FDE, and it contains two intuitions that later proved invaluable: clients in complex domains do not know what they want until they see something working; the fastest way to find out "what they want" is to put the people who build things right next to the people who use them.

### Formation: From a Stopgap Measure to Organizational Strategy

Early Palantir moved forward along the path of "one client, one set of customizations", and soon ran into the problem that all enterprise software companies crash into: what the second client wants is "subtly but crucially different" from the first.

The standard solution is to distill commonalities, build a universal product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's clients don't accept "no"—they are the CIA and the US military on the battlefield. What really led the company out of this dead knot was early employee Shyam Sankar (later the company's President and CTO). His solution was seen as heresy at the time: don't build two products, nor build a rigid one, but build a highly customizable platform and send engineers to station at the client's site to complete the last mile.

Sankar's most critical move was a rebellion on the ledger. In the software industry's financial language, "customizing for a single client" is called cost of service, which is the enemy of profit margins and must be minimized. Sankar redefined it as "product discovery"—on-site customization is not spending money; it's gathering intelligence for the product's next evolution. A change in terminology altered a company's capital allocation logic: what others saw as a cost center became Palantir's R&D center.

The organizational form took shape accordingly. Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") are embedded with clients, "one client, mobilizing multiple capabilities"; Deployment Strategists (codename "Echo") are responsible for understanding client missions and organizations; Platform Engineers are in the rear, "one capability, serving multiple clients". By 2016, the number of Forward Deployed Engineers at Palantir exceeded Platform Engineers—a software company where most of its forces are on the "front line".

### Trials: Battlefields, Hurricanes, and Oilfields

The value of the FDE model has been repeatedly proven in extreme on-site environments.

On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, roadside bombs were the number one killer of patrols. The stationed Palantir engineers found that soldiers had zero interest in exquisite intelligence charts—they just wanted to "mark this road as suspicious on the map". The engineers threw together a rudimentary map tool on the spot: a soldier clicks, and the risky road section becomes visible to everyone. This tool directly reduced casualties and later settled into a standard feature of the platform. Note: it could not have been born in any headquarters conference room; it could only be born in the exact moment when engineers and soldiers were looking at the same road together.

The prototype site for this playbook was even earlier than the map tool. Around 2007, Sankar took a small team into the classified information room of the US military's anti-IED operations center, co-working for two weeks. Even speakerphones were banned in the classified room, so he strapped the phone to his head with an elastic band to free his hands for coding—listening to analysts' suggestions in one ear and colleagues at the Silicon Valley headquarters in the other. Nineteen hours a day: demoing, integrating data, receiving feedback, changing it on the spot.

When the two weeks ended, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself, however, broke down and called Karp: "This is unsustainable, we're done." Karp's answer later became company culture: turn this "unsustainability" into an institution. (Source in Appendix C)

During the Hurricane Sandy relief efforts in 2012, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers stitched scattered rescue data into real-time maps to coordinate the deployment of rescue resources. Oil rig platforms, aircraft final assembly plants, bank trading floors—engineers took the act of "stationing on-site" to depths unprecedented in the software industry. Former Palantir engineer Barry recalled that the core components of the Foundry platform were born in Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku—a world map where every dot represents an on-site creation.

The costs were equally real. Barry's records are a must-read counterexample to understand this model: top-tier engineers, global travel, massive reinvention of wheels, free pilots, "many projects' profit margins were literally negative infinity"; in 2014, the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held", chaotic enough to keep equity-holding employees awake at night. Palantir was able to swallow these costs relying on two things: the capital thickness propped up by astronomical contract amounts, and the mentality of "treating pilots like VC bets"—it's okay if most bets go to zero, the few that win must earn it all back.

Commercialization also died once first. The first enterprise-facing product, Metropolis, received a dismal market response; it wasn't until the second attempt, Foundry, that they broke ground. The turning point was Airbus: in the Toulouse factory, a fuel pump failure on the A380 kept recurring, and Airbus's own engineers had investigated for a full two years without a clue. Palantir's people came in, plugged sensor data into the platform, and solved the case in two weeks—fuel was sloshing away from the pump during aircraft climbs. An insignificant fix saved orders reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars.

Airbus's head of digital transformation, Marc Fontaine, later publicly lamented: "For the same problem, it would have taken us 24 months before." (Source in Appendix C) From then on, Airbus became Palantir's most passionate evangelist in Europe, building its entire Skywise data platform on it, connecting over 10,000 aircraft and more than 50,000 users.

### Eruption: Bootcamps Ignite a Business Empire

In 2023, generative AI erupted. All enterprises wanted to "use AI", and all enterprises were stuck in the same place: they didn't know how to use it. Palantir's answer was to compress its twenty years of on-site methodology into an industrialized machine—the AIP Bootcamp.

The rules were extremely simple: clients come with real data and real problems, accompanied by Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers, and in one to five days, they build a deployable AI application prototype, for free or a nominal fee. Day 0 locks in an extremely focused core battlefield; Day 1 connects data and builds the ontology model; Days 2 to 3, engineers and the client's technical staff write code back-to-back, integrating LLMs into the business flow; Days 4 to 5, executives operate the system themselves and make the final decision on the spot.

The throughput and conversion rate of this machine left the entire industry dumbfounded: starting from less than a hundred pilots in 2022, doubling year over year, reaching a peak of nearly 6 per day in 2025; enterprise software sales cycles of 9 to 12 months were compressed to weeks; pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to 4,000 stores in eight months via bootcamps; after clients like market research firm J.D. Power learned the ropes, they started hosting bootcamps for their own clients—the model began to self-reproduce. The contract rhythm post-bootcamp was so fast that peers might not even believe it: a large healthcare company signed a five-year, $26 million ARR deal five weeks after a bootcamp; a global bank piloted for a month, signed a $2 million deal, and expanded to a three-year, $19 million ARR contract four months later. (Source in Appendix C)

Financial results exploded accordingly: U.S. commercial revenue in Q4 2025 grew 137% year-over-year; the "Rule of 40" reached 127%, hitting 145% in Q1 2026; single-quarter signings were $4.26 billion, with net revenue retention at 139%; market capitalization once broke through $400 billion. The Wall Street analysts who mocked its "human wave tactics" began rewriting their research reports overnight.

The biggest vote of confidence came from the U.S. Navy. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Karp jointly announced a "Shipbuilding Operating System" contract: $448 million, first using Foundry and AIP to transform the submarine industrial base—two massive shipyards, three naval shipyards, and 100 suppliers. The pilot data was mentioned earlier: production scheduling from 160 hours to 10 minutes, material review from weeks to one hour. The Secretary of the Navy specifically declared "this is not a concept, not a pilot, not a study"—it skipped the PoC and went straight into production, because early embedded pilots had already proven the value. In the same year, the U.S. Army also bundled 75 fragmented service contracts into a 10-year, $10 billion framework agreement with Palantir. The government's trust is the compound interest amassed from twenty years of on-site presence. (Source in Appendix C)

An engineer who once spent a full year on-site in Toulouse, Nabeel Qureshi, left an even more vivid footnote: he spent four days a week soaking in the final assembly plant, alongside manufacturing workers, writing software for the A350 production line—he called the thing an "Asana for building planes". A Silicon Valley engineer living in a foreign factory for a whole year, this is the most unadorned literal meaning of the words "Forward Deployed". (Source in Appendix C)

### Methodology Restored

Looking back at the first seven chapters, the Palantir case is practically an index for the whole book: the demo cycle and "clients don't know what they want" (Chapter 2); customization as product discovery, the gravel road and the highway (Chapters 1, 7); the strategic value of lighthouse clients (Chapter 3); the portfolio investment logic of free pilots (Chapter 6); on-site tools growing bottom-up into platforms (Chapter 7); and the most fundamental tenet—complexity cannot be eliminated remotely, someone must be present (Whole book).

## 8.2 OpenAI: When the Creators of ChatGPT Start Getting Their Hands Dirty

### The Turning Point: From "Model as Product" to "Deployment as Strategy"

OpenAI's first seven years were a pure technical epic: the GPT series surged all the way, and ChatGPT set the record for the fastest user growth in human history. In such a company narrative, "enterprise delivery" sounds like something from next door's industry.

The turning point occurred in 2024. Data from the enterprise market illustrated an undeniable fact: the subsequently widely known "95% failure rate" was playing out; meanwhile, the market share landscape of enterprise large models shifted drastically—industry tracking showed Anthropic climbing to about 32% in the enterprise market, while OpenAI fell back from its absolute lead of about 50% in early years to about 25%. The gap in model capabilities was narrowing, and the criterion by which enterprise clients voted with real money quietly changed to another question: **"Who can help me actually put this thing to use?"**

OpenAI's response was in two steps. Step one, assembling the Forward Deployed Engineering team in 2024—starting with 2 engineers led by Colin Jarvis, quickly expanding to a global network spanning San Francisco, New York, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, and Sydney, growing from 2 to 52 people in one year. The job description was unapologetic: "embedded into clients where model performance is critical, delivery is urgent, and ambiguity is the default state," with up to 50% travel. The Head of Europe, Fournier, put it more bluntly: "Market demand exceeded our expectations." (Source in Appendix C)

Step two, on May 11, 2026, establishing a "Deployment Company"—a joint venture controlled by OpenAI and partnered with 19 top-tier capital firms (led by TPG, co-led by Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, participated in by Goldman Sachs, SoftBank, Warburg Pincus, with Bain & Company, Capgemini, and McKinsey as founding partners), with an initial investment of over $4 billion and a pre-money valuation reported by media of about $10 billion; contemporaneously acquiring applied AI consulting firm Tomoro, assimilating roughly 150 deployment engineers as a structured unit. COO Brad Lightcap personally took command.

The structural design of this new company is worth writing into business school textbooks, because it welded "where do the clients come from" and "how does the capital return" together. The funders are private equity giants, and these giants hold hundreds of portfolio companies spanning healthcare, manufacturing, finance, retail, and logistics—these are the ready-made client pool for the deployment company; in exchange, OpenAI reportedly promised these PE funders a 17.5% annualized guaranteed return over five years (reported by media, unconfirmed officially by OpenAI), while retaining super-voting rights and controlling strategic direction.

The acquired Tomoro was no nobody either: the engineers of this British applied AI consultancy were previously doing enterprise rollouts at Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and gaming company Supercell. In a single day, OpenAI gathered all three cards: "engineers, channels, and capital". (Source in Appendix C)

A company valued at hundreds of billions of dollars, wielding the most powerful models known to humanity, spinning off a separate ten-billion-dollar company just to "send people to client sites to do the work"—this is the heaviest weight ever placed on the scales in the history of the FDE model.

### On-Site: John Deere's Farmland

The best display window of OpenAI's FDE methodology is its collaboration with John Deere. This nearly two-hundred-year-old agricultural giant wanted to solve a problem growing out of the soil: weed control. The traditional approach is whole-field spraying of herbicides—high costs, heavy chemical damage, and large environmental tolls. The ideal of precision agriculture is "spray only when you see the weed", but the difficulty of recognition lies in: every field's weed composition is different, crop growth is different, and seasons are different.

OpenAI's Forward Deployed Team's approach was a complete demonstration of the methodologies from Chapters 2 and 4 of this book: fly to Iowa, follow agronomy experts into the fields, and understand the workflows and constraints of precision agronomy (including that non-negotiable deadline—the farming season); review hundreds of real field operation cases alongside experts, coding "what makes a good spraying recommendation" into a custom evaluation system; rapidly iterate the model and system under the care of this evaluation system. The final result: a reduction in chemical usage by up to 70%, and a 6x increase in farmer engagement frequency.

Three noteworthy details are hidden in this case. First, the deadline was the farming season, not a project schedule—the rhythm of FDE must yield to the client's business rhythm. Second, the evaluation system preceded model optimization—define "good" first, then pursue "good". Third, the 70% figure was not retrofitted promotional spin; it was the target set jointly with experts before work began.

### On-Site: BBVA's 120,000 People

Another benchmark is Banco Bilbao Vizcaya Argentaria (BBVA). The starting point of the collaboration was not stunning—deploying ChatGPT Enterprise. But the compound interest of the FDE model lies here: the starting point is a tool, but the path is organizational. Starting from 3,300 accounts, employees spontaneously created over 20,000 custom assistants, with 83% active weekly usage, saving an average of about 3 hours per week; a year and a half later, the deployment expanded to a system covering 25 countries and 120,000 employees, and the bank's goal correspondingly upgraded to "building an AI-native global bank". From "giving a tool" to "co-building an organizational form", this is the ultimate manifestation of "cultivating existing accounts" in Chapter 6. (Source in Appendix C)

### Methodology Restored

The revelation of the OpenAI case lies in the "self-awareness of the model company": when model capabilities converge, deployment capability becomes the main battleground of differentiation; when the product form is undecided (no one knows what an agent should look like), the on-site environment becomes the only site for product discovery. Its three-stage work method (co-creation, validation, delivery), its engineering obsession with evaluation systems, and the capital structure innovation of its deployment company (using the industrial networks of PE capital as client channels), correspond respectively to the core propositions of Chapters 4, 6, and 3 of this book.

## 8.3 Anthropic and the Vertical Big Three: Three Variations of FDE

If Palantir invented the model and OpenAI put it on the front pages, then Anthropic and a batch of vertical AI companies show three variations of FDE in different soils.

### Variation One: Anthropic - Safety DNA and the Differentiation of "Auditability"

Anthropic's FDEs fall under the Applied AI team, and its job descriptions have two intriguing qualifiers: first is "safety and reliability"—all deliveries must meet Anthropic's safety standards; second is "defend the company's mission on the front lines"—FDEs are simultaneously ambassadors of values. In 2025, this team expanded at a rate of 5x in a year. Leader Kate De Jong explained: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are completely two different species." (Source in Appendix C)

The direct product of this differentiation is that "auditability" became a product feature. The collaboration with fintech giant FIS is a benchmark: Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineers embedded with FIS to co-design financial crime detection agents, compressing anti-money laundering investigations from hours to minutes, with every decision fully replayable and traceable, first deployed at Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank. In highly regulated industries, "every AI decision can be clearly explained to regulators" is not a bonus point, it's an entry pass—Anthropic turned it into a selling point.

The evidence of "safety paranoia" becoming a killer app in the enterprise market is written in client evaluations. Cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networks deployed Claude to 2,500 developers; the engineering director's feedback was direct: "They care more about safety than anyone else—every meeting is about safety implications. As the largest cybersecurity company, this is very important to us." The result: feature development speed increased by 20% to 30%, and junior developers completed integration tasks 70% faster. Pharma giant Novo Nordisk uses it to write clinical study documents: reports that took a dozen weeks now take ten minutes to draft. Meanwhile, it partnered with consulting giant Accenture to train 30,000 Claude specialized consultants, and its enterprise client count surged from under a thousand to over 300,000 within two years. (Source in Appendix C)

A deeper layer of differentiation lies in the official promise of "knowledge transfer": the explicit goal of embedding is to enable FIS to "independently build and scale their own agents in the future". Putting "teaching the client" into the contract might look like weakening their own irreplaceability in the short term, but long term it is the most forceful response to the skepticism of "vendor lock-in"—Gartner predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon such solutions due to vendor costs and skills hollowing out, and Anthropic's approach is defusing this time bomb in advance.

In May 2026, it was revealed that Anthropic was forming an enterprise AI services joint venture with Blackstone Group and others, reportedly scaled at around $1.5 billion, focusing on embedding Claude into the operations of mid-market enterprises—facing off against OpenAI's deployment company on the exact same day. Two model giants completing the organizational formalization of "deployment as strategy" on the same day is a fact that speaks louder to FDE's coordinates than any analysis.

### Variation Two: Sierra and Decagon - The Startups' "Delivery is the Product"

Sierra (founded in 2023 by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor and former Google VP Clay Bavor, reaching a valuation of $4.5 billion upon funding in October 2024, and reportedly climbing since) turned the FDE model into its business model itself: clients don't pay for software, they pay for "resolved conversations"; implementations are managed by Sierra's Forward Deployed Team, and clients only need to contribute brand tone and operational rules. The effect: brand clients like Sonos, Casper, WeightWatchers, and ADT gathered; the fastest publicly reported go-live time is four weeks, and the annual contract threshold generally starts in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Pricing based on outcomes naturally anchors Sierra's pricing to client value—the most radical real-world sample of "outcome-based pricing" from Chapter 6.

Sierra's internal naming for this role is also worth noting: they are not called FDEs, but "Agent Engineers". Their lead Natalie Mellin (formerly 5 years at Palantir, covering law enforcement, defense, and infrastructure clients) explained: the name should describe "the shape of the technical work", not just the "obsession with the client"—building conversational agents requires a special kind of "taste" (what sounds human, what sounds right), which is a narrower and deeper craft than a general FDE. The partnership with ticketing platform Vivid Seats is her team's brightest battle report: live in four weeks, self-service resolution rate up 40%, CSAT up 35%—and the mechanism of conversational data feeding back into the product meant that "ten thousand people asking for the same feature in a month" gets prioritized immediately. (Source in Appendix C)

Decagon (co-founder Ashwin Srinivas came from Palantir, total funding $231 million) took another path: productizing delivery experience directly into agent operating flows—allowing clients' operations personnel to define multi-step workflows in natural language, which the AI then deterministically executes. Client roster: Notion, Duolingo, Eventbrite, Rippling. Reportedly, Chime reached a 70% self-service resolution rate, Duolingo's issue deflection rate hit 80%, and ClassPass's customer service costs dropped by 95%. Decagon's FDEs play more the role of "the person who teaches the client's team how to use this system"—the goal of delivery is to give non-technical people on the client side self-service capabilities, which is the best practice for "Can it be used by people who don't write code" from the "Four Questions of Productization" in Chapter 7. (Source in Appendix C)

### Variation Three: Harvey - Breaking into Places Normal Software Can't Enter

The legal industry is the "Bermuda Triangle" of enterprise software: top-tier law firms have strict confidentiality disciplines, data never leaves the premises, and partners each run their own fiefdoms—self-serve SaaS struggles to win a single victory here. Harvey (founded in 2022, valuation reaching $11 billion after a new funding round in March 2026) forced its way in using the FDE model.

First, remember this company's origins: two founders, one a litigator from a law firm, the other a scientist from DeepMind (Google's AI lab); a $5 million seed round came from the OpenAI Startup Fund. (Source in Appendix C)

Reviewing its playbook, it's almost a perfect medley of the methodologies in this book. Lighthouse strategy—its first major client locked in global top-tier firm Allen & Overy (February 2023, 3,500 lawyers, 43 offices), followed closely by PwC (over 4,000 legal professionals), Cleary Gottlieb, and Macfarlanes; sponsor management—Allen & Overy's internal point of contact David Wakeling (Head of Markets Innovation) became the in-firm pivot of the deployment; depth rhythm—entering via a single practice group, pushed by sponsor partners, expanding laterally after six months of live combat; client research loop—partners pay but don't do the work, paralegals do the work but don't pay, knowledge management lawyers see the blind spots of both, so the FDE interview system must penetrate all three layers simultaneously, "the hardest part of deployment isn't the model, it's this client research loop"; ultra-long cycles—a single firm deployment takes six to nine months, with FDEs responsible for everything from document management system data pipelines all the way to partner-by-partner adoption demos.

The result is a ferocious growth momentum rarely seen in vertical AI: over 100,000 lawyer users across 1,300 organizations, covering the majority of the Am Law 100; Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR) grew from about $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026; valuation jumped four times in a year—$3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, $11 billion. (Source in Appendix C)

Harvey proved where the boundaries of the FDE model lie: **in any market where the three mountains of "confidentiality regimes, data residency, and key user autonomy" exist simultaneously, productized delivery is unsolvable; only when people go in, can they bring the system in with them.** And such markets—legal, healthcare, finance, government—happen to be the most profitable enterprise service markets on Earth.

## 8.4 China Cases: Planting FDEs in the Land of "Project-Based" Business

China is the world's most peculiar enterprise software market: here are the most hardworking delivery engineers, the most demanding customization requirements, and the deepest "curse of the project-based model"—large enterprises demand customizations, vendors lose money on every deal they win, costs cannot be amortized, they degenerate into IT outsourcing for Party A, and professional hollowing out occurs. The fate of the FDE model on this land is a question that must be answered separately: is it the antidote to the curse, or a new vest for it?

To see clearly how twisted this situation is, listen to the sighs of three practitioners. The first comes from database company DolphinDB, in a long industry piece asking "Who is Destroying China's Enterprise Software Industry?", with the answer being six mountains: freeloading, open source, outsourcing, bidding, state-owned digital tech subsidiaries, and deformed market structures—the most top-tier Chinese engineering talent has long been consumed in the non-standard deliveries of "high customization, strong relationships, price wars". The second sigh comes from an enterprise software veteran on the podcast "Indie Hackers": "Customization is the natural enemy of SaaS; this curse can only dissipate naturally when the market matures." The third comes from a former President of DingTalk: many companies get stuck at one or two hundred million in revenue—hard to acquire clients, hard to deliver, hard to maintain, high customization costs, economies of scale are nowhere to be found. 36Kr summarized these three sighs: "Software productization amortizes project-based R&D costs, SaaS amortizes sales costs—only project-based companies suffer the most; a project's costs are void the moment you leave the site, they can't be amortized." (Source in Appendix C) These three sighs are the Chinese questions the FDE model must answer.

### The Big Tech Path: Volcengine's Doubao FDEs

Volcengine, under ByteDance, is the player among China's internet giants that most explicitly waves the FDE banner: in 2026, it specifically established an FDE team to go deep into industries and co-create profoundly with benchmark clients. Volcengine President Tan Dai's phrasing in a public interview almost echoes verbatim the definitions in this book: "FDEs are not sales, nor pre-sales; they must have strong technical landing capabilities, especially for AI code landing." He also revealed that the team intentionally configures diverse industry backgrounds (e.g., having someone with a bioengineering background connect with the biopharma industry), and has currently covered key industries like automotive, healthcare, education, finance, and semiconductors. In July 2026, Volcengine signed a strategic partnership with consulting giant EY; the two plan to build a thousand-person FDE team to "explore a new full-stack AI delivery model." (Source in Appendix C)

The innate advantage of Big Tech doing FDE is their thick platform foundations (Volcano Ark provides an integrated foundation of models, toolchains, and cloud infrastructure); the innate challenge is the inertia of Big Tech sales systems and the financial inertia of "delivery is a cost center"—whether the FDE team can attain the organizational status of Palantir's "on-site equals R&D" determines whether they are true FDEs, or just a Pre-Sales Support Department with a new name.

### The Pioneers' Path: Putting "FDE Does Not Equal On-Site Outsourcing" on the Website

More worthy of attention is a batch of local startups. They lack the foundation of Big Tech, yet they use the clearest language to differentiate FDE from the old species of the Chinese market. A service provider focusing on the real estate and facility management industry wrote on its website the most refined defining distinction for FDEs currently in China:

> One: FDE delivers by stages and accepts by results; on-site outsourcing settles by man-hours. Two: FDE brings a product foundation to do engineering; on-site outsourcing writes and modifies from scratch on the spot. Three: FDE leaves after finishing, with capabilities deposited in the system and your team; on-site outsourcing stays longer and longer, and when the people leave, the system stops.

Three sentences, corresponding respectively to three core propositions of this book: outcome-based pricing (Chapter 6), platform foundation (Chapter 7), and knowledge transfer (Chapter 5). It also established a "dissuasion mechanism"—won't take it if the scenario is unverified, won't take it if the data can be exported in a single spreadsheet, won't take it if they just want to understand AI. "We would rather you start a bit later, than have you start at the wrong time." This is a conscious practice of "rejecting expensive PoC graveyards" from Chapter 2 in the Chinese market.

Another service provider brought the FDE model into the highly compliance-sensitive financial industry, and distilled a clear three-stage methodology: scenario diagnosis for 1-2 weeks (identifying the 3-5 core scenarios with the highest value, quantifying ROI) -> embedded delivery for 8-16 weeks (full-stack delivery from model selection to production integration) -> production deployment and continuous evolution (used daily by business units, continuously iterating with the business), while emphasizing "data does not leave the domain"—this is a local adaptation to the regulatory environment of Chinese financial institutions. (Source in Appendix C)

In June 2026, a practitioner from a domestic investment firm published a long article online, with a striking title: "The Hottest Job in Silicon Valley This Year, FDE: We've Been Doing It With Our Heads Down for Three Years". The article recorded their true physical sense of applying this playbook in Chinese enterprise services: the Forward Deployed Engineer "dives into one client after signing, capabilities unconstrained, truly installing AI into their business"; the metric "is not how much code was written or how many documents delivered, but whether this system is actually being used by anyone, and whether the client's business has improved"; and that summary perfectly consistent with this book—"going live doesn't count as finished; they must stay until this system becomes the client's routine, until the client's own people can take over". This article was heavily forwarded in domestic practitioner circles because it first clarified a comparison chart in Chinese: product engineers are responsible for the roadmap, pre-sales for the signing, implementation for the sign-off sheet, customer success for renewals—but the FDE is responsible for "whether the client's conversion and human efficiency actually increased". (Source in Appendix C)

### The Control Group: Set-Top Boxes and Paper Contracts

On who FDE should and shouldn't invest in, Baidao Data CEO Wu Guangyu gave two starkly contrasting clients at an entrepreneur roundtable.

One was a set-top box client, whose needs were extremely clear—"how to use AI in a set-top box". FDEs soaked with them, and finally built an Agent that watches dramas and gossips with you, with a very high ROI. Another was an outbound client; the boss opened by offering to invest 1 million USD in AI, but when signing the contract, he printed it out every time and made revisions with a pen, not even using Word's track changes. Wu Guangyu's judgment was direct: "For a client with zero digitalization foundation like this, throwing FDEs in will definitely yield no output. We told him directly, buy 100 Gemini accounts first and get everyone using it; come back when you have a specific idea."

His conclusion exactly matches the rejection mechanism in Chapter 2: **The premise of an FDE is that the client has a specific pain point.** Do not lightly invest in vague, boundaryless demands; they can be broken down into phases one, two, and three, doing the clear parts first, and once results are delivered, subsequent needs will naturally convert into long-term service contracts. (Source in Appendix C)

### The Other Side: Vacuum Layers and Dirty Work

However, if you think China's FDE practices are all as refreshing as the above, frontline practitioners will hand you a glass of cold water.

"Growth Hacker AI Weekly" recorded frontline executor Shen Yue's observations: in many domestic projects, FDEs look more like vendor outsourced labor, doing the dirty and exhausting work of filling holes and wiping rears. In a tens-of-millions-RMB state-owned enterprise project she personally experienced, the big boss vanished after signing the contract, no one was truly responsible for the project, and the FDEs were suspended in a vacuum layer—no decision-makers above, no executors below, ultimately relying on researching "reporting logic" to fight for sign-off. In another private home improvement company in Guangdong, the second-generation factory boss opened straight to the point: help her cut the staff from 6,000 to 3,000. But what AI can replace are tasks, not jobs; the project trudged forward amidst constant scolding.

Shen Yue's summary is worth copying here: the difficulties of China's FDEs lie in the isolation layer on the demand side, the weak digitalization foundation of enterprises, and the lack of patience from bosses—and all the overpromises made by front-end sales to sell the product end up having to be paid back by the FDEs. She also had a sentence I quoted as the epigraph of Chapter 4: "No matter how technology evolves, the human nature and power games within an organization are always a more complex puzzle than technology." (Source in Appendix C)

### Another Voice: Is FDE a Good Business?

Doubts don't only come from the execution layer. At the same roundtable, Kuse.ai founder Wu Xiankun questioned the FDE model itself, using strong wording: "It's very hard for FDE to become a good business model." His reasoning was direct: if you do it shallowly, the model vendors will do it themselves; if you do it deeply, it's essentially still outsourcing, with high delivery costs that are hard to scale. "Providing services for state-owned enterprises, you can't refuse out-of-scope demands; where is your leverage? Will you doubt yourself in the middle of the night that you've become cheap outsourcing—this is a problem everyone doing FDE must face."

His solution was quite radical: AI Roll-up (consolidation and M&A). Since clients worry about data leaks and teams loathe outsourcing betraying their original intentions, then just buy the company, everyone becomes a shareholder, interests align, and flip the whole company bottom-up; after modifying it, use the same methodology to acquire the next one. His logic is: if AI transformation really has that much value, then you should own the business, rather than sell man-hours. (Source in Appendix C)

This question might not have a standard answer, but it forms a fascinating echo with the Gartner prediction quoted in Chapter 5—that 70% of enterprises might abandon FDE-led solutions by 2028: **The FDE model's greatest enemy may not be technology, but its own cost structure.** This book doesn't intend to give a standard answer to this problem, but it's a question everyone wanting to enter the field should figure out first.

### Three Special Propositions for China's FDEs

Synthesizing these practices and industry observations, for FDE to be viable in China, it must answer three questions its American peers do not have to answer.

First, the pricing dilemma. The U.S. market can accept subscriptions, usage-based billing, or even outcome-based revenue sharing; Chinese clients' muscle memory is "buyout plus project-based sign-off". A viable transitional form is a "hybrid model": phase-based sign-off payments (conforming to habits) + value metrics written into sign-off terms (injecting the outcome gene) + annual maintenance and evolution contracts (nurturing renewal awareness). Copying a one-step jump to pay-per-resolution will die in the procurement departments of most industries.

Second, the platform dilemma. The economics of FDE rely on "platform foundation plus on-site customization", whereas the problem for a massive number of software companies in China is exactly "having the on-site, but no platform"—every deal is a manpower business writing code from scratch. For these companies, what's more important than imitating the form of FDE is shoring up the foundation of FDE: first consolidate the highest-frequency customizations into reusable components, then talk about forward deployment. Without the replication leverage of Chapter 7, FDE in China will only degenerate into "on-site outsourcing with a nice name"—this is exactly why local pioneers wrote "bring a product foundation" into their definitions.

Third, the talent dilemma and the talent opportunity. Chinese engineering culture—able to endure hardship, fast response, full-stack capability, accustomed to solving everything on the client site—is exactly what most closely aligns with FDE's requirements; this is the "opportunity". But the "dilemma" is that over the past twenty years, this type of talent has been trapped in the man-day priced outsourcing system, and market price signals have long undervalued them. The popularization of the FDE concept in China might bring a profound side effect: **re-pricing hundreds of thousands of delivery engineers in China**. When they know their peers across the ocean are making a median annual salary of $385,000, the value coordinates of this industry will loosen.

Veteran enterprise software companies are also corroborating this judgment. SAP Greater China President Yuan Xin mentioned on a podcast that the roles of Forward Deployed Engineers and traditional ERP consultants are converging, and what's most lacking right now is "hybrid talent with both product engineering capabilities and deep business understanding". She also offered a reminder worth remembering: the biggest challenge for AI landing is organizational inertia, not technology—the historical debt of underlying data architecture will not automatically disappear just because AI has arrived. (Source in Appendix C)

### Judgment

FDE will not cure all the chronic illnesses of China's enterprise software, but it provides a rare opportunity for "rectification of names": redefining "close to the client" from a mark of low-end outsourcing to a mark of high-end capability. What the Chinese market lacks is not engineers willing to go to the site, but platforms, methodologies, and pricing structures that allow on-site work to generate compound interest. Whoever fills these three gaps first will be able to grow China's Palantir in this largest and most complex enterprise services market in the world—or grow some new species that hasn't even been named yet.

## 8.5 An AI Startup Team's 180 Days: A Full FDE Combat Retrospective

This final case comes from a Chinese AI startup team I have been tracking and researching (anonymized at the team's request, referred to as "Company N"). It has no Palantir platform and no OpenAI halo, but its 180-day journey fully demonstrates how the methodologies of this book operate on a resource-constrained team. To protect commercial information, numbers have been blurred, but proportions and decision logic remain true.

### Days 1 to 30: Choosing the Battlefield, Not Grabbing Contracts

Company N does enterprise knowledge base AI, and had three potential client leads on hand: a top-tier brokerage firm (huge budget, grandiose needs), a regional chain retail group (medium budget, specific pain points), and a top-tier AAA hospital (high influence, sensitive data). The sales instinct was to pounce on the brokerage. But following the methods of Chapters 2 and 3, the team did a triple check and pre-entry due diligence: the brokerage's problem was a "universe-level need" (the first meeting demanded company-wide coverage), with no clear business owner; the hospital's pain points were real, but data exit restrictions made the delivery cycle uncontrollable; the retail group had its CFO personally tracking the project, with specific pain points ("regional managers simply can't keep up with the operational audit reports for 3,000 stores"), and though the data foundation was messy, it was accessible.

The team chose the retail group—the lighthouse value wasn't the highest, but it passed all three checks. This is the discipline of Section 3.1: **Scheduling is driven by strategic value and learning value, not by contract amount.**

### Days 31 to 75: Entry, Shadow Working, and a "Scrapped Mega-Plan"

Two engineers and an industry consultant entered the site. For the first two weeks, they didn't write a single line of product code; they did shadow working: following regional managers on store tours, watching how they chased store data in WeChat groups and how they did weekly reports in spreadsheets. The critical discovery came from a "workaround": the regional managers never looked at the reports in the company's data system; the data source they truly trusted was a spreadsheet manually filled out by store managers every day—because "the system data is three days late and always wrong".

This discovery overturned the initial mega-plan (an intelligent data analytics platform). The team narrowed the Minimum Viable Deployment to a small incision: **only building a "Store Anomaly Daily Report"**—every morning at 8 AM, compiling the anomaly signals (sales fluctuations, inventory anomalies, complaint surges) from 3,000 stores yesterday into a daily report readable within 3 minutes, pushed to the regional managers' WeChat. Real data, single pain point, six-week deadline.

### Days 76 to 120: The Hidden War of Activation

System go-live was only the beginning. The activation phase met two textbook obstacles. The first was trust calibration: in the first week, the AI misjudged the promotion peaks of two stores as "anomalous surges", and the managers complained in the group. The team didn't argue; within 48 hours they integrated the "promotional calendar" into the logic, and—the key move—publicly thanked the manager who reported the error in the group. The second was influencer management: a senior regional director (an uncrowned king type) initially watched coldly; the team invited him to participate in revising the evaluation standards, and his three pieces of experience were written into the system rules. Two weeks later, he became the system's hardest-working evangelist.

By Day 90, the natural open rate of the daily report stabilized above 85%. By Day 120, the retail group's CIO proactively showcased this system at the quarterly operations meeting—internal supporters were armed into salespeople.

### Days 121 to 180: The First Brick of Replication

There was no suspense in the renewal negotiation, but the team made two more important moves. First, they deposited the assets repeatedly used in this delivery: the retail industry data integration components, the "anomaly signal" evaluation framework, and the store scenario due diligence checklist—the first scenario playbook took shape. Second, they used the retail group's case (with the client's consent for a joint release) to knock on the door of their second client—the channel management department of an FMCG brand, with isomorphic scenarios. The delivery cycle for the second project was 40% shorter than the first: the flywheel of replication completed its first rotation.

180 days, a small team, no funding news, no disruptive technology. But it verified the simplest conclusion of this whole book: **Right problem + Real data + Close-knit service + Discipline of accumulation = A snowball that can roll.**

Five sets of cases have been told. From intelligence agencies to Iowa farmlands, from top-tier law firms to Chinese chain retail, the wider the span of scenarios, the clearer the commonalities of the methodology.
