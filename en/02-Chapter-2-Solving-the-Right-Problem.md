# Chapter 2 Solving the Right Problem

> "On the wrong problem, all execution is a waste."

## 2.1 Autopsy Report of the Proof of Concept Graveyard

In Silicon Valley, there is a term called "POC purgatory". Projects that enter it can't get out: they aren't dead enough to be declared failures, but not alive enough to warrant increased investment. So they appear in quarterly reports year after year as "continuously advancing," like a room full of patients on life support.

Researchers at MIT conducted a systematic autopsy of this graveyard. They summarized the top five roadblocks on the path to scaling enterprise AI projects, ranked by frequency of occurrence:

1. Employees are unwilling to use new tools—ironically, these same people use ChatGPT extensively in private every day.
2. Concerns about the quality of the model's output.
3. Terrible user experience.
4. Lack of executive support.
5. Difficulties in change management.

Please note what is absent from this list: models not being smart enough, computing power not being cheap enough, technology not being advanced enough—none of these are on the list. What kills these projects almost entirely happens at the level of "problem definition" and "organizational reality," not at the technical level.

There is a detail repeatedly cited in the report that is worth discussing separately. An enterprise spent $50,000 on a professional contract analysis tool with an extensive list of features. But a senior lawyer at the company just wouldn't use it—she continued to use the free ChatGPT to draft contracts. The reason was simple: the purchased tool produced summaries that were too rigid and couldn't be customized to her habits. The procurement department's reports stated "deployed," but the real daily situation was that the official system was idling while employees bypassed it. (Source in Appendix C) This detail reveals the true cause of the first roadblock: it's not that employees are conservative; it's that consumer-grade products have spoiled their appetites—people who use AI extensively at home every day cannot tolerate "artificial idiocy" in the office.

There is another, more piercing contrast: **the success rate of projects in collaboration with external professional vendors is about twice that of internally built ones**. Why do external teams have a higher win rate? It's not because they are smarter, but because they can't afford to lose—for those who charge based on results, the cost of defining the wrong problem is paid out of their own pockets. An internal project at a Japanese company serves as a typical negative example: they pulled three or four of their best engineers to form a task force; the demos were stunning, the leaders nodded in approval, yet from day one, no one could answer the question, "By what standard is the value of this system measured?" Half a year later, the project quietly disappeared from reporting materials. No one announced its failure; it just died a natural death. (Source in Appendix C)

This is the first principle of this chapter: **On the wrong problem, all execution is a waste; and the density of wrong problems within an enterprise far exceeds imagination**. This chapter focuses on only one thing: before writing the first line of code, how to ensure you are solving the right problem.

## 2.2 PSF: Finding the Problem-Solution Fit

In Internet startup methodology, there is a core concept called PMF (Product-Market Fit): if the product is right, the market will drive growth on its own. In the world of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), the corresponding unit is not "product and market" but "problem and solution"—which I call PSF (Problem-Solution Fit).

The difference is subtle yet critical. PMF asks "Does my product have a market that wants it?", viewing things from the supply side; PSF asks "Is this specific problem of the client worth solving, and can it be solved by our capabilities?", viewing things from the demand side. Within an enterprise client, there might be hundreds of opportunities where "AI could do something," but those truly worth pursuing must simultaneously pass three tests.

The first test, the pain point check: Is this problem a specific pain for a specific person? Note the two "specifics." "Improving customer service efficiency" is not a pain point; it's a direction. "The customer service supervisor has to spend three hours every Monday morning manually compiling last week's escalation tickets from four different systems, while her real job should be analyzing the reasons for the escalations"—this is a pain point. McGrew provided an even sharper standard: solve one of the top five problems the CEO cares about most. The reason is highly practical: only problems of this magnitude can help you crush bureaucratic resistance within the enterprise (Chapter 3 will expand on how to use this to overcome the IT department barrier). The standard from Sierra's head of agent engineering, Moeller, is another articulation of the same principle: only accept two types of problems—those that are truly hard, and those that have a real business impact. Hard without impact is just showing off; impact without being hard won't fall to you.

The second test, the economic check: How much is solving this problem worth? Many pain points are real pains, but they aren't worth money; projects that can't calculate this ROI won't survive the next budget season even if they are executed. You can do a rough calculation: How many person-hours does this problem consume every week? How much labor cost does that equate to? How much is compensated for a single error? What can the freed-up personnel go do? Palantir's bootcamps simply bring this test forward—clients must lock in a "core battlefield" and provide quantitative targets before starting, such as "reduce scheduling conflicts by 30%" or "shorten inventory turnover by 15%." A widely cited finding in that report perfectly illustrates how easily this test is skipped: over half of enterprise AI budgets are poured into front-office sales and marketing, yet the returns are concentrated in back-office areas like contract review, procurement, and risk control—the "unsexy" places. Everyone is solving "problems that demo well" rather than "problems that are valuable."

The third test, the feasibility check: With our current capabilities and the client's data reality, how well can we do? This test is the most easily drowned out by enthusiasm. There are two questions that must be answered on-site: Where is the data, and in what state is it? The answers are often worse than imagined—scattered across seven systems, three versions that don't match, and the most authoritative copy is in a veteran employee's private spreadsheet. Also, what is the accuracy threshold required for this problem? There is an order of magnitude difference in engineering effort between "99% usable" and "90% usable," and in many business scenarios, 90% plus manual review is actually the optimal solution. Judging this requires not technical skills, but an understanding of business consequences.

Only when all three tests are passed have you touched the problem-solution fit. And these three tests must be passed at the client's site—sitting in a headquarters conference room making judgments based on second-hand information is the root of all errors in this chapter.

## 2.3 Rejecting the Expensive "Proof of Concept Graveyard"

In 2025, a Chinese service provider working on enterprise AI deployment wrote a rather ruthless paragraph on its official website to dissuade potential clients: if the scenario hasn't been validated yet, go attend a demo event first; if the data can be exported in a single table, a lightweight service is enough; if just wanting to understand AI, use free methods. The last sentence was the most ruthless: "FDE is a heavy investment. We would rather you start a bit later than have you start at the wrong time." (Source in Appendix C)

This paragraph deserves to be copied onto the walls of everyone doing enterprise AI. It states a truth that goes against sales intuition: **rejecting the wrong projects is the most important profitability capability of the FDE model**.

Why are wrong projects dangerous? Because the cost structure of an FDE is front-loaded—the best engineers, the most expensive travel, the longest on-site commitments all happen before any payment is collected. Once you fall into the mud, it's not a matter of losing a single deal; it's a matter of dragging down an entire elite team and suffering an avalanche of opportunity costs.

Former Palantir engineer Barry recalled that the company once "burned millions of dollars on client pilots, and the profit margins for many projects were literally negative infinity because we did them for free." The perspective he immediately added is the real point: Palantir could afford to burn money because it invested in pilots as a portfolio—like a venture capitalist, it didn't matter if most bets went to zero, as long as the successful ones won everything back. But if you have neither their capital depth nor a mechanism to "turn failed pilots into product assets," then every wrong pilot is pure bleeding. (Source in Appendix C)

Therefore, an FDE team needs a "mechanism of rejection," not just the courage to reject. Three actionable lines of defense:

- **First line of defense: Proof of concepts must have "graduation criteria".** At the launch of every validation project, it should be explicitly stated: after how many weeks, using what metrics, and reaching what numerical values will the project "graduate" into a paid deployment; if not achieved, the two parties part ways decently. Palantir's bootcamps took this logic to the extreme—it's not a proof of concept; it's an industrialized replacement for proof of concepts: one to five days, clients bring real data, an deployable prototype is built on-site, and executives make a decision on the spot. Either see real results in a few days, or don't start at all. Traditional POCs devolve into graveyards precisely because they are "indefinite, metric-less, and referee-less."

- **Second line of defense: Watch out for three high-risk signals.** Synthesizing practitioners' experiences, if two or more of these three signals appear, you should be on high alert. First is "no ownership of the land"—the project lacks a clear business owner within the company and is only interfacing with the IT department; the IT department cares about compliance and stability, and compliance and stability are never reasons to do new projects. Second is "look but don't touch"—the client asks you to prove your capabilities first but refuses to provide real data; validation without real data is destined to produce self-deceiving false positives. Third is "universe-scale requirements"—clients who demand to "cover all scenarios across the company" in the first meeting are usually not ready to do any single scenario.

- **Third line of defense: Leave a graceful exit for "rejection".** Rejecting does not mean severing ties. The best approach is to translate "not doing it now" into "when to do it": "The data foundation for this scenario is still missing three things. We suggest starting with another scenario first, fixing these three things along the way, and coming back to this next quarter." Packaging a rejection as a roadmap preserves both capacity and relationships—"lighthouse client filtering" in Chapter 3 will continue along this line.

## 2.4 Pain Points: The First Mover of Deployment

Where do the right problems come from? Textbooks will tell you: from requirement surveys. The field experience of an FDE will tell you: **from pain. And pain doesn't appear in conference rooms; it only appears in the workplace.**

Palantir wrote this methodology in blood twenty years ago. Look back at the story of the Iraq battlefield in Section 1.2: soldiers needed an early warning tool for roadside bombs—this requirement couldn't have been asked in any interview because the soldiers didn't know they "could ask software for this"; they thought it was just part of patrol life. The pain point was "seen" by the engineers, not "spoken" by the users. The forward deployed engineers accompanied patrol teams on missions, witnessing with their own eyes the convoy's hesitation and fear before suspicious road segments, which led to that crude map tool that changed the battlefield.

This method has a name in anthropology: "participant observation"; in the Toyota Production System, it's called "Genchi Genbutsu" (go and see)—go to the site, see the real things, and get the facts. FDE turned it into an actionable field methodology, which I call the "Shadowing Method."

Follow a real user and live through their real day. Not interviewing them, but sitting next to them watching them work—watching which systems they open, between which spreadsheets they copy and paste, at which steps they frown, and which "official processes" they bypass. The FDE team at OpenAI did exactly this in the John Deere project: they flew to Iowa, went into the fields with agronomy experts and farmers, watching how they made pesticide decisions, what information truly entered those decisions, and how seasonal deadlines dominated everything. That repeatedly quoted practitioner's maxim speaks to the objects of discovery for this method: "The hard part is finding that workflow no one wrote into the documentation, that data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." These three things can only be found on-site.

Focus your observation on "workarounds," not "processes." Official flowcharts tell you how the organization "should run"; workarounds tell you how it "actually runs"—every workaround is an unmet pain point screaming out. Why do employees insist on exporting data into a spreadsheet to recalculate it? Why does everyone in the department agree that "you have to find Xiao Wang for this table"? Why, despite having a data system, is there always someone manually checking numbers before a decision meeting? Workarounds are the organization's scars; under every scar is a system failure, and also an opportunity for an FDE.

Beware of "translated pain points." If the requirements you hear are relayed by the client's IT department, procurement department, or consultants, they become distorted with each handoff. The IT department will translate business pains into technical requirements ("need a data platform"); the procurement department will translate them into compliance items ("need to meet such-and-such standards"). The disasters of traditional software projects often start here: vendors are held responsible for the translation, not the pain point itself. So your first priority is to bypass the translation and reach the nerve endings of the pain directly. This is also the core responsibility of the "Echo" in Palantir's dual model—understanding the client's "mission" rather than their "requirements": requirements are a second-hand narrative of the pain point, while the mission is the first-hand source.

Once you find the real pain point, the next step is to validate with minimum cost: Can our solution really stop this pain?

## 2.5 Validating Value with "Minimum Viable Deployment"

In Internet startup methodology, there is a famous concept called MVP (Minimum Viable Product): validating market demand with the smallest product. The corresponding concept for FDEs is what I call **MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment)**—using the minimum engineering investment, in the client's real environment, against a real pain point, to validate the actual occurrence of value once.

A one-word difference, but the distinction is in the bones. MVP validates "whether this product should be built," and the referee is the market; MVD validates "whether this solution can generate value for this client," and the referee is the specific business of this specific client. A solution validated across ten clients might still fail at the eleventh—different data foundations, different organizational inertia, different shapes of pain points. This is the cruelty of enterprise delivery: **value cannot be inherited; it can only be validated one by one**.

MVD has three iron rules.

- **Rule One: Real data, no exceptions.** Using "desensitized (sensitive info removed) sample data" provided by the client or mock data you construct yourself for validation is the first brick of the proof of concept graveyard. All the devils hide in real data: field meanings that don't match the documentation, 30% null values, coding rules from three years ago, and most fatally—the data itself recording broken processes. **How to implement this rule:** Palantir's bootcamps write "clients must bring their own real business data" into the rules; the John Deere project placed "reviewing hundreds of real operational cases" before modeling. Solutions established on fake data are just carefully crafted self-consolation.

- **Rule Two: Narrow the incision, not the ambition.** A common mistake is understanding MVD as a "castrated grand solution"—cutting 70% of the features and making it neither fish nor fowl. The correct approach is to narrow it down along a different dimension: don't cut value, cut scope. Instead of pursuing "intelligent customer service covering the whole company," pursue "only covering return and exchange tickets, but making it end-to-end with no human intervention"; instead of pursuing "group-wide supply chain optimization," pursue "only handling scheduling conflicts for this one production line, but tangibly saving 20 person-hours every week." The incision should be small enough that the value density is high enough to be visible to the naked eye of the business department, leading to proactive evangelism. The expansion path of the legal AI company Harvey is a textbook example of this playbook: rather than rolling out firm-wide, they first penetrated a single global practice group, turning the first batch of partners into believers, and then expanded horizontally after six months of real combat.

- **Rule Three: Fix a hard deadline to force trade-offs.** The validation cycle for MVD should be measured in "weeks," not "months." Palantir's bootcamps are one to five days; Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live case was four weeks; Decagon's typical deployment is four to eight weeks. The significance of the deadline is not in speed, but in forcing honest trade-offs from both sides: anything that cannot demonstrate value within these few weeks is not yet core value. A six-month "minimum validation" almost inevitably morphs back into an I-want-it-all mega-project—which is just another groundbreaking for the proof of concept graveyard.

### Bootcamps: The Industrialization of MVD

Palantir's AIP Bootcamp, launched in 2023, is worth breaking down separately as a benchmark for "MVD industrialization"—it is currently the only MVD assembly line that has been validated at scale.

First, look at the data: starting from less than a hundred pilots in 2022, the number of sessions has doubled year after year. Media tracking shows that the cumulative number of sessions has long surpassed a thousand, averaging nearly 6 sessions per day at its peak in 2025; the traditional enterprise software sales cycle (nine to twelve months) was compressed to weeks; US commercial revenue grew by 137% year-over-year in Q4 2025, and the company publicly attributed almost all of this growth to this initiative. (Source in Appendix C)

Next, look at the process. It breaks MVD into five standardized actions. Day 0, Preparation: both parties lock onto an extremely focused core battlefield—"optimize scheduling for a specific production line," "reduce inventory turnover days"—rejecting all grand narratives. Day 1, Integration: connect to the client's existing systems, extract isolated data, and build a preliminary ontology model. Days 2 to 3, Build: FDEs and the client's technical staff write code and configure rules back-to-back, integrating large models into the business flow to create an automated workflow that can execute real actions. Days 4 to 5, Demo and Decision: the output is not a report, but a live software interface. Business executives click it themselves, watching the AI provide recommendations based on their own company's data—after the shock, they move straight into commercial negotiations.

The brilliance of the bootcamp is that it simultaneously solves the three classic problems of MVD: the real data problem (the client brings their own), the deadline problem (capped at five days), and the referee problem (executives use it themselves). It also conveniently solves a deeper problem—trust. Letting decision-makers personally operate a system based on their own data beats a hundred-page feasibility report.

How fast are contracts signed after the bootcamp? Palantir disclosed a set of real rhythms on an earnings call, so fast that even peers might not believe it: a large healthcare company attended a bootcamp in December, and five weeks later signed a five-year agreement worth $26 million in annual contract value; a global bank piloted for one month, signed an initial $2 million contract, and four months later expanded it to a three-year agreement worth $19 million annually; the pharmacy chain Walgreens first piloted in 10 stores, improving in-store operational efficiency by 30%, and then rolled it out to 4,000 stores within eight months, with AI-driven end-to-end workflows automatically processing roughly 384 billion decisions every day that otherwise would have been made manually. (Source in Appendix C) These numbers answer "what happens after MVD": projects that pass validation don't grow slowly; they scale by leaps and bounds—clients have already seen the value with their own eyes in those five days, and all that's left is the commercial process.

Of course, duplicating this model has a barrier: there must be a mature platform foundation behind it, otherwise you can't even set up the environment in five days. For teams without a platform, an executable simplified version is the "two-week sprint validation": week one for entering the site, connecting data, and setting metrics; week two for building a prototype that only solves a single-point problem but can run real business logic, demonstrating it to the business side over the weekend and deciding on the spot whether to advance or retreat. The format can be tailored, but the iron rules cannot be compromised.

## 2.6 Should We Accommodate the Client's Existing Environment Early On?

During the MVD phase, there is a divergence that almost every project crashes into: the client's existing technical environment—that pile of legacy systems running for twenty years, small tools built internally by departments, and strict security compliance boundaries—to what extent should our solution accommodate it?

Both factions have a point. The "Accommodation Faction" says: validating within the client's real constraints is true validation. The "Refactoring Faction" says: doing deep adaptation for an old environment that is about to be replaced is wasting a precious validation period on engineering that is destined to be thrown away.

FDE practice offers a middle path: be compatible with old systems on data, but absolutely never accommodate old systems on architecture—what I call "read old, write new."

At the data level, deeply accommodate the old environment. Read the client's data wherever it is—even if it's in a vintage mainframe, in a spreadsheet on a shared drive, or in a private API of some ancient system. Vintage mainframes in finance, and heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in healthcare, have always been the core battlegrounds for deployment work. There are no shortcuts for data read compatibility, because data is the prerequisite for validating value, and data will never move to accommodate your architecture. The good news is that this layer of work is being transformed by AI itself: data mapping, cross-system migration, and extracting data from old systems without APIs, which previously required manual interpretation, can now largely be handed over to agents—for example, using a browser agent to simulate human operations to extract data from a legacy system without APIs. Integration costs are dropping by orders of magnitude. (Source in Appendix C)

At the architecture level, resolutely refuse to be a parasite on the old environment. The system during the validation phase should run within its own controllable boundaries, interacting with old systems through APIs, rather than writing code into the old systems. There are three reasons: there is more than a 50% chance that the validation phase solution itself will be overturned and rewritten; the deeper the parasitism, the greater the waste. Writing to an old system requires going through the client's change management process, with cycles measured in months, which fundamentally conflicts with the weekly rhythm of MVD. Maintaining an "evacuable" posture is itself a negotiating chip and a stance of honesty—FDEs will leave after the job is done, but parasites can never leave.

At the process level, submit to human habits, not system habits. This is the rule that technical teams most easily get backward. Technical environments can be rigid, but human habits must be submitted to. If business users' core actions take place in spreadsheets and emails, the MVD interface should appear as spreadsheet plugins and within emails, rather than requiring users to log into a brand-new portal. OpenAI's deployment at BBVA, which cut in through the ChatGPT interface that 120,000 employees were already using, rather than starting from scratch, is a paragon of submitting to habits. Remember that cause of death in Section 2.1: employees unwilling to adopt new tools ranked first among the top five roadblocks. **The biggest enemy of a new system is not the old system; it's old habits.**

## 2.7 "Actions Speak Louder Than Words" User Research

At the end of this chapter, let's pull the camera back to the source of methodology and discuss the divide between FDE-style user research and traditional research.

The credo of traditional research is "ask": questionnaires, interviews, focus groups. The credo of FDE is "watch" and "do"—actions speak louder than words. There are three reasons, building upon each other.

First layer: Clients don't know what they want. This is not demeaning; it's a cognitive law. Faced with an entirely new category—intelligence analysis software in 2004, AI agents in 2025—users have no frame of reference to describe their needs. Palantir's demonstration loop is effective precisely because it doesn't ask "what do you want?", but says "here is what I built, you tell me what's wrong with it." Human judgment regarding "what's wrong" is far stronger than their imagination of "what they want." Finding fault in a solution is a human talent; describing an ideal solution out of thin air is a human shortcoming.

Second layer: What clients say and what they do are two different things. The example of the lawyer group in Section 2.1 is the best proof: if a questionnaire asks "are you willing to use professional legal AI?", the procurement department will tell you the willingness is strong—after all, they just spent $50,000 on a tool; but observing the lawyers' actual behavior, they are using ChatGPT to draft contracts. In a corporate context, "saying" is polluted by too many factors: political correctness, politeness to vendors, and self-preservation of roles. Only behavior does not lie. Shadowing observation of behavior, and MVD measurement of behavior, are both built on the foundation that "doing is truer than saying."

Third layer: The highest quality research happens during joint labor. In interviews, clients are "research subjects," guarded and performative; when working side-by-side, clients are colleagues, relaxed and authentic. During the two or three days of bootcamps when FDEs write code back-to-back with the client's technical staff, the density of information exchanged exceeds any formal research—in the margins of debugging, the client might casually drop, "actually, we never trust this field," or "this process goes through the system on the surface, but in reality, we still make phone calls." These words will never appear in formal interviews because they seem "informal." Yet they are precisely the critical intelligence for the success or failure of a deployment.

There is also a new variable from 2026 worth adding here: the interview process itself is being transformed by AI. Tezign CTO Ding Xindong shared their practice: instead of sending structured questionnaires, they use Agents to conduct autonomous interviews with all company employees—customizing the communication based on different scenarios and the actual pain points of different individuals, and finally aggregating a global diagnostic map showing "what stage each product line is at, what the pain points are, and what is the suitable way to advance." Questionnaires ask for answers, while interviews chat out pain points—AI has driven down the cost of the latter. (Source in Appendix C)

Combining the three layers, the FDE research methodology can be condensed into one sentence: **Use the Shadowing Method to find pain points, use Minimum Viable Deployment to validate solutions, and use joint labor to win the truth.**

By this point, the right problem has been locked in, and the value has been preliminarily validated. The next battlefield is to turn this validated single-point value into a real contract and a relationship that truly begins—how to win the client.
