# Chapter 5 Securing the Renewal

> "If it's bustling when you're present but cools down rapidly after you leave, that's not activation, that's just a backup dance."

## 5.1 Renewal and Churn

Doing business starts with doing the math: the cost of acquiring a new customer is several times that of retaining an existing one. Enterprise-level business amplifies this math by two orders of magnitude: **the customer acquisition cost (CAC) for a large client—months of sales cycles, boot-camp-style investments, Proof of Concept (PoC) costs—can easily amount to hundreds of thousands of dollars, whereas the cost of renewal approaches zero. The renewal rate is the lifeline of the FDE business model.**

Let's first look at churn. The churn of enterprise customers and consumer products are entirely different in shape: consumer product churn is a silent uninstallation, while enterprise customer churn is a prolonged death by a thousand cuts. It starts with a slow decline in usage rate, followed by someone questioning "is this thing really worth it" in a regular meeting. Then, renewal negotiations are indefinitely postponed with "let's look at it next year," and finally, it gets completely uprooted during a budget season. What's even more terrifying is the collateral damage: as mentioned in Chapter 3, the enterprise market holds grudges. A publicized failure will be repeatedly chewed over for years within the small circles of the industry.

The reasons for enterprise customer churn can be summarized into five categories, ranked by their preventability:

- **Value Evaporation:** The system is still running, but no one remembers what problem it solved. This usually stems from a disconnect in the value narrative—after the business problem that initiated the project is solved, no one continues to reiterate to the organization (especially newly appointed managers) "why we have this system." Value is not proven once and for all; it needs to be constantly reproven.

- **Champion Departure:** Your internal ally gets promoted, transferred, or resigns. The successor did not personally experience the initial selection process and is naturally indifferent, or even hostile, to the system—viewing it as "the predecessor's vanity project." There is a jargon in the enterprise software circle: "When the champion moves, the contract hangs in the balance."

- **Quality Drift:** Businesses change, data changes, models update, and the system's output quality slowly declines, followed by a slow collapse in user trust. By the time management notices the collapse, it is usually too late.

- **Cost Backfire:** The higher the system usage and the larger the bill, the stricter the scrutiny from the finance department. If the value narrative cannot keep up with the billing growth, "success" ironically becomes an obstacle to renewal.

- **Vendor Withdrawal Reaction:** The customer's vigilance against being "locked in." The consulting firm Gartner even made a startling prediction: by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon agent solutions led by Forward Deployed Engineers due to high vendor costs and a lack of internal skills. (See Appendix C for the source) This prediction itself is a warning to all FDE teams: **If your model makes customers feel hijacked, the market will collectively revolt.**

Now let's look at how to measure "retention." Consumer products look at retention rates; enterprise businesses need to look at three levels (see Appendix A for complete metrics): usage rate and active depth (behavioral layer), health score (relationship layer), and Net Revenue Retention (NRR - financial layer, measuring whether the same cohort of existing customers pays more or less this year compared to last year). Among these, Net Revenue Retention is the ultimate judge: exceeding 100% means that even without signing a single new deal, the existing business is still growing—this is also the most direct proof of "delivery as operation."

What does it look like when this metric reaches the top tier? Palantir's Q4 2025 earnings report provided three numbers: a Net Revenue Retention of 139%—meaning existing customers automatically grew by nearly 40%; Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO) increased by 145%—meaning they won't worry about a lack of business in the coming years; and a record-breaking total contract value signed in a single quarter of $4.26 billion. Management specifically explained one detail: the 139% figure doesn't even include revenue from newly signed customers in the last twelve months—it is purely the "appreciation of existing customers' trust." In its early years, this company was most ridiculed for being "project-based, with no repurchases," but twenty years later, it used the same cohort of customers to prove: if hands-on delivery can continuously create value, renewal is not a sales problem, but a matter of time. (See Appendix C for the source)

The remaining five sections of this chapter address the five causes of churn respectively: performance and stability (preventing quality drift), the art of trade-offs (preventing cost backfire), onboarding and training (preventing usage decay), organizational maintenance (preventing champion risk), and wake-up mechanisms (preventing value evaporation).

## 5.2 Optimizing System Performance and Stability

The rule for consumer products is: one second slower in loading drops a chunk of retention. Performance issues in enterprise systems have a different pathology: enterprise users' tolerance for "slowness" is actually higher than consumers' (they are used to the sluggishness of legacy systems), but their tolerance for "unreliability" approaches zero.

The weight of reliability in an enterprise context is ten times that of a consumer scenario, for three reasons. First, the output of enterprise systems feeds into real business decisions—the loss caused by one wrong inventory recommendation could wipe out a year's worth of the system's ROI. Second, errors are amplified and spread—if the system makes a mistake once, the story will circulate in the department for three months; if it's right a hundred times, no one remembers. Third, trust in an enterprise system is built over quarters but destroyed in minutes.

The FDE team's four lines of defense in safeguarding reliability.

- **First Line:** Clarify service level commitments (SLAs) and make them visible. Commitments regarding availability, latency, and error rates shouldn't just be written in the contract; they should be built into a monitoring dashboard that customers can view themselves. Turn "the system is stable" from a stance you have to defend into a fact the customer can check at any time—transparency itself is a trust asset.

- **Second Line:** Design guardrails for the "probabilistic nature" of AI. It is impossible for AI systems to be 100% correct. Accept this reality in engineering, and manage it in product: outputs the model is unsure of must be flagged or routed to a human; high-risk actions must have a human in the loop; every major model update must go through a re-evaluation to prevent old issues from recurring—the evaluation system established in Chapter 4 becomes part of the production guardrails here.
- **Guardrail Blueprint:** In its collaboration with financial institutions, Anthropic made "auditable and traceable" a core design, ensuring every agent decision can replay its chain of evidence. In scenarios like finance and healthcare, auditability is not a bonus; it's an entry ticket.

- **Third Line:** On-call and response must make the customer feel you are always there. If the deployment crashes at 2 AM, the FDE's reaction speed is the perceived temperature of the customer's relationship with you. As the practitioner's iron rule quoted in Chapter 1 goes: "If the deployment crashes at 2 AM, you don't file a ticket, you don't blame other teams, and you don't go back to sleep. You fix it." The spirit of this must be grounded in mechanisms: on-call rotations, incident post-mortems, and honest debriefs to the customer after every incident—enterprise customers can accept incidents, but they cannot accept concealment.

- **Fourth Line:** Synchronized planning of capacity and cost. Growth in usage is a happy problem, but if handled poorly, it becomes an assassin during renewal. The performance team must always run half a step ahead of the usage curve: before the customer's peak business season arrives, capacity, rate-limiting, and degradation plans are already in place. Eliminate slowness before the customer even feels it—the best performance work is the work that goes unnoticed.

## 5.3 Degraded Services — Letting Go of Unnecessary Persistence

"Lossy service" (graceful degradation) is a concept in internet product design: proactively degrading the service under extreme scenarios to preserve core value. This concept has a deeper variation in the FDE context—it concerns the eternal tear between customization and standardization, which I call the "art of restraint."

The background is the gravity every FDE team will encounter: as long as the customer is there, the demands are there; as long as the demands are there, the customization won't stop. Three months later you look back, and this customer's deployment is overgrown with custom features, half of which are used by only three people, yet the maintenance cost is entirely on your shoulders. Leave it unchecked, and you'll be burdened with a mountain of "customization debt"—it gnaws at your profits on one side (maintenance costs eat up contract revenue) and ties your hands on the other (every platform upgrade risks stepping on a custom landmine).

The wisdom of "lossy" lies in proactively making subtractions across three dimensions.

- **Feature Dimension | Daring to say "no" to long-tail requests:** The judgment criterion is not whether the request is reasonable (most requests are reasonable in isolation), but two questions: Does the number of users it serves multiplied by the frequency justify its lifetime maintenance cost? Can it be generalized into a platform capability (if it's worth it, it goes into the feedback loop in Chapter 7)? For requests where the answer to both is no, the best response is providing a workaround, not code. FDEs are not order-takers—an order-taker culture is exactly the yes-man attitude that the "French Waiter" model opposes.

- **Commitment Dimension | Tiered commitments, not a blanket highest standard:** Not all features deserve 99.99% availability. The core transaction link is guarded to the highest standard, while reports and exploratory features generously accept degradation—degradation strategies (turning off heavy computation features during peak hours), off-peak strategies (running heavy tasks at night), and tiered commitments communicated clearly to the customer in advance. Concentrating reliability resources on the vitals is more honest and more sustainable than evenly distributed, mediocre reliability.

- **Cost Dimension | Proactive management of usage bills:** As mentioned in 5.1 regarding "cost backfire": higher usage leads to higher bills; when the value narrative can't keep up, success ironically becomes a hurdle for renewal. A proactive FDE team will act before the bill becomes painful: offering cost optimization solutions (caching, batch processing, model tiering—using cheaper models for simple requests), redesigning the pricing structure (shifting from pure usage to a smoother "platform fee plus usage" structure), and most importantly—calculating the "value equation corresponding to this bill" for the customer before their finance head even asks. **By the time you are called in to explain the bill, you have already lost half the battle; the one who proactively balances the books holds all the cards during renewal negotiations.**

The essence of "lossy" is acknowledging that resources are always finite, and continuously betting those finite resources on what the customer truly cares about. This is in the same vein as "refusing expensive failures" in Chapter 2: restraint is not inaction; it is the ability to choose your battles.

## 5.4 Guiding New Users to Quick Onboarding

After the system is activated, the user base will not remain static: new employees join, organizational adjustments occur, and new departments are brought into scope. "New user onboarding" for enterprise systems is an endless, rolling process. If the onboarding mechanism is well-designed, the usage rate increases over time; if poorly designed, the usage rate naturally decays as the initial batch of trained users churns.

Onboarding in an enterprise scenario is fundamentally different from consumer products: consumer product onboarding is a one-off, self-service flow, while enterprise system onboarding is a "human-to-human" organizational engineering effort. There are three reusable structures:

- **Tiered Training:** A one-size-fits-all training for everyone is the biggest waste. Effective tiering involves three levels: in-depth training for administrators and internal champions (their future role is internal experts); scenario-based training for regular users (not talking about features, but "how to do your three daily tasks using the system," capped at 30 minutes); and a one-sentence training for executives ("open this, and this number is the answer"). The spirit of tiering aligns with section 4.4: each role only learns the part relevant to them.

- **The "Train the Trainer" Leverage:** The FDE team will eventually withdraw, and training work must be handed over before that happens. Identify enthusiasts within the customer's organization and cultivate them into internal instructors and Q&A contacts—providing official certification, exclusive support channels, and exposure opportunities in front of executives. The core design of the collaboration between Anthropic and FIS was precisely this: "Transferring knowledge to enable FIS to independently build and scale its own agents." **Teaching the customer to teach themselves is the highest form of delivery.**

Two forms of scaled "human-to-human" training are worth comparing. When BBVA rolled out to 120,000 people, they didn't rely on the vendor's training team, but on two batches of internal roles: a bank-wide "AI Pioneer Network" responsible for conducting workshops and uncovering scenarios in various business departments; and a group of power users referred to by colleagues as "AI Geeks," who provided hands-on guidance to people around them. The collaboration between consulting giant Accenture and Anthropic is on another scale entirely: 30,000 consultants underwent systematic training on Claude, forming one of the world's largest networks of AI practitioners—Accenture then brought this team into their own clients. (See Appendix C for the source) The scale differs by three orders of magnitude, but the structure is identical: **Vendors never train "users"; they train "people who will go train others."**

- **Documentation and Self-Service Systems:** Most enterprise documentation goes unread once written, unless it meets two criteria: it is organized by tasks rather than features ("how to process an abnormal refund" instead of "refund module feature description"), and it is embedded in the product rather than existing independently (appearing right where the user gets stuck). Documentation is both a handover deliverable to the customer and a scalable asset for yourself—70% of the training system for the next similar customer can be inherited.

## 5.5 Organizational Maintenance and Single-Point Dependency

Section 5.1 listed "Champion Departure" as the second biggest cause of death for enterprise renewals. This section addresses it specifically because both its prevalence and lethality are severely underestimated.

The formation of a single-point dependency is almost natural: the project is initiated by a champion, the relationship is maintained by the champion, the success narrative is endorsed by the champion—and then one day they leave. The successor arrives with their own agenda, your system doesn't make the top twenty on their to-do list, renewal season comes, no one speaks for you, and the contract dies a silent death. In the industry, countless systems that were "working perfectly fine but still cut" die from this exact script.

Defense lines must be built in three places.

- **Grid-ification of Relationships:** From the day you realize the single-point risk, systematically broaden your relationship network: beyond the one champion, develop at least two more independent relationship lines—the daily user community in the business lines (internal instructors are natural nodes), and an executive sponsor one level higher. High-level relationships do not need frequent maintenance, but they must remain visible at key junctures (quarterly reviews, before renewals). The test standard for the relationship grid is: no matter which single individual leaves, the information channel remains unbroken.

- **Organizationalization of Value:** Rewrite the system's value from "the champion's political achievement" to "an organizational asset." Specific actions include: regular organization-wide announcements of value data (making the user departments feel they cannot live without it), repetitive recounting of success stories in internal customer meetings (forming collective memory), and embedding the system into process documents (when the system is written into Standard Operating Procedures, replacing it requires rewriting the procedures—this is the strongest institutional lock-in). The goal is to ensure that any newly appointed manager forms the impression in their first week that "this system is a fixed asset here."

- **Turning Departures into a Ritual:** When a champion leaves, the reaction of most vendors is passive lamentation. The correct reaction is to treat it as a relationship-building opportunity: hold a proper "mission accomplished" ceremony for the departing champion (thank-you notes, a summary of achievements, gifting them take-away professional capital—like the credentials to share the case study publicly), while immediately initiating the handover with the successor—using "helping the successor achieve quick wins" as the entry point, rather than "persuading them to keep our system." The departing champion moving to a new employer is a potential gateway to your next customer; **treating departing individuals well transforms a churn risk into an acquisition channel.**

## 5.6 Designing Health Scores and Wake-Up Mechanisms

The final section gathers all the actions for safeguarding renewals into a single system: the Customer Health Score System. Its goal is to turn "relationship deterioration" from a sudden death into a slow curve that has long been monitored—ensuring you always have time to intervene.

What does health look at? Consumer products monitor retention and just looking at Daily Active Users (DAU) is enough. For enterprise customers, you have to look at four types of signals simultaneously: usage signals (trends in weekly active users, what percentage of people are using key features, whether it's genuine usage or just checking the box), value signals (how are the original business metrics performing now, does the ROI story still hold), relationship signals (the champion's employment status, number of high-level contacts in the past 30 days, customer response speed to your team), and commercial signals (trends in usage-to-bill ratio, contract expiration date, competitor movements). Synthesize these four types of signals into a health score, and trigger an alert if it drops below a warning line. The biggest use of the health score isn't the score itself; it's that it forces the team to review all customers one by one every week.

Quarterly Business Reviews (QBRs): Turning "what value we have created" into a fixed routine every quarter. The QBR (the regular meeting where vendor and customer align on value and plans every quarter) is the most important renewal project in enterprise services, yet it is often run as a going-through-the-motions report. A good QBR has three disciplines: speak the customer's language, not your product's language ("how many work hours we saved you this quarter," not "what features we launched this quarter"); let the customer's business side be the protagonist, not the audience (let the champion tell their team's story, while you provide the data ammunition); and end with a "value plan for the next quarter"—renewal shouldn't be a one-time negotiation before expiration, but a serialized drama naturally renewed every quarter.

Wake-up mechanisms: What to do when usage declines. Consumer products rely on push notifications and emails to wake up users; to wake up an enterprise customer, you need a combination of "people plus data"—as soon as the health score drops, a tiered response immediately kicks in. Mild decline (reduced usage in a certain department): followed up by internal instructors for a visit. Moderate decline (overall activity drops by 30%): the FDE team launches a targeted diagnostic—usually one of three things: business change, personnel change, or quality drift, and treated accordingly. Severe decline (on the verge of deactivation): executive layer intervention, having a candid conversation about "is it still worth continuing"—sometimes the answer is a graceful exit or downgrade, which preserves the relationship and reputation, leaving the door open for future reunions.

With this, the five lines of defense for safeguarding renewals are complete. However, an enterprise relationship that only defends and never attacks will atrophy—there is always another unsolved problem within the customer's organization.
