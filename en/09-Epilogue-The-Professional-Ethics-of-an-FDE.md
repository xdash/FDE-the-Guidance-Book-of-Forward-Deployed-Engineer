# Epilogue: The Professional Ethics of an FDE

Every book on methodology must discuss one question at its end: when you master this set of methods, where do the boundaries lie? And the topic this book must discuss is heavier than most methodologies. Because what an FDE holds in their hands is not ordinary technology—**it is the deepest secrets of the client's organization, and the ever-growing power to make decisions in place of humans**.

The nature of an FDE's work determines what they will see. To deliver successfully, you have to look at the client's truest operational data—including the ugly parts; you must map out the organization's power dynamics—including who is incompetent and who has lost influence; you will come into contact with core business processes—including workarounds that walk in gray areas. The client opens all of this up to you based on a simple understanding: you are here to help. This trust is the foundation of the FDE model, and to destroy it, all it takes is crossing the line once.

I believe there are at least six bottom lines to an FDE's professional ethics, which I write here for mutual encouragement with all peers.

**First, data sovereignty belongs to the client.** Not a single byte of data seen at the client site should appear where it shouldn't—it must not enter AI training data (unless explicitly authorized by contract), it must not enter case study materials (unless written consent is given by the client), and it must not become conversational fodder for your next job. The principle of data access minimization is not just a technical specification; it is professional conduct: if you don't have to see it, don't look at it; if it can be anonymized, anonymize it.

**Second, honestly report results, including bad news.** In a pay-for-results model, the greatest moral hazard is sugarcoating the outcomes—packaging "the system went online" as "value was realized," or packaging correlation as causation. The value measurement system in Chapter 6 can be either the most honest tool or the most exquisite lie machine; the difference lies solely in the human heart. The foundation of an FDE is "daring to be tested"; therefore, when bad results are discovered through testing, they must be presented with the exact same mindset.

**Third, do not create dependence, and do not peddle fear.** This industry harbors two hidden evils: one is intentionally making systems into black boxes so clients can never leave you; the other is exaggerating the panic of "you will die if you don't use AI" to close a deal. Gartner predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon forward-deployment-led solutions due to costs and skill hollowing-out—this prediction is a wake-up call for the entire industry. A healthy FDE model should be "leave when done": transfer knowledge to the client, and consolidate capabilities within the client's team. **Make the client strong, rather than making the client addicted.**

**Fourth, take "those being replaced" seriously.** The systems delivered by FDEs will indeed replace parts of people's work in many scenarios. This book covers a lot of "change management" techniques, but beyond technique lies ethics: do not celebrate efficiency in front of those who are being replaced, do not write people off as "cost items" in proposals without providing a way out, and do not play dumb about whose fate you are changing. The neutrality of technology is a myth; the choices of the deployer are reality.

**Fifth, say no to "things the client demands but shouldn't be done."** You will encounter demands that walk the edge of compliance: "Help us make employee behavioral monitoring a bit more granular," or "These data are a bit fuzzy on compliance, but let's connect them first anyway." The metaphor of the "French waiter" holds its deepest meaning here—true professionalism is not satisfying every client demand, but daring to guide the client in a direction that is truly beneficial to them and harmless to the world. The confidence to say no comes from having other clients on your ledger; thus, ethics are always tied to the business model as well.

**Sixth, remember that you represent "technology" itself.** To many clients, you are the first human face they associate with artificial intelligence. Every exaggeration of yours overdraws the entire industry's credit in their minds; every promise you fulfill deposits into the entire industry's account. As AI enters deeper and deeper into the functioning of society, the deployer is the final translator between technology and everyday human life—the cost of a distorted translation is paid by everyone.

The company Palantir itself is full of controversy: its history of serving intelligence and military agencies makes many people wary of everything about it—including the FDE model. My heavy quotation of its methodologies in this book does not equate to endorsing all of its client choices. On the contrary, precisely because the domains it serves are so sensitive, the disciplines in its engineering culture regarding permissions, audits, and the principle of least privilege are especially worth learning from. **The more capable the deployer, the more they need to write the boundaries before the methodology.**

FDE is a young profession, and its industry rules have not yet been written. I hope this book serves as its first boundary stone.
