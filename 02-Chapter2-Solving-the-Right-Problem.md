# Chapter 2 Solving the Right Problem

> "On the wrong problem, all execution is waste."

## 2.1 An Autopsy Report on the POC Graveyard

Silicon Valley has a term for it: "POC purgatory." Projects that go in don't come out — not dead enough to be declared a failure, not alive enough to warrant greater investment. So they linger quarter after quarter in status reports as "ongoing," like patients in a ward full of tubes.

Researchers at MIT once performed a systematic autopsy on this graveyard. They distilled five major roadblocks standing in the way of enterprise AI projects scaling up, ranked by frequency of occurrence:

1. Employees are unwilling to use the new tools — ironically, the same people are flying through ChatGPT in private, every day
2. Concerns about the quality of model output
3. Poor user experience
4. Lack of executive sponsorship
5. Difficulty managing change

Notice what's absent from this list: the model isn't smart enough, compute isn't cheap enough, the technology isn't advanced enough — none of these make the cut. What kills these projects happens almost entirely at the level of "problem definition" and "organizational reality," not at the technical level.

There is one detail from the report, repeatedly cited, that deserves to stand on its own. A company spent fifty thousand dollars procuring a specialized contract-analysis tool with a lavish feature list. But one of the firm's senior attorneys simply refused to use it — she kept drafting contracts with free ChatGPT instead. Her reasoning was plain: the summaries produced by the purchased tool were too rigid, and couldn't be customized to her habits. The procurement report read "deployed"; the daily reality was the official system sitting idle while employees routed around it (see Appendix C for sources). This detail reveals the true origin of roadblock number one: it's not that employees are stuck in their ways — it's that consumer-grade products have spoiled their appetites. People who fly through these tools at home every day simply can't tolerate the "artificial stupidity" they find in the office.

There's an even more pointed comparison: **projects done in partnership with an external specialist vendor succeed at roughly twice the rate of projects built in-house.** Why do outside teams win more often? Not because they're smarter — because they can't afford to lose. When you get paid based on results, the cost of defining the wrong problem comes straight out of your own pocket. An internal project at a Japanese company is a textbook counterexample: they pulled together three or four of their strongest engineers to form a task force. The demo was dazzling, leadership nodded along — but from day one, nobody could answer the question "by what standard do we measure whether this system is any good?" Six months later the project quietly vanished from the status reports. No one announced its failure; it simply died of natural causes (see Appendix C for sources).

This is the first-principle of this chapter: **on the wrong problem, all execution is waste — and the density of wrong problems inside an enterprise far exceeds what you'd imagine.** This chapter is about exactly one thing: before you write a single line of code, how do you make sure you're solving the right problem?

## 2.2 PSF: Finding Problem-Solution Fit

Internet startup methodology has a core concept called PMF (Product-Market Fit): get the product right, and the market will pull growth along on its own. In the world of the FDE, the corresponding unit isn't "product and market" — it's "problem and solution." I call it PSF (Problem-Solution Fit).

The difference is subtle but crucial. PMF asks "does my product have a market that wants it," and the vantage point is the supply side. PSF asks "is this specific problem the client has worth solving, and can our capabilities actually solve it," and the vantage point is the demand side. Inside a single enterprise client, there may be hundreds of "here's where AI could do something" opportunities — but the ones actually worth doing must clear three gates at once.

Gate one, the pain test: is this problem some specific person's specific pain? Note the two instances of "specific." "Improve customer-service efficiency" isn't a pain point — it's a direction. "Every Monday morning, the customer service supervisor spends three hours manually collating last week's escalation tickets across four systems, when her actual job should be analyzing why those escalations happened" — that's a pain point. McGrew offered an even sharper standard: go solve one of the five problems the CEO cares about most. The logic is entirely practical — only a problem of that magnitude can help you flatten the bureaucratic resistance inside an enterprise (Chapter 3 will unpack how to use this to get past the IT department). Sierra's head of agent engineering, Möller, holds the same principle in different words: take on only two kinds of problems — the genuinely hard ones, and the ones with genuine business impact. Hard but without impact is just showing off; impactful but not hard, and it's not your turn to solve it.

Gate two, the economics test: how much money is this problem actually worth solving? Plenty of pain points are real pain, but not worth much money; a project that can't do this math won't survive the next budget cycle even if it gets built. You can rough it out: how many person-hours does this problem eat up per week? What does that translate to in labor cost? What's the cost of a single mistake? What could the freed-up staff do instead? Palantir's bootcamp puts this gate right up front — before the client even begins, they have to lock in a "core battlefield" and produce a quantified target: "reduce scheduling conflicts by 30%," "shorten inventory turnover by 15 days." One widely cited finding from that report shows exactly how easily this gate gets skipped: more than half of enterprise AI budgets go toward front-office sales and marketing, yet the returns concentrate in unglamorous back-office areas — contract review, procurement, risk control. Everyone is busy solving "the problem that demos well," not "the problem that's worth money."

Gate three, the feasibility test: given our current capabilities and this client's data reality, how far can we actually get? This is the gate most easily drowned out by enthusiasm. There are two questions that must be answered on-site: where is the data, and what state is it in? The answer is often worse than imagined — scattered across seven systems, three versions that don't reconcile, and the most authoritative copy sitting in some veteran employee's personal spreadsheet. And: what accuracy threshold does this problem require? "99% viable" and "90% viable" are separated by an order of magnitude in engineering investment, and for many business scenarios 90% plus human review is actually the optimal solution — recognizing this takes not technical skill, but an understanding of business consequences.

Only once all three gates are cleared have you actually found problem-solution fit. And all three gates must be cleared on-site at the client — making these judgments from a headquarters conference room, working off secondhand information, is the root cause of every mistake in this chapter.

## 2.3 Refusing the Expensive "POC Graveyard"

In 2025, a Chinese enterprise AI implementation vendor posted a rather ruthless passage on its website, meant to talk potential clients out of hiring them: if your use case hasn't been validated yet, go to a demo event first; if your data fits in a single spreadsheet, a lightweight service will do; if you just want to learn about AI, use the free option. The last line was the sharpest: "The FDE model is a heavy investment. We'd rather you start a little later than start at the wrong time." (see Appendix C for sources)

That passage deserves to be printed and hung on every wall in enterprise AI. It states a truth that runs against every sales instinct: **refusing the wrong projects is the single most important profit lever in the FDE model.**

Why are the wrong projects so dangerous? Because the FDE cost structure is front-loaded — the best engineers, the most expensive travel, the longest on-site engagements, all happen before any payment comes in. Once you're stuck in the mud, it isn't a matter of losing one deal — it's your entire elite team getting tied down, and opportunity cost avalanching from there.

A former Palantir engineer, Barry, recalled that the company once "burned through millions of dollars on client pilots — a lot of those projects had margins that were literally negative infinity, because we did them for free." But the point he adds right after is the one that matters: Palantir could afford to burn that money because it treated pilots as a research-and-development portfolio — like venture capital, where most bets going to zero is fine, as long as the winners pay for everything. But if you don't have that kind of capital depth, and you don't have a mechanism for "converting a failed pilot into a product asset," then every wrong pilot is pure blood loss. (see Appendix C for sources)

So an FDE team needs a mechanism for saying no — not just the courage to say it. Three actionable lines of defense.

- **Line of defense one: every POC must have a "graduation criterion."** At the launch of every validation project, write down, up front: after how many weeks, using what metric, at what value, does the project "graduate" into a paid deployment; and if it doesn't hit that number, the two sides part ways with dignity. Palantir's bootcamp takes this logic to its extreme — it isn't a POC at all, it's an industrialized replacement for one: one to five days, the client brings real data, a deployable prototype is built on-site, and an executive signs off on the spot. Either you see something real within a few days, or you don't start. Traditional POCs collapse into graveyards precisely because they are "open-ended, metric-free, and refereeless."

- **Line of defense two:** watch for three categories of red flags. Pooling practitioner experience, if two or more of these three signals show up, treat it as a serious warning. First, "land with no owner" — the project has no clear business-side owner inside the client organization; only IT is engaging with you, and what IT cares about is compliance and stability, which have never been a reason to build something new. Second, "look but don't touch" — the client wants you to prove your capabilities first, but won't provide real data; validation done without real data is guaranteed to produce a self-deceiving false positive. Third, "requirements the size of the universe" — a client who, at the very first meeting, wants you to "cover every use case across the whole company" is usually a client who isn't ready to actually do a single one of them.

- **Line of defense three:** give "no" a dignified exit. Refusing doesn't mean severing the relationship. The best approach is to translate "not now" into "when": "the data foundation for this use case is missing three things — we'd suggest starting with a different use case first, quietly shoring up those three gaps along the way, and coming back to this one next quarter." Package the refusal as a roadmap — it protects your capacity and preserves the relationship at the same time. Chapter 3's "lighthouse client selection" picks up this same thread.

## 2.4 Pain Is the Prime Mover Behind Deployment

Where does the right problem come from? Textbooks will tell you: from requirements gathering. FDE field experience will tell you: **it comes from pain. And pain never shows up in the conference room — it only shows up on the shop floor.**

Palantir wrote this method in blood twenty years ago. Look back at the story from Section 1.2 about the battlefield in Iraq: soldiers needed a tool to warn them of roadside bombs — a requirement no interview could have surfaced, because the soldiers had no idea "you could ask software for this." They assumed it was just part of patrol life. Pain points are things engineers *see*, not things users *say*. The on-site engineer went out on patrol with the convoy, watched with his own eyes the hesitation and fear at a suspicious stretch of road, and out of that came the crude mapping tool that changed the battlefield.

This method has a name in anthropology: "participant observation." In the Toyota Production System it's called *genchi genbutsu* — go to the actual place, look at the actual thing, get the actual facts. The FDE has turned this into an operational field method, which I call the "shadow-work method."

Follow a real user through a real day of their real work. Don't interview them — sit next to them and watch them work. Watch which systems they open, which spreadsheets they copy-paste between, where they frown, which "official processes" they route around. OpenAI's FDE team did exactly this on the John Deere project: they flew to Iowa and followed agronomists and farmers into the fields, watching how they made spraying decisions, watching which information actually made it into those decisions, watching how the seasonal deadlines dictated everything. That oft-quoted line of practitioner wisdom describes exactly what this method is looking for: "the hard part is finding the workflow that nobody wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process works the way it does." Every one of those three things can only be found on-site.

Focus your attention on the "workaround," not the "process." An official process flowchart tells you how an organization is *supposed* to run; the workaround tells you how the organization *actually* runs — every single workaround is an unmet pain point screaming for attention. Why do employees insist on exporting the data to a spreadsheet and recalculating it themselves? Why is it common knowledge in the department that "you have to go to Wang for that spreadsheet"? Why, even though a data system exists, does someone always manually double-check the numbers before a decision meeting? Workarounds are the organization's scar tissue — under every scar is a system failure, and every one of them is an opportunity for the FDE.

Beware of "translated pain points." If the requirement you're hearing has passed through the client's IT department, procurement department, or a consultant, it distorts a little with every hand-off — IT will translate a business pain point into a technical requirement ("we need a data platform"), procurement will translate it into a compliance line item ("we need to meet standard X"). This is precisely where traditional software project disasters begin: the vendor is accountable to the translated version, not to the pain itself. So your first priority is to bypass the translation and go straight to the nerve ending of the actual pain. This is also the core job of the "echo" role in Palantir's two-person model — understanding the client's "mission" rather than their "requirements": a requirement is a secondhand narrative of a pain point, while the mission is its firsthand source.

Once you've found the real pain point, the next step is to validate it at the smallest possible cost: can our solution actually stop this pain?

## 2.5 Validating Value with the "Minimum Viable Deployment"

Internet startup methodology has a famous concept called MVP (Minimum Viable Product): use the smallest possible product to validate market demand. The FDE's corresponding concept, which I call **MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment)**, uses the smallest possible engineering investment, inside the client's real environment, against a real pain point, to validate that value has actually, genuinely occurred once.

One word different, but the difference runs to the bone. MVP validates "should we build this product," and the referee is the market; MVD validates "can this solution generate value for this specific client," and the referee is this specific client's specific business. A solution validated successfully at ten clients can still fail at the eleventh — different data foundations, different organizational inertia, differently shaped pain points. This is the cruelty of enterprise delivery: **value cannot be inherited — it can only be validated one client at a time.**

MVD has three iron rules.

- **Rule one:** real data, no exceptions. Validating with a client's "de-identified (sensitive information removed)" sample data, or with demo data you've constructed yourself, is the first brick in the POC graveyard. Real data hides every devil imaginable: field meanings that don't match the documentation, thirty percent null values, coding rules from three years ago, and worst of all — data that records a broken process as if it were correct. **How this rule gets implemented:** Palantir's bootcamp writes "the client must bring their own real business data" directly into the rules, and the John Deere project put "review hundreds of real field cases" before any modeling work began. A solution that only holds up on fake data is just an elaborately constructed form of self-delusion.

- **Rule two:** shrink the scope, not the ambition. A common mistake is to understand MVD as a "gutted version of the big vision" — cut seven-tenths of the features and end up with something that resembles nothing. The right move is to shrink along a different dimension: don't cut value, cut scope. Don't aim for "smart customer service that covers the whole company" — aim for "cover only returns-and-exchanges tickets, but do it end-to-end with zero human intervention." Don't aim for "supply chain optimization across the whole group" — aim for "just fix the scheduling conflicts on this one production line, but genuinely save 20 person-hours a week." Make the scope small enough that the value density is high enough to be visible to the naked eye of the business unit, high enough that they start spreading the word themselves. The expansion path of the legal AI company Harvey is a textbook example of this move: rather than rolling out firm-wide, they pushed all the way through with one global practice group first, turned the first cohort of partners into true believers, and only then expanded horizontally after six months of real-world use.

- **Rule three:** fix a hard deadline, and force trade-offs. An MVD's validation cycle should be measured in weeks, not months. Palantir's bootcamp runs one to five days; Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live is four weeks; Decagon's typical deployment is four to eight weeks. The point of a deadline isn't speed for its own sake — it's forcing both sides into honest trade-offs: whatever can't demonstrate value within these few weeks isn't core value yet. A six-month "minimum validation" will almost inevitably regrow into a big project that wants everything — which is just the POC graveyard breaking ground again.

### The Bootcamp: Industrializing the MVD

Palantir's AIP Bootcamp, launched in 2023, deserves to be dissected on its own as the benchmark for "industrializing the MVD" — it is currently the only MVD pipeline that's been validated at scale.

Start with the numbers: starting from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, the count has doubled year over year — media tracking shows the cumulative total has long since passed a thousand, and at the 2025 peak the pace ran to nearly six per day. The traditional enterprise software sales cycle (nine to twelve months) has been compressed to a matter of weeks. U.S. commercial revenue grew 137% year over year in Q4 2025, and the company has publicly attributed almost all of that growth to this. (see Appendix C for sources)

Now look at the process: it breaks the MVD down into five standardized moves. Day 0, preparation: both sides lock in one extremely focused core battlefield — "optimize scheduling on a specific production line," "reduce inventory turnover days" — and reject all grand narratives. Day 1, connection: the client's existing systems are wired up, isolated data is pulled out, and a preliminary ontology model is built. Days 2 through 3, construction: the FDE and the client's technical staff write code and configure rules back-to-back, wiring the large model into the business process to produce an automated workflow that can execute real actions. Days 4 through 5, demo and decision: what comes out isn't a report — it's a live piece of software with an interface, and the business executive clicks through it with their own hands, watching AI produce recommendations based on their own company's data. Once the shock wears off, they go straight into commercial negotiation.

The elegance of the bootcamp is that it solves all three classic MVD problems at once: the real-data problem (the client brings it themselves), the deadline problem (five days, hard cap), and the referee problem (the executive operates it with their own hands). It also incidentally solves a deeper problem — trust. Letting a decision-maker operate a system built on their own data with their own hands beats a hundred-page feasibility report.

How fast do contracts get signed after a bootcamp? Palantir has disclosed a real cadence on earnings calls fast enough that even peers might not quite believe it: one large healthcare company attended a bootcamp in December, and five weeks later signed a five-year deal worth $26 million in annual contract value; a global bank signed an initial $2 million contract a month after its pilot, then expanded four months later into a three-year deal worth $19 million in annual contract value; the pharmacy chain Walgreens piloted in ten stores first, saw a 30% improvement in in-store operating efficiency, then rolled out to 4,000 stores within eight months, with the AI-driven end-to-end workflow automatically handling roughly 384 billion decisions a day that would otherwise have required human labor. (see Appendix C for sources) These numbers answer the question of "what happens after the MVD": a validated project doesn't grow slowly — it scales in a jump. The client has already seen the value with their own eyes over those five days; everything left is just commercial process.

Of course, replicating this model has a threshold: behind it you need a mature platform foundation, or you won't even be able to stand up the environment in five days. For teams without a platform, an executable simplified version is the "two-week sprint validation": week one, get on-site, connect the data, define the metric; week two, build a prototype that solves only a single point problem but runs on real business, then demo it to the business side on the weekend and decide on the spot whether to move forward or stop. The format can be trimmed. The iron rules cannot.

## 2.6 Should Early-Stage Work Accommodate the Client's Existing Environment

There's a disagreement almost every project runs into during the MVD stage: to what extent should our solution accommodate the client's existing technical environment — that pile of systems that have been running for twenty years, the little tools departments built for themselves, and the strict security and compliance boundaries?

Both sides have a point. The "accommodate" camp says: validating inside the client's real constraints is the only kind of validation that's actually real. The "rebuild" camp says: doing deep integration work for an old environment that's about to be replaced anyway is wasting a precious validation window on engineering that's destined for the trash.

FDE practice offers a middle path: be compatible with the old system at the data layer, never accommodate the old system at the architecture layer. I call this "read old, write new."

At the data layer, be deeply compatible with the old environment. Read the client's data wherever it actually lives — even if that's on an old mainframe, in a spreadsheet on a shared drive, or behind the private interface of some ancient system. Old mainframes in finance and the heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in healthcare have historically been the core battlegrounds of deployment work. There's no shortcut around compatibility for reading data, because data is the precondition for validating value, and data will never move house to accommodate your architecture. The good news is that this layer of work is itself being transformed by AI: field mapping, cross-system data transfer, and pulling data out of interfaceless legacy systems — work that used to require a human to interpret — can now largely be handed off to agents. For instance, a browser agent can simulate manual operation to pull data out of an old system with no interface at all. Integration costs are dropping by orders of magnitude. (see Appendix C for sources)

At the architecture layer, resolutely refuse to become a parasite on the old environment. A system in its validation phase should run within its own controlled boundary, interacting with the old system through interfaces — not writing its code into the old system. There are three reasons for this. First, there's better than a fifty-percent chance the validation-phase solution itself gets scrapped and rewritten — the deeper it's embedded, the greater the waste. Second, writing into the old system means going through the client's change-management process, a cycle measured in months, which fundamentally conflicts with the MVD's week-scale cadence. Third, maintaining a posture of being able to "withdraw cleanly" is itself both a negotiating chip and an act of honesty — the FDE finishes and leaves; a parasite can never leave.

At the process layer, defer to people's habits, not the system's habits. This is the point most easily gotten backward by technical teams. The technical environment can be forced to bend; a person's habits must be accommodated. If a business user's core actions happen in spreadsheets and email, the MVD's interface should show up inside spreadsheet plugins and email, not demand that the user log into a brand-new portal. OpenAI's deployment at BBVA cut in through the ChatGPT interface that 120,000 employees were already using, rather than building something new from scratch — a model example of deferring to habit. Remember the cause of death from Section 2.1: employees being unwilling to adopt new tools ranks first among the five roadblocks. **The biggest enemy of a new system isn't the old system — it's old habits.**

## 2.7 User Research That Puts Action Before Words

To close this chapter, let's pull the camera back to the source of the methodology and talk about where FDE-style user research diverges from traditional research.

Traditional research's creed is "ask": surveys, interviews, focus groups. The FDE's creed is "watch" and "do" — action over words. There are three reasons for this, each building on the last.

First layer: clients don't know what they want. This isn't a put-down — it's a fact of cognition. Facing an entirely new category — intelligence analysis software in 2004, AI agents in 2025 — users have no frame of reference to describe what they need. Palantir's demo loop works precisely because it doesn't ask "what do you want," it says "here's something I built, tell me what's wrong with it." People's ability to judge "what's wrong" is far stronger than their ability to imagine "what I want." Finding fault with a solution is a human gift; describing an ideal solution out of thin air is a human weakness.

Second layer: what clients say and what they do are two different things. The example of the group of lawyers from Section 2.1 is the best proof of this. Survey the question "are you willing to use specialized legal AI," and procurement will tell you the appetite is strong — after all, they just spent fifty thousand dollars on a tool. But observe the lawyers' actual behavior, and they're drafting contracts with ChatGPT. In an enterprise context, "saying" gets contaminated by too many factors: political correctness, politeness toward a vendor, protecting one's own role. Only behavior doesn't lie. The shadow-work method observes behavior; the MVD measures behavior — both are built on the foundation that "doing is truer than saying."

Third layer: the highest-quality research happens through shared labor. In an interview, the client is a "research subject" — guarded, performing. Working side by side, the client is a colleague — relaxed, genuine. During those two or three days when FDEs and the client's technical staff write code back-to-back at the bootcamp, the density of information exchanged exceeds any formal research session — the client will casually mention, in the middle of debugging, things like "honestly, we've never trusted that field" or "on paper this process goes through the system, but in reality people are still doing it over the phone." These things never surface in a formal interview, because they look "unofficial." But they are exactly the critical intelligence that determines whether a deployment succeeds or fails.

There's a new variable from 2026 worth adding here: interviewing itself is being reshaped by AI. Ding Xindong, CTO of Tezign, describes an approach where instead of sending out structured questionnaires, they use an agent to conduct autonomous interviews with an entire company's staff — tailoring the conversation differently depending on the scenario and each person's actual pain points, and finally aggregating everything into a single global diagnostic map showing what stage each product line is at, what its pain points are, and what approach would suit moving it forward. A questionnaire produces answers; an interview surfaces pain points — AI has driven down the cost of the latter. (see Appendix C for sources)

Put all three layers together, and the FDE's research methodology boils down to one sentence: **use the shadow-work method to find the pain point, use the minimum viable deployment to validate the solution, and use shared labor to win the truth.**

At this point, the right problem has been locked in, and the value has been provisionally validated. The next battlefield is turning that validated point of value into an actual signed contract, and into a relationship that's actually just beginning — how to win the client.
