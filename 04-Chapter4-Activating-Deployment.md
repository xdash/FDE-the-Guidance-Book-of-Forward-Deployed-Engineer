## 4.1 Going Live Is Not Activation: The "First-Day Curse" of Enterprise Deployment

Let's first define the core concept of this chapter. In consumer internet, "activation" means new users completing a key behavior and experiencing the product's "aha moment." In enterprise deployment, activation means **the target user group forming a stable habit of using the system in their daily work** — not applause at a demo meeting, but the system still being used at high frequency three months later, with no one chasing anyone to use it.

In other words: an enterprise software "go-live" is an administrative event, while "activation" is a behavioral event. The former can be celebrated; only the latter actually counts.

A set of numbers from that MIT report is worth putting up again at the start of this chapter: only about 40% of enterprises provide employees with an official AI tool subscription, while as many as 90% of employees routinely use personal consumer-grade AI tools for their work. This means the real state of many enterprises is a "dual track": the official system idles while shadow AI runs rampant. The system went live; activation never happened.

Why do enterprise deployments so commonly die at the activation stage? Because they must clear three gates, and these gates are nothing like the ones consumer products face. Consumer products die from being "not fun"; enterprise deployments die in the users' hands: users find it inconvenient — if it adds even one more step than the old habit, no one uses it; users find it untrustworthy — get it wrong once, and trust drops to zero; users find it irrelevant to them — and then even fewer will touch it. None of these three gates can be cleared back at headquarters — you can only get through them in the customer's conference rooms, workshops, and workstations. This is why activation is the FDE's home turf, and it's also the biggest difference from traditional "deliver and leave" implementation: traditional implementation treats the sign-off document as the finish line; the FDE treats the change in customer behavior as the finish line.

The seven sections of this chapter correspond to the seven weapons of activation.

## 4.2 Rapid Iteration: The "Hotfix" Culture of the Deployment Period

Internet products iterate through data experiments, closing in on the optimum through small, fast steps. In enterprise deployment scenarios, rigorous data experiments aren't possible — samples are too small, there's too much interference — but the spiritual core of that approach, fast, incremental, evidence-based iteration, becomes a way of working during the deployment period: responding to every small user complaint as fast as fixing a live production incident — I call this "the hotfix."

Consumer products iterate on a "version" cadence — weekly, biweekly releases. The FDE's deployment-period iteration cadence is measured in "days" or even "hours": in the morning a business user says, "this output is missing the supplier code field," and by afternoon the field has been added; today the shop floor supervisor says, "I can't tap this accurately wearing gloves," and tomorrow the button is doubled in size. The meaning of this response speed to the user goes far beyond the feature itself — **every overnight fix is a deposit into the user's trust account**. In the very first month, users form a judgment: "this team is for real" versus "just another one that delivers and disappears."

The hotfix culture has three points of execution.

First, feedback must go directly to the person writing the code, with no telephone-game intermediary in between. In the old model, user feedback had to pass through customer success, product management, and scheduling before finally reaching the engineer — losing half its context at every handoff, and by the time it made it onto a sprint, a week had gone by and the user's enthusiasm had cooled. Under the FDE model, feedback goes directly to the person writing the code — ideally that person is sitting right next to the user. This is exactly the loop Palantir shortens: the on-site deployment engineer can build, test, learn, and feed back on the spot, without waiting for a formal product requirement to work its way through the process.

Second, iteration priority is ranked by "usage-blocking severity," not "feature importance." The trade-off logic during deployment is the reverse of the trade-off logic during the product-building phase. In the product phase, requirements are ranked by strategic value; during deployment, the question is "what's blocking tomorrow's use" — if a color scheme makes shop-floor workers feel "this was built for office white-collars," that's the top priority; a powerful forecasting feature the user can't currently make use of gets pushed to after activation is complete. Win adoption first, win depth second.

Third, ask one question at the end of every day: what did today's change make smoother for the user tomorrow? Iterating this way isn't just "answering every request" — it's actively eliminating every point of friction between the user and the system. A fix isn't just defect removal — every fix is a deliberate act of activation design: you are dismantling, one by one, the friction points between the user and the system. The list of friction points is hidden in the process maps and daily observations from onboarding due diligence.

OpenAI's FDE methodology has a corresponding rhythm breakdown: upfront co-creation (on-site whiteboard alignment), validation (building the evaluation system), and delivery (multi-day on-site build). Note that the delivery stage is still measured in "multi-day on-site" units — the ability to fix things this fast comes from having a person stationed right next to the problem.

## 4.3 Iterating Inside the Customer's Environment: Quality Improvement Driven by Evaluation Systems

If Section 4.2 was about the "speed" of iteration, this section is about its "sense of direction." In AI deployment, the vehicle for that sense of direction is a practice that only became mainstream after 2024: the **evaluation system** — using a batch of standard cases to continuously score the system's outputs.

Traditional software quality has only two states: the function works or it doesn't, the test passes or it fails. AI system quality is continuous, probabilistic, and context-dependent — the same answer can be stunning in a demo and a disaster in a specific business context. Worse still, the authority to define "good" belongs to the business side, not engineering: an answer the model considers perfect might strike a business expert as amateurish at a glance. Without this evaluation ruler, iterating an AI deployment is running blindfolded — you think you're optimizing, but you're actually drifting randomly.

The engineering practice of evaluation systems has settled into three steps among leading teams.

Step one: grow it out of real cases. The evaluation set cannot be written by engineers from scratch — it must come from the customer's real business material. The essence of this step is making the business expert's implicit judgment explicit and executable as a ruler.

Step two: make the business side the judges. An evaluation system is not an engineer's private plaything — the reviewers must include the business side. The best practice is to shape evaluation into a form business experts can participate in: side-by-side output comparisons, simple better/worse annotations, regular review sessions. This process has a double payoff: the evaluation set gets more accurate, and the business side's understanding of the system deepens — they watch the system answer better and better on their own cases, and the accumulation of trust comes from seeing it with their own eyes, not from being told about it in a report.

Step three: wire the evaluation system into the production loop. Going live is not the endpoint of evaluation. Continuously collect real inputs and outputs in production, sample and evaluate them regularly, and alert immediately if the score drops — this turns "quality" from a one-time pre-launch sign-off into continuous care spanning the whole lifecycle. What users are most sensitive to isn't average quality, it's the stability of quality: the trust collapse caused by a single wrong answer takes ten correct answers to rebuild.

The John Deere collaboration is the most complete public example of "evaluation-system-first," and it's worth slowing down to examine closely. The problem this company set out to solve was herbicide waste: traditional sprayers blanket the entire field, whereas its "See & Spray" technology uses 36 cameras plus machine vision to spray only weeds while moving at 12 to 15 miles per hour — covering the area of three soccer fields per minute. The ambition of precision agriculture is enormous: the U.S. plants 12 trillion corn and soybean plants each year; the best farmland yields 200 bushels per acre, while top growers achieve 600 — "if every single plant could be individually cared for, yields could change dramatically." Those are the actual words of Justin Rose, a John Deere technology executive.

But farmers don't want technology — they want trustworthy advice. OpenAI's front-line deployment engineers flew to Iowa and went out into the fields with agronomists, first reviewing hundreds of real work cases together with experts to build a custom evaluation system, then rapidly iterating the model — and having to keep pace with the farming season, because missing the planting window means losing a whole year. The final result: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmer engagement frequency increased sixfold. Note the order of these two numbers: **"good" as defined by the evaluation system came first, and the "good" delivered by the model came second**. (see Appendix C for sources)

The deeper significance of the evaluation system is this: put simply, it turns the veteran expert's mental phrase "what counts as good" into a scoring standard the system executes automatically, every day. This is precisely a microcosm of the FDE model — the customer's knowledge is no longer just text in a requirements document, it becomes a living ruler inside the system.

## 4.4 Finding Another Way In, Lowering the Barrier to Use

Getting the user to value with the fewest possible actions is common sense in product design. But the barriers to using enterprise systems are usually obstacles outside the system itself. There are four "barrier-lowering" techniques repeatedly validated in deployment practice.

Technique one: embed within the interface the user already has. Wherever the user's main battlefield is, that's where your system should appear: if they work in spreadsheets, build a spreadsheet plugin; if they approve things by email, let the approval happen inside the email; if they work in a ticketing system, embed the AI suggestion right into the ticket card. Requiring them to log into a new system is equivalent to putting up a wall between you and them that they have to climb over every single day. BBVA's entry point into OpenAI's product was the ChatGPT interface already used by its 120,000 employees; Anthropic lets its model enter the customer's existing workflow tools via open protocols — both follow the same logic. **Every additional login costs you a slice of the activation rate**.

BBVA is the best example of "entering the organization by riding existing habits." It began collaborating with OpenAI in May 2024, and the first step was issuing only 3,300 ChatGPT Enterprise accounts — not a company-wide push, but letting seed users experiment on their own first. Employees quickly and spontaneously created more than 20,000 custom mini-assistants, roughly 4,000 of which saw heavy, frequent use; management didn't ban "shadow AI" — instead it did the opposite: "We give people a safe platform where they feel free to try things." Alongside this came structured training: 250 executives (including the chairman himself) took the training first, an "AI Pioneers Network" was built across the bank, and a group of advanced users internally nicknamed "AI geeks" was cultivated. The data over a year later: employees saved an average of about 3 hours per week, with 83% actively using it weekly. In December 2025, the two sides went on to jointly announce a bank-wide rollout: 25 countries, 120,000 employees, and the launch of an end-to-end transformation roadmap called "Eight Things." From 3,300 to 11,000 to 120,000 — every expansion happened only after the usage data from the previous step had been made public. This is activation achieved by "letting the habit walk on its own." (see Appendix C for sources)

Technique two: the activation rate is hidden in the defaults. A new user's first reaction when facing a blank system is "now what," and most churn happens in those three seconds. The FDE's solution is to pre-install "the first use": pre-set templates, pre-filled examples (based on the customer's own data), pre-configured guidance (the moment they open it for the first time, there's already a to-do list waiting that's specific to them). On day one, the system should already understand what the user might need to do better than the user does — this is exactly where the observations from those days of on-site hovering pay off.

Technique three: translate "asking the AI" into "clicking a button." Enterprise users' fluency with "conversing with AI" varies far more than expected. Making the user compose their own prompt is equivalent to offloading the engineering burden onto the person least equipped to bear it. The mature approach is to package high-frequency scenarios into one-click actions: "generate last week's exception report," "reconcile this batch of invoices," "draft this customer letter" — behind each button sits a complete, evaluation-tuned prompt and process. Leave conversational interaction for exploration; leave button-based interaction for the everyday.

Technique four: be a "co-pilot" first, before talking about "autopilot." When facing high-risk, high-resistance processes, don't push straight for full automation. Let the system first exist in the role of "advisor" — AI drafts, human confirms; AI flags, human decides. Through repeated confirmations, users build a calibrated trust in the system's judgment; only once the confirmation-approval rate reaches a certain level does automation come up for discussion. John Deere's solution still preserves the agronomist's final decision-making authority to this day; agentic designs in financial compliance scenarios universally retain human gatekeeping. The co-pilot strategy isn't conservatism — it's the optimal path between activation rate and risk.

## 4.5 The Long, Drawn-Out Integration War

Every veteran FDE carries a body full of scars, and they all come from the same war: the integration war against the customer's "legacy systems" — those old systems that have run inside enterprises for years and that no one dares touch.

The brutality of this war exceeds the imagination of anyone who has never spent time on the front line. a16z's description hits it right on the head: the context AI applications need — historical records, business logic, permission systems — is all locked inside the enterprise's internal databases, interfaces, and workflows, and the work of connecting them "was never optional — it's a required course." Old mainframes in finance, heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in healthcare, dozens of workshop systems each doing their own thing in manufacturing — what these environments have in common: documentation is outdated, interfaces are broken, and half the people who understood them have already retired.

There are four strategic points to this war.

- **Treat integration as a campaign, not an errand.** Integration work in most project plans is written as a single line — "system connectivity: 2 weeks" — and then balloons into four months during execution. The root of this error is treating integration as a technical chore — it's simultaneously technical archaeology, organizational politics, and data governance — a genuine hard-fought battle in every sense. The correct posture: map out the system landscape during onboarding due diligence, rank and schedule integration risks, and gnaw on the hardest bones earliest — integration carries the greatest uncertainty, and every day you delay starting it, the entire project timeline runs one more day exposed and unprotected.

- **Second, solve the data problem before the model problem.** That report contains a widely cited insight: the underlying reason many AI projects fail is "garbage in, garbage out" — AI is connected to ungoverned data sources, ten versions of the same document get hit at random, and naturally the output can't be trusted. Palantir's solution is the ontology: model the enterprise's data assets into a layer with business semantics first, clarify "which field is authoritative, which version is valid, who is allowed to see what," and let AI run on top of this foundation. The universal lesson from this path is: **data governance is not preliminary work for an AI project — it IS the AI project itself.**

The U.S. Navy's "shipbuilding operating system" is the most spectacular footnote to this lesson. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Palantir's CEO jointly announced a $448 million contract, initially covering two major shipyards, three naval yards, and a hundred suppliers. The data environment in shipbuilding is a textbook-level disaster: ERP systems, decades-old databases, and paper blueprints all coexisting. And two numbers from the pilot phase silenced everyone: at General Dynamics Electric Boat, the nuclear submarine manufacturer, **submarine production scheduling was compressed from 160 human-hours to under 10 minutes**; at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, **materials audit time was compressed from weeks to under an hour**.

The Secretary of the Navy specifically emphasized: "This is not a concept, not a pilot, not research — this is already being done." (see Appendix C for sources) Note the sequence in this case: it's only after a great deal of effort was put into connecting the data into a unified semantic layer that the efficiency miracle became possible — never the other way around.

The same logic recurs across three completely different industries. Fast-food chain Wendy's: scheduling syrup restocking across 6,450 stores in North America used to require 15 employees checking things over an entire day — after connecting to a data platform, a plan comes out in five minutes. Mortgage giant Fannie Mae: uses AI to identify mortgage fraud with a detection rate exceeding 99%, far surpassing the original rules-based system. Citibank: customer credit approval compressed from hours to minutes — AI reads through credit history, transaction habits, industry risk, and affiliated enterprises in one pass. Three industries, one common precondition: only after scattered data is organized into a semantic layer machines can understand does intelligence have anywhere to land. (see Appendix C for sources)

- **Third, use AI to fight AI's own integration war.** An important variable emerging after 2025: the integration work itself is beginning to be automated by AI. The scenario a16z envisioned has partly become reality already — for legacy systems with no API, browser agents simulate a human to retrieve data; field mapping, format conversion, and interface documentation interpretation are increasingly handed off to models. The self-demand of leading teams is worth quoting: "automate the integration process as much as humanly possible — process mining, data pipelines, system connectivity, interface documentation organization — this speed advantage compounds." Using AI to do the work of AI deployment may be the most elegant thing about this role.

- **Fourth, know when to route around something instead of conquering it.** Not every legacy system is worth a head-on integration. For some systems the right solution is "shadow reading" (read-only snapshots, nightly sync), for others it's "manual ferrying" (keeping a human-in-the-loop step during the transition period), and for still others it's simply "declared isolation" (the data for that process is explicitly stated to be out of scope for this project, communicated clearly to the customer). An engineer's pride always wants to conquer every fortress; the FDE's judgment is expressed in choosing the battlefield — remember that your goal is not total technical victory, it's the customer's outcome.

## 4.6 Change Management: Getting the Customer's Organization to Back You Up

This section addresses the largest source of soft resistance to activation: the organization. Among the report's five major roadblocks, "employee resistance" and "change management" together account for nearly half. Getting the technology live only requires engineers; changing behavior requires the entire organization.

In the FDE context, change management is fundamentally about managing three groups of people.

- **The Champion | your internal ally:** Behind every successful deployment stands a champion inside the customer organization: someone who genuinely believes in this, and is willing to stake their own credibility to clear the path for you. David Wakeling of Allen & Overy is a textbook case — as the firm's Head of Markets Innovation, he was Harvey's internal counterpart, advocate, and umbrella within the firm. The key points of managing a champion: give them material they can tell a story with (a story worth telling externally), give them career wins (turn their vision into a highlight of their own career), give them a sense of security (you have their back when things fail). A well-cared-for champion is worth more than ten product pitch sessions.

- **The Influencer | the informal opinion leader:** Every organization has a group of uncrowned kings: the senior analyst, the veteran on the shop floor, the person in the department everyone goes to with "just ask them." They don't hold formal power, but they hold trust. Their single comment "this thing actually works" outweighs three company-wide emails from management; their comment "just window dressing" is enough to let the system die quietly at ground level. During the activation period, deliberately manage this group: let them try it first, take every one of their complaints seriously, and make the adoption of their suggestions visible — "this field was added based on Master Wang's feedback." Turning influencers into co-authors is the shortest path to breaking the mindset of "I don't use what I didn't build."

- **The Displaced | the interest groups touched by the solution:** Automation inevitably redistributes work, and redistributing work inevitably creates people who are displaced: approval roles whose sense of presence gets compressed, departments whose information barriers get pierced through, veteran employees whose "unique craft" gets replaced. Ignore them, and they become the system's most stubborn underground resistance — passive non-cooperation, spreading stories of incidents, voting against it at sign-off. Mature change management designs "a path forward for the displaced" in advance: redirect freed-up manpower toward higher-value work (and publicly commit to no layoffs), transform "gatekeepers" into "coaches" (using the veteran's experience to train the system), and let the displaced see their place in the future. This is both humane and purely pragmatic — the cost of resistance far exceeds the cost of reassurance.

The collaboration between ticketing platform Vivid Seats and Sierra shows what "full customer-side mobilization" looks like during activation. After deciding to bring in the agent, the company's product, customer experience, and engineering teams all committed fully — "once the decision was made, we went all in 100%, with everyone retesting." The result: from kickoff to launch in under four weeks, post-launch self-service resolution rate up 40%, and customer satisfaction up 35%.

But even more valuable is what happened afterward: with routine issues taken over by the agent, the customer experience team shifted from "firefighting in the queue" to fixing root-cause process problems, and even had bandwidth left over for delight-driven projects like "surprise upgrades." Meanwhile the agent's conversation data began feeding back into the product — "if ten thousand people ask about the same feature in a month, we can immediately move it up in priority. The best moment is when a pattern drives a product improvement that means the user never needs to ask for help at all." (see Appendix C for sources) **The highest state of activation is when the system makes itself unnecessary** — this case also foreshadows Chapter 7's theme of "on-site work feeding back into product."

Corresponding examples are also emerging among Chinese companies. Ren Xiliang, head of efficiency engineering at e-commerce company Dewu, broke down AI transformation for a company with tens of thousands of employees into three steps: first, build company-wide consensus, lower the barrier to using the tool, and get everyone using it; second, split business scenarios into four quadrants by tolerance for error, and hand scenarios with high tolerance for error (such as business analytics) to AI first; third, set up a dedicated knowledge-operations team that embeds within business teams to move experts' tacit knowledge out of their heads and into a space visible to AI. Consensus, scenarios, knowledge — the order cannot be reversed: first get people willing to use it, then pick the right places to use it, and finally give the AI something to work with. (see Appendix C for sources)

There is only one ultimate test standard for change management: **whether the system is still being used after your team has withdrawn from the site**. If things are lively while you're present and quickly cool off after you leave, that isn't activation — it's a dance performance. A truly activated organization keeps moving forward on its own.

## 4.7 I, Robot — Automating the Delivery Work Itself

Delivering automation to the customer is the product itself; automating your own delivery work is efficiency. This section is about the latter — it's the first lever by which FDE teams escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount."

An astonishing proportion of the actions in an FDE's daily work are templatable repetitive labor: initializing the deployment environment for a new customer, standard pipelines for data ingestion, filling out security review questionnaires, pre-launch checklists, the format of periodic reports back to the customer. Every action done for the second time should trigger a question: "can this become a script, a template, or a checklist?"

The practices of leading teams have already crystallized into four categories of assets.

- **Deployment templates:** Package environment setup, permission configuration, and monitoring integration into one-click infrastructure-as-code. On the new customer's first day, you can spin up a standardized environment in their cloud environment right away, instead of configuring everything from scratch over a week. Decagon can compress deployment for simple scenarios down to 15 days, backed by a highly templated integration layer. (see Appendix C for sources)

- **Integration component library:** Connectors for mainstream enterprise systems (pre-built system connectivity components), written once and reused everywhere. Palantir's ontology model essentially takes this logic to the extreme: the output of data ingestion isn't a one-time pipeline, but a reusable data asset carrying business meaning.

- **Checklist culture:** Security review checklists, launch-readiness checklists, handover checklists for wrapping up on-site work. The checklist is humanity's oldest tool against complexity, and it's also the bedrock of quality stability for FDE teams — it converts "dependence on individual experience" into "dependence on organizational memory."

- **Automated reporting:** Weekly reports to the customer, and on-site intelligence back to the company, should both be semi-automatically generated. An FDE's time is too expensive to be spent on copy-pasting — and even less should "forgetting to report" cut off the loop between the field and the product that Chapter 7 will discuss.

Just how considerable the compounding effect of automation can be is confirmed from one angle: a16z lists "building or acquiring tools to automate service delivery" as a key recommendation for building a forward-deployed team, and judges this to be the key variable that lets this generation of AI companies move faster and lower their customer-acquisition cost threshold compared to the previous generation of enterprise software companies. Sierra's publicly reported fastest launch case took four weeks, and Palantir compressed its sales cycle from nine months to a few weeks — neither achieved through engineers working overtime, but by turning yesterday's delivery into today's scaffolding.

This section also answers the most common skepticism — "isn't FDE just human-wave tactics?" The essence of a human wave isn't having a lot of people — it's every person doing non-reusable, one-time labor. **When every delivery by an FDE team paves the road for the next delivery, it's no longer a human wave — it's a machine that is self-accelerating.**

The seven weapons of activation deployment have now been covered: the speed of hotfixes, the direction of the evaluation system, the cleverness of lowering barriers, the patience of the integration war, the soft skill of change management, and the leverage of automation. The system is alive, and the people have moved too. But the cruelty of the enterprise business is this: activation only extends life — renewal is what means survival. The next chapter is about holding onto the renewal.
