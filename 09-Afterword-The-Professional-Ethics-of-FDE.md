# Afterword: The Professional Ethics of the FDE

Every book about methodology must, in its closing pages, address a question: once you have mastered this method, where are its boundaries? And the topic this book must address is heavier than most methodologies. Because what the FDE holds in their hands is not ordinary technology—**it is the deepest secrets of the client's organization, and an ever-growing power to make decisions on people's behalf.**

The nature of FDE work determines what you will see. To deliver well, you must look at the client's most honest operating data—including the ugly parts; you must map the organization's power structure—including who is incompetent and who has lost influence; you must touch core business processes—including the workarounds that skirt gray areas. The client opens all of this to you based on a simple understanding: you are here to help. This trust is the foundation of the FDE model, and it takes only a single overreach to destroy it.

I believe the professional ethics of the FDE have at least six bottom lines, which I offer here for all colleagues in the field to reflect on together.

**First, data sovereignty belongs to the client.** Not a single byte of data seen on a client site should ever appear where it does not belong—it should not enter AI training data (unless explicitly authorized by contract), should not become case material (unless the client agrees in writing), and should not become conversation fodder for your next job. The principle of minimizing data access is not just a technical standard, it is a professional ethic: don't look at what you don't need to see, and anonymize what can be anonymized.

**Second, report results honestly, including bad news.** In an outcome-based pricing model, the greatest moral hazard is dressing up results—packaging "the system went live" as "value was realized," and packaging correlation as causation. The value measurement system described in Chapter 6 can be either the most honest tool or the most refined lie-making machine; the difference lies only in the person's intent. The FDE's foundation is "daring to be tested"—and that means also having the courage to present the results when the test reveals something bad.

**Third, do not manufacture dependency, and do not sell fear.** This industry harbors two hidden evils: one is deliberately building systems as black boxes so the client can never leave you; the other is exaggerating panic—"you'll die without AI"—to close a deal. Gartner predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon field-deployment-led AI solutions due to cost and skills hollowing-out—this prediction is a warning bell for the entire industry. A healthy FDE model should be one where "you leave when the job is done": knowledge is transferred to the client, and capability accrues to the client's own team. **Make the client strong, not addicted.**

**Fourth, take "the people being replaced" seriously.** The systems an FDE delivers do, in many scenarios, genuinely replace part of someone's job. This book has covered extensive techniques for "change management," but beyond the technique lies ethics: don't celebrate efficiency in front of those being displaced; don't reduce people to a "cost line item" in your proposal without offering them a path forward; don't pretend not to know whose fate you are changing. The neutrality of technology is a myth; the choices of those who deploy it are the reality.

**Fifth, say no to "things the client asks for but shouldn't be done."** You will encounter requests that skirt the edge of compliance: "help us make employee behavior monitoring a bit more granular," or "these data practices are a bit murky on compliance, but let's just take the job and deal with it later." The metaphor of the "French waiter" has its deepest meaning here—true professionalism is not satisfying every client demand, but having the courage to guide the client toward what is genuinely in their interest, and does no harm to the world. The confidence to say no comes from having other clients on your books; so ethics has always been tied to business model as well.

**Sixth, remember that you represent "the technology" itself.** For many clients, you are the first human face of AI they ever encounter. Every exaggeration you make draws down the entire industry's credibility in their eyes; every promise you keep deposits trust back into the industry's account. As AI penetrates ever more deeply into how society functions, the deployer becomes the last translator between technology and everyday human life—and the cost of a distorted translation is paid by everyone.

Palantir itself is a deeply controversial company: its history of serving intelligence and military agencies has made many people wary of everything associated with it—including the FDE model. My extensive citation of its methodology in this book does not amount to an endorsement of all of its client choices. Quite the opposite—precisely because it serves such sensitive domains, the discipline embedded in its engineering culture around permissions, auditing, and minimal need-to-know is all the more worth learning from. **The greater a deployer's capability, the more they need to write their boundaries before their methods.**

FDE is a young profession, and no one has yet written its code of conduct. I hope this book can be its first boundary marker.
