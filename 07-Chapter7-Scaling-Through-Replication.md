# Chapter 7 Scaling Through Replication

> "If you don't convert on-site learning into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects."
> — Barry, former Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer

## 7.1 Using Replicability to Leverage Delivery

The highest state of internet growth is having one user bring in more users — the growth engine shifts from external acquisition to internal generation. The FDE model has to answer the same kind of question: **after finishing one customer, the next customer should be easier — not because you added headcount, but because you banked the lessons from the last one.**

This proposition is the "last mile" of the entire book, because it is the last line of defense between the FDE model and the consulting or outsourcing business. If you strip this chapter's content out of the playbook from the first six chapters, what's the outcome? An elite team capable of fighting hard battles takes on one customer, delivers for that customer, earns enough to cover costs, then takes the next one — revenue growth scales perfectly linearly with headcount growth. Congratulations, you've reinvented the consulting firm.

Barry states this dividing line most bluntly in his memoir: "If you don't convert on-site learning into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects." McGrew frames it from another angle: the sign that the FDE model is working is that "the amount of customization required declines with each subsequent customer" — if the customization work you do doesn't decrease as your customer count grows, you're not running FDE, you're running a headcount-billing business.

The leverage of replicability comes in three tiers, each harder than the last — and each more valuable than the last.

- **Tier 1 Leverage | Knowledge Replication (the Playbook):** Write "how it's done" down as documentation — playbook templates, checklists, training materials. It converts dependence on individual experience into dependence on organizational memory: onboarding time for new hires drops from a year to three months. This is the most basic form of leverage, and where most teams stop.

- **Tier 2 Leverage | Component Replication (Tools and Code):** Turn "what has been done" into "what can be used directly" — integration connectors, evaluation frameworks, deployment templates, industry data models. The next team's starting point is no longer zero — it stands on the shoulders of the previous project. The automation assets discussed in Section 4.7 belong to this tier.

- **Tier 3 Leverage | Product Replication (Platform Capability):** Abstract "recurring customization" into a standard platform capability — from then on, this requirement no longer needs any engineer on site at all. Palantir's ontology, Decagon's agent workflow configuration, and Sierra's agent platform are all products of this third tier. What this tier of leverage changes is the cost structure of the entire business: **the first two tiers of leverage lower delivery cost; the third tier eliminates delivery cost.**

The progression through these three tiers is the complete version of McGrew's line about "gravel roads and highways": the FDE builds the gravel road (solving the problem right in front of them), the component team lays the gravel roadbed (making the next vehicle's trip easier), and the platform team pours the highway (from then on, everyone can travel it). The remaining seven sections of this chapter cover this full engineering process, from road-building to road-paving.

## 7.2 Bad News Travels Fast — Spreading the Post-Mortems of Failed Deployments

Turning incidents into assets is a scarce organizational capability. An FDE organization's attitude toward failure determines whether it can build replication capability at all: **failure is the raw material of replication; an organization that covers up its failures is burying a gold mine as if it were trash.**

Let's be clear about one thing first: failure in the FDE model is not an accident — it is inevitable. Barry's recollection is unsparing: "There's plenty of failure — huge amounts of time, money, and travel expense, burned spectacularly." Palantir has written the acceptance of failure directly into its institutional practice: treat a pilot like a venture-capital bet — most will die, and once they die, a post-mortem is mandatory. He specifically reminds people to say thank you to the projects that crashed, the teams that worked themselves to exhaustion for nothing, the engineers who burned out — the lessons were bought and paid for by them. This is the hardest thing to learn, because engineers' nature is to celebrate success and hide failure.

Turning failure into an organizational asset requires three mechanisms.

- **Decriminalizing the post-mortem:** The one rule of a post-mortem meeting: focus on the event, not the person. The moment a post-mortem gets tied to performance reviews, people start dressing up their failures — and a dressed-up failure has zero teaching value. Decriminalization does not mean no accountability; it means redefining "accountability" as "whether the lessons were extracted honestly and completely." A project lead who ran an honest post-mortem on a failure may contribute more to the organization than one who succeeded by luck.

- **Structuring the lessons:** The output of a post-mortem cannot be a vague "we'll be more careful next time" sentiment — it must be retrievable, actionable knowledge: What are the red flags for this type of customer? Which integration assumption got overturned? Which evaluation metric was designed wrong? These conclusions should go into the "minefield" section of the playbook and into the due-diligence checklist for new projects — so the next team's "pre-engagement due diligence" automatically carries the historical lessons forward. **A failure is only truly learned once it has been written into the process.**

- **Sharing failure — in moderation — with the outside world:** This layer is the most counterintuitive: sharing lessons from failure externally (after anonymizing sensitive details) is an extremely efficient form of trust marketing. As discussed in Section 3.5, Barry's own "airing the dirty laundry" essay became one of the best pieces of evangelism for the Palantir model — readers can tell the difference between someone reciting booth-talk and someone telling the truth from the trenches. A team willing to publicly dissect its own failures is signaling: "We have already paid tuition for this industry's wrong turns" — which is exactly what enterprise customers most want to hear.

## 7.3 Riding the Wave: Standing at the Industry's Tipping Point

Borrowing the attention of an external event for your own purposes is a classic move in marketing. Teams in the FDE space are currently standing at an epic tipping point — and the window of that tipping point will not stay open forever.

The industry tipping point of 2025–2026 was formed by the convergence of several currents: that famous report made "a 95% failure rate" a chronic worry for every enterprise CIO — and a customer's chronic worry is your opportunity; OpenAI and Anthropic both launched deployment-focused ventures on the same day, pushing "deployment" onto the front page of business news — when giants spend money educating the market, the whole industry benefits; and an 800% surge in FDE hiring made the role a media fixture — even "whether or not to hire an FDE" has become a source of corporate anxiety. Together, these three currents produced a rare situation: **customers are actively seeking you out, to discuss the very thing you want to sell them.**

The first move of riding the wave is "defining the agenda." Attention around a tipping point is a public resource — whoever's content defines the agenda is the one who harvests the attention. a16z set the tone for the entire sector with a single landmark industry essay; communities like OpenFDE are aggregating not just practitioners but also the power to define the discourse. For an individual company, the best way to seize this defining power is to make public the things kept locked in the drawer: playbooks, failure post-mortems, value data — proprietary, first-hand insight is the only currency that buys agenda-setting power.

The second move is "binding to the industry agenda." The biggest driver of enterprise AI procurement is upgrading from "efficiency" to "survival" — CEOs are being grilled at board meetings about "what is our AI strategy," and that anxiety transmits directly into budget. Translate your offering into "boardroom language": not "we can help you optimize your customer service workflow," but "we can give you a quantifiable AI scorecard to bring to your next board meeting." The same delivery, anchored to a different level of agenda, reaches a different budget pool.

The third move is "building assets during the window, rather than spending down the window." Tipping points end. The discussion sparked by a report will cool off; media buzzwords will go stale. The most valuable thing during a tipping-point window is not signing a few extra deals — it's converting the attention the wave brought into long-term assets: lighthouse case studies, authority over industry methodology, a replicable delivery system. **After the wave passes, those swimming naked get exposed by the tide going out; those who built assets are already on shore.**

## 7.4 Building a Word-of-Mouth Loop Outside the Customer

Propagation that runs on word of mouth rather than advertising, in the FDE business, mainly happens through three circles.

Circle One: the practitioner community. FDE is a professional community still in the process of forming — in 2025 it was still scattered inside individual companies; by 2026 there are already dedicated communities like OpenFDE, dedicated compensation reports, dedicated interview guides. The formation of a professional community is a gift from heaven for early movers: contribute content to the community (methodology, tools, data), and your name gets bound to this emerging profession. The way concepts spread has always followed this path: the early evangelists end up becoming synonymous with the concept itself.

Circle Two: the customer's industry community. Every industry has its own inner circle — bankers' forums, law-firm partner conferences, hospital administrators' summits. The trust dividend discussed in Section 3.3 mostly circulates within these closed circles. The ticket into these circles is not advertising — it's a customer advocate who gets invited to speak. Your job is to give that person something worth talking about, and to help them talk about it well. Ten minutes of a customer sharing at their own industry summit, on your behalf, beats a hundred days of your own booth at an industry trade show.

Circle Three: the ecosystem partner network. Section 3.7 covered the customer-acquisition value of ecosystem bundling; here we emphasize its propagation properties: cloud providers' solution architects, consulting firm advisors, integrators' project managers — these people are moving through different customer sites every day. The list of "teams you can trust" that they carry around in their heads is the fastest, most accurate word-of-mouth channel there is. The key to managing this circle is to help them "win" — let ecosystem partners gain business results, case studies, and reputation from working with you. **The people who help others succeed get remembered by the entire network.**

Combined, these three circles form a flywheel: delivery creates stories, stories spread through the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, and new opportunities create new stories. The difference between this flywheel and traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "value already delivered" — the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is making every bit of past effort keep attracting future customers.

## 7.5 Built-In Virality Factors in the Product

A good product spreads itself — a line of "sent from such-and-such email" in a signature was one of the earliest self-propagation designs. The systems an FDE delivers can also be pre-loaded with "built-in propagation mechanisms," turning the system itself into an ambassador for expansion.

Factor One: Visibility by design. The outputs of a system naturally circulate — weekly reports, analysis results, approval workflows. Make every piece of output carry its "origin": a footer on the report reading "generated by system X," a link in an anomaly alert leading to the handling console. When a beautifully produced analysis report gets forwarded to a director in the department next door, whoever sees it will naturally ask "what made this?" The essence of visibility-by-design is letting the system's value put itself on display within the daily workflow, rather than relying on dedicated presentations.

Factor Two: Collaborative access. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: cases flagged by an investigator need a supervisor's review; a reports produced by an analyst need business-side confirmation — every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. Users brought in this way aren't "being marketed to" — they're "being brought in by the work itself," and acceptance is entirely different.

Factor Three: A self-service exploration layer. Alongside your core delivery, leave room for a low-risk practice area (a sandbox): customer employees can try things out themselves, like "let AI help me do an analysis." Usage patterns that grow organically in the sandbox are often the most viable leads for expansion — a use case that a department employee comes up with on their own has deeper roots than one your sales team pitches ten times. **Let everyone help you discover; let the FDE handle the hard part.**

Two new products from 2026 turned this factor into an explicit feature. In March, Sierra launched "Ghostwriter": customers upload their own standard operating procedures, historical conversation logs, or even a photo of a whiteboard, or simply describe a goal in plain language, and the system automatically generates a production-ready agent, covering voice, chat, and email channels across more than thirty languages — for the first time, a customer's own business staff can "build an Agent themselves," and the forward deployment team shifts from "hand-building every single one" to "quality review and backstop." Decagon's agentic workflow configuration blazed this trail even earlier: a customer's customer-service supervisor writes the workflow in natural language ("for returns past 30 days, first check membership tier, and process it if it complies with policy"), without waiting for engineering to schedule the work — reportedly, Chime achieved a 70% self-service resolution rate, Duolingo achieved an 80% issue-routing rate, and fitness platform ClassPass cut customer service costs by 95%. (see Appendix C for sources) **Once the customer starts building for themselves, your role shifts from construction crew to design institute** — this is the ultimate form of Factor Three.

Factor Four: Cross-boundary evidence of value. Automatically generate a "quarterly value report" every quarter, and let it naturally flow up to the customer's executive tier. This report is both the renewal engineering discussed in Chapter 5 and a knock on the door for vertical expansion — every number the executives see plants a seed for "where else could this system still be worth deploying."

The shared principle behind all these built-in factors is: **if you want other people to propagate your work for you, you first have to make them genuinely realize real value from using it.** Any design built purely for the sake of propagation will be recognized in an enterprise setting as a "vendor gimmick" and will backfire. There is no viral spread in the enterprise market — only "a chain reaction of word of mouth": slow, but every step of it solid.

## 7.6 Navigating Organizational Politics During Scaling

The biggest obstacle to scaling has never been technology — it's people's turf.

When a system expands from one department to ten, you are effectively rewriting the power map inside the organization: information monopolies get broken (data that one department alone controlled is now visible company-wide); approval authority gets compressed (things AI can resolve in a second no longer need three days of layered sign-offs); professional barriers get flattened (a senior employee's "secret know-how" gets encoded into the system). Every one of these changes creates a silent enemy.

FDE veterans have distilled three principles for handling organizational politics.

- **Principle One: Every expansion must bring along a "local."** Every time you enter a new department, first find that department's "local kingpin" — the most respected senior employee — and invite them into the adaptation process: "What's different about this scenario in your department?" Their answer will make the solution more accurate, and their participation will make the solution travel unimpeded on their own turf. **People don't oppose things they helped build** — this is one of the most reliable levers in organizational behavior.

Harvey's expansion inside top-tier law firms is a textbook case of reading organizational politics correctly. Within a firm, there are three coexisting layers of people: partners — the ones who pay, but don't do the work themselves; associates — the ones who do the work, but don't sign off; and knowledge management attorneys — who touch neither directly, but can see the blind spots on both sides. A generic survey would flatten all three layers into the same set of dropdown options, producing a batch of distorted average scores; Harvey's forward deployment team instead designed separate interviews and demos for each of the three layers: show the partners "how much more profitable this deal becomes," show the associates "three fewer late nights this week," and show the knowledge management attorneys "the whole firm's tacit experience finally has somewhere to accumulate." A single deployment has to make all three layers feel like beneficiaries at the same time — leave out any one layer, and the system quietly gets shelved at that layer's corresponding step. (see Appendix C for sources)

- **Principle Two: Give every step of expansion its own "beneficiary."** Before expanding, work out exactly what each affected department gets: a department whose data silo just got pierced gains globally visible analytical capability; a department whose approval authority just got compressed gains the ability to focus on the exceptions (AI handles the routine, humans focus on the hard cases). Expansion with no beneficiary is invasion; expansion with a beneficiary is liberation — the only difference is whether you've thought through, and clearly articulated, exactly what they get.

- **Principle Three: Always leave the "old order" its dignity.** The process being replaced by the system was, once, also designed by someone capable; the experience being encoded was, once, also the life's work of some expert. In your rollout messaging, respecting the old order is respect for the people who maintained it: "This process has supported the company for the past decade — now we're going to take its ten years of accumulated experience and amplify it a hundredfold." An old world that is treated with respect won't cause trouble in the new one.

## 7.7 Using the Playbook to Bank Replication Efficiency

At the start of this chapter, the playbook was listed as Tier 1 leverage; this section unpacks its engineering details — because for most teams, the playbook becomes a document graveyard the moment it's written.

- **The playbook's first property: it must live inside the process, not sit in a knowledge base.** A playbook that can only be found by searching a document system might as well not exist. The effective approach is to embed it directly into the workflow: during a new project's due-diligence phase, the system automatically surfaces the due-diligence checklist for that customer type; during deployment, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; during the post-mortem, updating the playbook is a mandatory deliverable of the post-mortem itself. **Knowledge that isn't embedded in the process is just archaeological material.**

- **The playbook's second property: organize it by "scenario," not by "function."** A failing playbook is organized around the company's org chart ("delivery process," "technical specifications"); a successful one is organized by customer scenario ("anti-money-laundering scenario playbook," "manufacturing scheduling scenario playbook," "law firm knowledge base scenario playbook"). Each scenario playbook should have a fixed set of seven components: typical pain points and fit-check criteria, a due-diligence checklist (the high-risk red flags specific to this scenario), known minefields in data and integration, metric templates for evaluation and acceptance, a role map for change management, a reference structure for pricing and expansion, and a library of lessons from historical post-mortems. The fact that Harvey's law-firm playbook could be replicated at PwC and Cadwalader is because the experience from the first customer (Allen & Overy) was written into an inheritable scenario asset.

- **The playbook's third property: it must be "alive" — with an owner, a version, and a depreciation schedule.** Every scenario playbook is assigned an owner (usually the most senior delivery team), each project's conclusion forces a review-and-update pass, and every six months a full overhaul is performed — deleting what's outdated, merging what's duplicated. A playbook with no owner will rot within a year into a trap that misleads new hires — worse than having no playbook at all.

The relationship between the playbook, Tier 2 leverage (the component library), and Tier 3 leverage (the platform) is this: the playbook records "judgment," the component library banks "craft," and the platform hardens "capability." With all three in place, a newly assembled FDE squad can reach 80% of a veteran team's combat effectiveness within two weeks — that is the real meaning of replication.

## 7.8 The Planning and Refinement of Productization

The final section covers the crowning achievement of Tier 3 leverage: crystallizing on-site experience into a product — the alchemy at the heart of the entire FDE model.

Let's start with the most spectacular success story: Palantir's Foundry. Today it's a platform generating billions of dollars a year in revenue and supporting a company worth hundreds of billions of dollars in market cap — yet its core components were born on customer sites scattered across the globe: Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, Baku. Forward deployed engineers at each site built their own tools to solve local problems; after years of natural selection and platform-team consolidation, they eventually grew into a unified product. Barry describes this process as "entirely bottom-up": as of 2014, the company's product strategy was, literally, "strong opinions, loosely held" — letting a hundred flowers bloom on the ground, then absorbing whatever survived into the core.

This process is not a pastoral idyll — it has a rigorous internal logic that can be distilled into "the four questions of productization."

- **Question One:** Is this on-site solution a "one-off" or a "commonality"? The test: has the same type of problem independently shown up at three or more customers? The same pain point at three customers is a product signal; a special request from a single customer might just be an idiosyncrasy. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let "multiple forward deployment teams independently reinventing the same wheel" naturally reveal the commonality — once three teams have each independently built something similar, the platform team knows: it's time to absorb it.

- **Question Two:** Is the cost of generalizing smaller than the benefit? McGrew has warned: on-site code is "fast and dirty," and the cost of generalizing it "is often higher than writing the first version." Productization decisions must run this math: the investment required to generalize, weighed against the customization cost saved across future customers. If the math doesn't work, leave it at the component layer — don't force it onto the platform. Every capability on the platform is a lifetime maintenance commitment.

- **Question Three:** Can it be used by "people who can't write code"? The user of an on-site tool is an engineer; the user of a platform capability is everyone. The hardest step of productization is often not the technology, but the interaction design: turning an engineer's script into a feature a business user can configure. Decagon's agentic workflow configuration (letting operations staff define workflows in natural language) is a textbook example of this step — once you pick the right abstraction layer, the user base for that capability grows a hundredfold.

- **Question Four:** After absorption, is there still room left for the field? This is the most delicate balance: if the platform absorbs too much, field teams degenerate into mere configuration clerks, and the soul of the FDE model — on-site creativity — dies; if it absorbs too little, the leverage of scaling never materializes. Palantir's solution is to maintain "aggressive delegation from headquarters to the front line" — leadership sets only the objectives; the tactics belong to the field. **The platform's job is to make the common things easy; the field's job is to make the impossible things happen.**

The most powerful rebuttal from those who doubt this flywheel is always about gross margin: how can margins possibly stay high if you're sending people to the customer's site? History has answered this question three times. ServiceNow's gross margin at IPO was 63.2%, and it was criticized as "too much like a services company" — ten years later it was 79%, with a market cap approaching $200 billion. Workday's gross margin at IPO was 54.1% — today it's 76%. Palantir's was 79% at IPO, and by 2025 it had reached 82% — better than most "pure software" companies.

Venture firm Insight Partners put it perfectly: **the companies that heavily invested in implementation and services during the platform-transition period are exactly the ones that stuck it out while everyone else ran the opposite direction.** The secret is that gross margin can climb year after year — as the platform's accumulated depth thickens, customers themselves can do more and more of the implementation work (a "bootcamp" model has customers handling a large share of the integration work themselves), and service costs get continuously diluted. Ugly first, beautiful later; heavy first, light later. (see Appendix C for sources)

To close this chapter: a story about "betting on the right direction." When ChatGPT burst onto the scene in late 2022, everyone was guessing what Palantir would do. Shyam Sankar placed a bet that would later be quoted again and again: large models would rapidly commoditize, and value would concentrate at the two ends — on one end, compute chips; on the other, the ontology (the semantic layer that lets models safely operate on enterprise data). He put the majority of the company's engineers onto the AI platform, AIP. The result: Palantir's stock price, sitting in the single digits when he took over as CTO, climbed all the way to around $200; U.S. commercial revenue posted triple-digit growth for multiple consecutive quarters. This bet's logic mirrors Chapter 1 of this book: **the stronger and cheaper the models get, the more valuable the engineering work of "connecting models to reality" becomes.** (see Appendix C for sources)

The end point of the four questions of productization is the convergence of the entire FDE business model: **the field is the probe, the platform is the lever, and the product is the vehicle of compounding returns.** The more real the world the probe explores, the more solid the capability the platform accumulates; the more solid the platform, the farther the probe can reach. Once this flywheel starts turning, you are no longer a company that "sends people out to do work" — you are a company that "continuously converts the complexity of the world into software assets" — the species with the highest margins and the deepest moats since the birth of the software industry.
